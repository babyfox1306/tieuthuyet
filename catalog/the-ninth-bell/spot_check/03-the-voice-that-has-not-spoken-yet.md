The ninth bell rang with a sound like cracking bone, and from its bronze throat spilled Clara's own voice: "I knew she would fall."

I didn't move. I couldn't. The words hung in the air, my own vowels, my own cadence, my own breath shaping syllables I had never consciously formed. The recording software showed the waveform scrolling past, a jagged mountain range of sound that matched every acoustic marker I had spent the last hour cataloging.

My hands stayed on the keyboard. My eyes stayed on the screen.

"Play it again," I said, and my voice sounded like it belonged to someone else.

The software obeyed. The waveform rewound, the cursor jumped back to the start of the ninth strike's aftermath, and then I heard myself say it again.

"I knew she would fall."

This time I listened differently. Not for content. For construction.

The first word—"I"—had a soft attack, the vowel slightly rounded at the edges. The second word—"knew"—was sharper, the /n/ sound clipped. Between them, nothing. Absolute silence. No breath, no glottal transition, no sliding of the tongue from one position to the next.

I zoomed in. 100x. 200x. 400x.

The gap between "I" and "knew" was a vertical cut. A straight line in the waveform where the amplitude dropped from normal speech level to zero and then snapped back up. No decay. No fade. A decapitation.

My voice had been cut apart and reassembled like a ransom note made from magazine clippings.

I pulled up the analysis tools and ran a frequency breakdown on the silent gap between syllables. The software highlighted the edges of the cut with red markers. The first segment ended with a faint harmonic tail that didn't belong to any natural speech sound. It was metallic, resonant, carrying a signature I recognized.

The double pulse.

I isolated the seam. The same metallic bracket that had appeared in the eight death recordings, the same compressed coupling sound that had buried itself beneath the threshold of hearing, was here too. It was the glue holding the fragments together.

I checked the duration. 0.03 seconds of pulse, then an edit point, then another 0.03 seconds of pulse. A sonic staple.

The dead fisherman had one of these at the moment his voice stopped. The woman who fell from the cliffs had one where her scream cut off. Every single archived death had a seam. And now my own confession had three of them—one before "I," one between "I" and "knew," and one after "fall."

My voice had been dismantled and reassembled in a workshop that didn't exist in any physical space I could find.

I opened the cloud archive and ran a matching algorithm against the confession waveform. The software churned through thousands of hours of audio, comparing pitch contours, formant structures, and noise profiles. I watched the progress bar crawl from 0 to 100 percent, my chest tight, my breath shallow.

The results appeared in a list of potential source files.

Top match: "Clara_Study_03_14_2002_A.wav."

The same file Mara had identified. The recording of Thomas coercing me in his study, weeks after Elin died.

I opened the file and let it play. Thomas's voice filled the storage room, distant but clear, captured on the reel-to-reel he had set up in his study.

"That's not what happened, Clara. Think carefully. Try again."

My own voice answered, younger, thinner, frayed at the edges. "I don't remember. I don't remember what I said to her."

"You said something. You must have said something. Try again."

The recording continued. Question after question, answer after answer, each one nudged, corrected, reshaped until I started repeating the same words back to him.

I scrolled through the waveform, looking for the source of "I knew she would fall." It wasn't there. Not in that file, not in any of the other top matches. The algorithm had found fragments that matched the voice—the same pitch, the same resonance, the same vocal fingerprint—but the exact phrase didn't exist in any single recording.

It was assembled. Stitched. Fabricated from pieces of different sessions, different days, different emotional states.

I looked at the confession waveform again, this time paying attention to the background noise. The hiss between words was inconsistent. The first segment had a low-frequency hum at 60 Hz, the signature of a refrigerator compressor. The second segment had a higher noise floor with a slight metallic ring, like the acoustics of a tiled room. The third segment had almost no background noise at all, as if recorded in a soundproof space.

Three different environments. Three different recording sessions. Three different pieces of me, glued together by a metallic pulse that shouldn't exist.

I ran a spectral analysis on the background hiss of the first segment. The software identified the 60 Hz hum and its harmonics, matching it against my archived recordings. The refrigerator compressor from the family home. The Sunday lunch recording. A moment of normal life, a table with food, my sister laughing, my father grumbling about fish.

The second segment matched the acoustic profile of Thomas's study. The hard walls, the wooden floor, the slight echo that came from the high ceiling.

The third segment didn't match anything in my archive. The noise floor was too clean, too artificial, as if someone had recorded in a space designed to eliminate all ambient sound.

I stared at the screen, my mind running through possibilities. A recording studio. A soundproof booth. A room built specifically for capturing clean audio.

The hidden chamber.

Gabriel had built it. Gabriel had wired it. Gabriel had heard voices bleeding through the walls for years.

I pulled up the floor plan again, the published version that showed the east wall at a uniform thickness. My measurements had already proved it was wrong, that there was a space 1.8 meters deep behind the seventh floor. But the plan showed nothing. No room, no chamber, no indication that the tower had been hollowed out.

I calculated the volume. 8 meters by 1.8 meters by 3 meters in height. Roughly 43 cubic meters. More than enough space for recording equipment. Enough space for a man to sit in a chair and manipulate audio files.

The third segment of my confession had been recorded there. In the hidden room. In the space Thomas had built and Gabriel had maintained.

I checked the timestamp on the cloud archive, looking for any file that matched the clean noise profile of the third segment. Nothing. The hidden chamber's acoustics weren't in my archive because I had never been inside that room.

But my voice had.

I played the confession again, this time focusing on the transition between the second and third segments. The double pulse appeared exactly where I expected it, a metallic seam holding two worlds together. The first world was my father's study, where I had been broken down and rebuilt into a version of myself that could produce the answers he wanted. The second world was the hidden chamber, where someone had taken those fragments and assembled them into a confession I had never made.

The third segment ended with the word "fall." The waveform dropped to zero, held there for a full second, and then the recording stopped.

I rewound and played the last few moments again, listening for anything I might have missed. The word "fall" was clean, precise, the /l/ sound held for a fraction of a second before cutting off. But beneath it, buried in the noise floor of the hidden chamber's recording environment, there was something else.

A hiss. Faint, almost imperceptible, but present.

I isolated the frequency range of the hiss and ran a comparison against my archived recordings. The algorithm churned for a few seconds and then returned a match.

The hiss matched a recording made three years after Elin's death.

I felt the floor drop out from under me. The hidden chamber's recording environment hadn't been static. It had changed over time. The noise floor of the third segment matched a file labeled "Tower_Test_2005_09_12.wav" that I had never listened to, never cataloged, never even known existed.

The confession wasn't just assembled from Thomas's recordings. It was assembled from recordings made years later, in the hidden chamber, by someone who had access to my voice from multiple sources across time.

I closed my eyes and tried to think clearly. The confession was a fabrication. It had to be. I had never said those words in that order, with that intent. But the voice was mine. The resonance was mine. The breath patterns, the vocal fry, the subtle quirks of my pronunciation—all of it belonged to me.

Someone had spent years collecting pieces of my voice. Years storing them, cataloging them, arranging them into sentences I would never speak. And now, in a bell tower that shouldn't have been able to produce any sound at all, those pieces had been reassembled and played back as a confession.

I opened my eyes and looked at the waveform one more time. The double pulse sat at each seam, metallic and precise, the signature of an editor who didn't care about hiding their work.

I ran one final analysis, comparing the background hiss of the confession against every file in my cloud archive. The results painted a picture I didn't want to see.

The first segment of my confession came from Thomas's study in 2002. The second came from the family home in 2003. The third came from the hidden chamber in 2005.

Three different years. Three different rooms. Three different recordings, stitched together by a metallic pulse that appeared in every death recording in the tower's archive.

The confession was a collage. And the person who made it had been working on this project for years, long after Elin was dead and buried, long after I had left Dunmouth and tried to forget.

I looked at the timestamp on the hidden chamber's recording. 2005. Three years after Elin's death. Three years of someone sitting in a soundproof room, listening to my voice, cutting it into pieces, and arranging it into a narrative.

The background hiss of the third segment didn't match the hidden chamber's current acoustic profile. The room had been modified, its noise floor altered, its recording environment changed. But the hiss from 2005 was still there, preserved in the digital file, a ghost of a room that had been rearranged.

I stared at the screen, the confession waveform glowing in the dim light of the storage room. My voice, my words, my guilt—all of it manufactured, assembled, weaponized by someone who knew me better than I knew myself.

The ninth bell had rung with the sound of cracking bone, and from its bronze throat had spilled a confession I had never made.

But the hiss beneath the words told a different story.

It told me that the confession had been built over years, in a hidden room, by someone who had access to my voice from multiple points in time. It told me that the person who built this had been patient, methodical, and utterly without conscience.

It told me that the confession was assembled from fragments of a life I had lived, recorded, and archived.

And it told me that the person who did this was still out there, waiting for me to discover what they had made.
