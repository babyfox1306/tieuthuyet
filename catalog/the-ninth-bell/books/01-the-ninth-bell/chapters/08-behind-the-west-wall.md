The key was warm in my palm, its brass teeth showing fresh abrasion—as though it had turned in a lock within the last forty-eight hours. Gabriel stood in the open doorway of the ground-floor storage room, his hand still extended from the transfer, his face carrying the particular grey of a man who has just surrendered a loaded weapon to someone he isn't sure he trusts.

“I should have thrown it into the sea when I had the chance,” he said.

“But you didn't.”

“Your father made me swear on the day they took Elin out of the tower. Told me the room was dangerous, that it held things no one should see. He gave me the key and said if I ever opened that door, I'd be responsible for whatever came through it.” He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. “I've kept it eighteen years, Miss Vale. Every time I passed that wall I felt it breathing at me through the plaster.”

I closed my fingers around the brass. It was heavier than it should have been for a key of its size. The shaft was stamped with a number—seven.

“You've been inside,” I said. Not a question.

Gabriel's jaw tightened. “Twice. Once to help him move the equipment in, once to bring a replacement reel when the original tape jammed. Both times I kept my eyes on the floor and my hands in my pockets. I didn't want to know what he was building in there.”

“What did you see?”

“Wires. Reels. A desk with more switches than a ship's bridge. And the smell—hot metal and old coffee and something chemical I couldn't place.” He looked past me toward the staircase. “It's on the seventh floor. Behind the west wall.”

I had spent the morning measuring the east wall. The discrepancy of fifteen centimeters suggested a void, but the entrance could be elsewhere. The entire floor was a lie.

I left Gabriel in the storage room and climbed.

The seventh floor was exactly as I had left it: the laser distance finder still mounted on its tripod, the contact microphone taped to the east wall, the chalk marks mapping a phantom geometry across the plaster. But now I knew which wall mattered.

The west wall looked no different from the others. Lime-washed stone, aged to a soft cream, with a single iron bracket that held a now-extinguished lantern. I ran my fingers along the mortar joints, feeling for variations. The key was made for a lock, but I could see no keyhole.

I started at the lantern bracket. It was bolted through the stone, not flush. I pressed the bracket upward. It resisted, then gave with a grinding sound. The lantern arm rotated ninety degrees and a section of the wall slid inward, seven inches, into the void behind. The gap was barely six inches wide, just enough to admit a hand.

Behind the displaced stone I found the lock. It was set into a steel plate mortared into the brickwork, invisible until the panel shifted.

The key turned with a click that echoed through the stone.

I pushed.

The door was four feet tall and three wide, a section of wall mounted on hidden hinges. It swung inward into darkness. The air that spilled out was still and dry, carrying the ghost of hot electronics and something acrid—burnt dust, the kind that collects inside old amplifiers.

I retrieved my torch from the equipment bag and stepped through.

The chamber was a wound carved into the thickness of the tower. Seven feet wide, fourteen deep, the ceiling low enough that I could touch the stone with an outstretched arm. The walls were lined with shelves bolted directly into the brick, each shelf stacked with tape reels in uniform boxes. At the far end, a desk ran the full width of the space, cluttered with equipment that hummed with a faint, steady thrum.

I stood in the doorway and catalogued.

Two reel-to-reel tape decks, a Studer and a Revox, both modified with additional input modules. A mixing console with eight channels, each labelled in Thomas's hand: Causeway, Stairwell, Bell 1, Bell 2, Bell 3, Bell 4, Bell 5, Gallery. An oscilloscope with a persistent green line drawn across its screen. Splicing blocks, editing blades, leader tape. A rack of amplifiers filling the lower shelf with their glowing faces.

It was a studio. A forensic audio lab, built inside a wall.

And it was still running.

The Studer deck was engaged, its reels stopped mid-cycle. The tape counter glowed red: 14:37. I stared at it. That number had been frozen on the 'clarification' cassette I found in the family files—the one Thomas had labeled with my name and the word clarification. The cassette I had assumed was benign. The cassette whose counter I had logged at 14:37 five chapters ago, before I knew what the number meant.

I stepped forward and saw the tape box lying open on the desk. The label was typed, formal: Elin Vale Master / Original / Do Not Copy. The cassette shell inside was cracked along one edge, the clear plastic showing a tangle of brown tape that had been repaired with splicing tape and surgical precision. A damaged master. The tape that had been running inside Elin's recorder at the moment of her fall.

My hand hovered over it.

I did not touch it.

The chamber was not the tomb Thomas had pretended it was. The dust lay in specific patterns: thick on the lower shelves, thinner on the desk, nearly absent from the floor around the chair. I crouched and looked at the floorboards. The wood showed a path worn lighter—a narrow channel traced by the daily swivel of a chair. Thomas had sat here. Often. Recently.

I touched the motor housing of the Studer. It was warm. The valves in the amplifiers glowed with residual heat. The oscilloscope's screen was still radiating faint warmth. Someone had been here within the last few hours.

Thomas knew I was in the tower. He had been here, maintaining the machine, checking the archive, ensuring nothing had been disturbed. And now the key had turned in the lock.

I stood and turned to the shelves. The boxes were arranged chronologically, each spine dated and labelled. Causeway 03–07, Gallery ambient, Bell samples 1–12, Elin interviews, Thomas dictation, Clara sessions. My name was there, repeated across half a dozen boxes. Sessions. Plural.

I pulled one at random. Clara sessions / week three / set 4. Inside were compact cassettes, each with a sticker bearing the date and a time code. I did not need to listen. I knew what they contained.

The archive spanned the entire back wall. Hundreds of reels. Thousands of hours. Years of surveillance, all routed through this room. Thomas had been recording the tower's environment continuously since before Elin died, and he had never stopped. The system was designed to capture everything.

I looked at the mixing console. Each channel had a label for a location. Gallery was the top of the tower. Causeway was the approach. Bell 1 through Bell 5 corresponded to the bells themselves. But there were three unlabeled channels at the end, their inputs dark.

I knelt and examined the patch panel. Three cables terminated in connectors that had no destination—cut ends, taped off. As though they had once led somewhere, and someone had severed them deliberately.

The floor beneath the desk was uneven. I dragged the chair aside and found a loose board. I pried it up.

Inside the cavity was a logbook, leather-bound, the pages soft with handling. I opened it to the most recent entry.

Date: 21 October. Archive accessed. Tape 9E secured. Do not disturb.

The writing was Thomas's. Sharp, forward-slanting, the letters pressed deep into the paper. Below it, a second line, written in a different ink:

Causeway recording re-engaged. Subject movement detected on east stair. Recommend maintaining current lockdown. T.

The date was three weeks ago.

I turned back a page. 12 October. System diagnostic. All channels operational. Re-spliced Bell 3 input. Tape 9E reviewed. No degradation.

Nine pages before that, the entries became routine, daily, as though the room was a watch station and Thomas was the only watchman. He had been coming here for years. The patterns of my investigation—the subpoenas, the access requests, the questions—had triggered him to secure Tape 9E.

I closed the logbook and looked at the editing desk. The damaged master sat in its cracked shell, the tape counter still frozen at 14:37. The number was a reference point, a moment in time that Thomas had marked. I did not know if it was the moment before Elin fell, or the moment after.

The oscilloscope's green line flickered.

Somewhere in the tower, a relay clicked.

The humming intensified for a fraction of a second, then settled back to its steady pitch. The system was live. It was always live. And now I had turned the key in the lock, and Thomas would know the chamber had been breached.

I looked at the logbook again, at the final entry in his tight, furious hand.

Do not disturb.

I had disturbed it. And the machine was still running.
