The waveform resolved at 3:47 a.m.—Elin's voice, eighteen years silent, suddenly clear and furious and asking our father why he had been listening to all of us.

I need to back up. The reconstruction took two hours. Two hours of splicing, aligning, and calibrating editing equipment I had not touched since a graduate seminar on forensic audio, in a room that smelled of copper dust and cable insulation, with the man who helped build it sitting three feet behind me pretending his hands were not shaking.

Gabriel had followed me into the chamber after I cracked the seal. He had not spoken much. He sat on the floor near the door with his back against the false wall, knees drawn up, watching me work the way a man watches a surgeon operate on someone he loves. Which, I supposed, was not entirely wrong. The tape in the editing deck contained Elin's last words. Gabriel had heard them bleeding through the stone for years.

The master tape was damaged. That was the first problem. The shell was cracked on the right corner, and the leader—the first few seconds of blank tape before the recording begins—had a tear that ran diagonally across the oxide surface. I examined the tear under the desk lamp. The edges were not frayed from age. They were sheared, clean, the kind of break that happens when tape is yanked from a running transport at speed. A panic eject. Someone had pulled the cassette out of a recorder mid-operation, not to preserve the recording, but to stop it.

Thomas had done that. He had been recording Elin, and when she said whatever she said, he grabbed the tape and ripped it from the mechanism.

The second problem was splice alignment. The master had been cut—surgically, the same violent vertical excision I had seen on the published confession tapes. But here the cuts were raw, unrefined. This was not the work of a skilled audio engineer. This was someone with a razor blade and a flat surface, severing the sections he wanted to destroy. The problem for Thomas was that he had missed. Or been interrupted. Or panicked. Because between the cuts, fragments of original audio remained attached to both sides of each splice. Milliseconds of intact waveform, buried in the noise floor, waiting for someone with the right tools to pull them back into alignment.

I had the right tools. The chamber's own editing desk was built for exactly this kind of work. The reel-to-reel transport was a Studer A80, professionally maintained, and the patch bay could route any input to any output. Thomas had kept this room in working order because he still used it. The logbook proved that. He had been here three weeks ago, reviewing Tape 9E, maintaining the system. He had built this archive over decades, and he was not done with it.

I threaded the damaged master onto the transport and ran it at half speed. The speakers crackled. Noise floor. Tape hiss. The particular low-frequency hum of a recording made in this tower—the bells, the wind, the groaning of old timber. I isolated the first intact fragment. It was eleven seconds long.

Elin's voice. Not a ghost. Not a composite. Not a stitch job from stolen samples. Elin's actual voice, recorded by their father's own equipment, captured by the microphone mounted in this room.

She was breathing hard. Not from exertion—she had climbed the stairs, and she was twenty-three and fit, so the breathing was not from the climb. It was anger. I recognized it because I had heard that breathing pattern from Elin my entire life. It was the sound she made before she said something she had been holding back for months.

"Dad. Dad, stop. Stop the machine."

The tape hiss swallowed the next two seconds. I adjusted the noise gate, narrowed the frequency band, pulled her voice forward from the static. Gabriel shifted behind me. I did not turn around.

Thomas's voice came next. Low. Controlled. The voice he used when he thought he was being reasonable.

"Elin, sit down. We're having a conversation."

"We're not having a conversation. You've been recording me. You've been recording all of us. In the kitchen. In the garden. In my room." Her voice cracked on "my room." Not with grief. With outrage. "How long?"

The next fragment was shorter. Three seconds, damaged at both ends. I spliced it to the first piece and aligned the waveforms. Thomas's reply emerged from the static like something pulled from deep water.

"The recordings are for the archive. The tower needs documentation. You know the trust requires—"

"Don't talk to me about the trust. I found the speakers. I found the patch cables behind the bookshelf in Clara's room. You've been listening to all of us. Every conversation. Every—" The word dissolved into a splice artifact. I isolated the next intact section.

"—and I'm telling Clara. I'm telling her tonight."

The air in the chamber changed. Not the temperature. The weight of it. Gabriel made a sound behind me, a small intake of breath, as though he had been holding it for years and only now let some of it go.

I looked at the tape counter. We were at 11:22 of the original recording. The master ran for seventeen minutes total, but the damage meant I could only recover fragments. I worked them into sequence by matching waveform signatures at the splice points, the torn edges fitting together like broken glass reassembled. Each recovered piece was a window. Through each window I could hear the room—this room, the chamber where I was sitting—echoing with my sister's voice and my father's voice and the particular acoustic signature of a space with stone walls and copper wiring.

The fourth fragment was the longest intact stretch: four minutes and eleven seconds. It began with the sound of a chair scraping against the floor. Then Elin, closer to the microphone now, as though she had moved toward the desk where the recorder sat.

"You built a room. Under the tower. For this. For all of this." The sound of her hand striking a surface—wood or metal. "Hundreds of tapes. Thousands of hours. Our whole lives, you've been—"

"Elin."

"No. You don't get to say my name in that voice. That voice you use when you think you're in control." A pause. Breathing. Then, quieter: "Clara doesn't know. Does she? About any of this?"

"Clara is fragile. Clara doesn't need to—"

"Clara is not fragile. Clara is the strongest person in this family, and you have been breaking her down into pieces small enough to manage. I found the tapes of her. The sessions. Where you asked her the same questions until she gave you the answers you wanted."

I stopped breathing. The tape continued to play, but the sound receded to a distant pulse behind the roar in my skull. Sessions. The word she used. Not recordings. Sessions. Elin had known about the sessions. She had found them.

The fifth fragment began mid-sentence. Thomas's voice, stripped of its controlled calm, raw and sharp.

"—cannot leave this tower with those tapes. Do you understand what would happen? The trust, the archive, everything I've built—"

"Everything you've built is a prison. You recorded your own children and you stitched their words into whatever story you wanted, and I am taking these tapes to Clara and then I am taking them to the police."

A sound I could not identify. Fabric. Movement. Two bodies in a confined space, close together.

Thomas spoke again. "Sit down."

"No."

"Elin, sit down."

"You don't get to tell me what to do anymore."

The sixth fragment was the one that changed everything. It was short. Eight seconds. But in those eight seconds, I heard the platform.

The recording captured it clearly because the microphone was six meters from the gallery access door, and the door was open. The gallery platform was an exterior structure, wooden planking over iron brackets, bolted to the tower's north face. In the recording, I heard the brackets groan. Not the slow groan of age and weather. A sharp, metallic stress sound. Then the crack. The sound of timber splitting under load. Then Elin's voice, and for the first time it was not angry. It was afraid.

"Dad—"

And Thomas's voice, very close. Not across the room. Not at the door. Close enough that his breath would have been on her face. Close enough that his hand could have reached her.

"Hold on."

Not a command. Not a reassurance. An instruction. The way you tell someone to hold a position while you decide what to do. The way you speak when you are standing close enough to grab someone and choosing not to.

The platform collapsed. The recording captured the sound of iron tearing from stone, of timber shattering, of a human body falling through open air and the wind swallowing the scream. All of it in the first two seconds. The remaining six seconds were silence, then Thomas's breathing, then the sound of footsteps moving away from the door, back into the chamber, and a hand reaching for the recorder.

The panic eject. The torn leader. He pulled the tape and ran.

I stopped the transport. My hands were steady. That was the worst part. I could feel the entire architecture of my life shifting, the foundation cracking, the guilt I had carried for eighteen years dissolving into something much worse than guilt. But my hands were steady because I was an analyst and this was evidence and the evidence said one thing: Thomas was close enough to catch her. He was close enough to save her. He stood there and told her to hold on, and he did not reach out his hand.

Gabriel spoke from behind me. His voice was hoarse.

"You need to copy that. Right now."

He was right. I threaded a blank tape onto the secondary transport. The chamber had a dual-deck system—the original and a backup. Thomas had built redundancy into his surveillance infrastructure. I pressed record on the second deck and played the master through. The reconstruction rolled onto fresh oxide, clean and intact, every recovered fragment in sequence. The whole thing ran seventeen minutes and forty-three seconds.

When it finished, I labeled the copy. Elin's voice. 14 October, year unknown. Confrontation. Fall.

I removed the copy from the transport and held it in my hand. A cassette. Seventy-four minutes of recording time. The entire reconstruction fit on one side. Elin's last words, and the proof that our father had watched her die and chosen not to act.

The sound came from the stairwell. Footsteps. Fast, heavy, the deliberate cadence of a man who knew exactly where he was going. Thomas's footsteps. I had heard them on recordings my entire life. I could identify them the way a musician identifies a particular instrument in an orchestra.

Gabriel was on his feet. He looked at me. I looked at him.

"He'll have the outer key," Gabriel said. "But the inner lock is manual. From this side."

"Can he break through the wall?"

"Not without equipment. Not quickly."

The footsteps reached the seventh floor. A pause. Then the sound of a key in the outer lock of the chamber door. The mechanism turned. The handle rotated. The door did not open because the inner bolt was engaged—massive iron, original to the tower's construction, the one element of the false wall that Gabriel had not built but had instead incorporated into his design. A lock that needed no key. Only a lever.

Thomas spoke through the door. His voice was calm. The same controlled tone he had used on the tape when he still believed he was managing the situation.

"Gabriel. I know you're in there. Open the door."

Gabriel looked at me. I shook my head.

"Gabriel, open the door. Whatever she told you, whatever she showed you, this is not what it looks like. The tapes are complicated. The context is—"

"The context," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, "is that you were six feet from her when the platform broke and you did not reach out your hand."

Silence. Through the door, I heard his breathing. The same breathing I had heard on the tape. Controlled. Measured. The breathing of a man deciding what story to tell next.

"Clara. You don't understand what you've found."

"I understand exactly what I've found."

"The reconstruction is incomplete. The fragments are damaged. Without context, without the full archive, that tape is—"

"It's enough."

The inner lock engaged with a sound like bone settling into socket. I had thrown the lever when I heard him on the stairs. Through the door, Thomas's voice dropped to a whisper.

"That tape is the only version. You play that and everything I built collapses."

On the other side of the door, I heard him slide a cassette into a deck.
