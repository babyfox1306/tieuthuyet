The wall was supposed to be thirty-two centimeters thick. My tap meter read forty-seven.

I stared at the digital display, then at the grey plaster surface I'd just struck with the rubber mallet. The sound had been wrong — not the flat, dead thud of solid masonry, but something hollower, a note with a tail. Like tapping a coffin lid that had been sealed too recently to match the cemetery.

Fifteen centimeters of discrepancy. That isn't weathering. That isn't mortar creep. That's architecture lying about itself.

I'd started with Thomas's cassettes still warm in my hands, the tape counter frozen at 22:15 like a wound that refused to close. His voice — weaponized patience, the coaxing cadence of a man dismantling a child's reality one careful sentence at a time — had been circling in my skull for twenty minutes. I needed it to stop. Not by processing. Not by feeling. By doing what I always do: measuring something.

The seventh floor of the bell tower was the logical starting point. Not because of Thomas. Because of the recordings.

Every anomalous sound on every tape in this tower's archive shared a characteristic I hadn't yet explained: a consistent reverberation pattern that didn't match the published dimensions of the structure. Sound bounces off surfaces at predictable angles. The speed is constant — 343 meters per second in dry air at sea level. If you know the delay between direct signal and first reflection, you can calculate the distance to the reflecting surface. Basic acoustics. Nothing supernatural about it.

I'd run the math on the flight over. The reverb tail on the Arthur Judd recording — the one that ended with the surgical cut and percussive spike — implied a reflective surface approximately 1.8 meters behind the seventh-floor east wall. The published floor plan showed solid masonry at that depth. No gap. No chamber. No room for sound to bounce.

The published floor plan was wrong.

I set up my equipment methodically: tap meter, laser distance finder, a small contact microphone paired with the portable analyzer, and a bag of powdered chalk. Gabriel was still out on the causeway — the tide was turning, he'd said, and he needed to check the walkway for debris. He'd been gone over an hour. I didn't wait for him. I worked.

The seventh floor was a single rectangular space, roughly eight by four meters, with the bell mechanism occupying the center. The walls were stone beneath a coat of plaster that had been painted and repainted over decades — cream, then grey, then cream again, each layer a geological record of someone's taste. I started at the northeast corner and worked south along the east wall, tapping every thirty centimeters and recording the depth reading.

Thirty-one centimeters. Thirty-two. Thirty. Thirty-one.

Normal. Consistent with a double-leaf stone wall filled with rubble core, which is what the 1847 construction documents described. I marked each point with a small chalk cross and moved on.

At the southeast corner, the readings began to shift.

Thirty-five centimeters. Thirty-eight. Forty-one.

I stopped. Ran the tap again at the forty-one mark. The sound was subtly different — higher pitched, less dead. I pressed my palm flat against the plaster. Cool. Dry. No visible cracking, no bulging, no signs of recent repair. But the acoustic signature was wrong.

Forty-seven centimeters at the midpoint of the east wall.

I knelt and tapped the base. Same reading. The plaster was seamless — no join line, no patch, no indication that this section had been modified after original construction. Whoever had altered this wall had been meticulous about the surface finish. But they couldn't alter the physics. You can't add fifteen centimeters of masonry without changing the sound it makes when struck.

I mapped the anomaly with chalk, outlining the zone where thickness exceeded the published specification. It formed a rough rectangle, approximately two meters wide and two-point-four meters tall, centered on the east wall between the second and third window embrasures. The dimensions were deliberate. Human-scale. A door.

Thomas had been the tower's caretaker for eleven years. I knew this from the estate documents — a fact I'd noted with the same detached precision I applied to roof condition and foundation settlement. In every recorded session I'd found, Thomas spoke about the tower with proprietary ease: the weight of the bells, the routing of the ropes, the way the wind entered through the louvers at certain angles. He described the seventh floor as "a simple room, nothing hidden, nothing extraordinary." His words from one of the cassettes, played back in my memory with the flat fidelity of a playback head on old tape.

A simple room. Nothing hidden.

I looked at the rectangle of chalk marks on the east wall. Nothing about this was simple, and something was very well hidden.

The contact microphone confirmed what the tap meter suggested. I affixed it to the wall at the center of the anomaly and ran a sweep on the analyzer — a low-frequency ping, barely audible, designed to map internal structure through echo response. The display lit up with a return pattern that made my stomach drop.

Behind the visible wall there was a space. Not a filled cavity. Not rubble or insulation. An empty volume, approximately forty-five centimeters deep, extending the full width of the anomalous zone. The floor of this space was at the same level as the seventh floor. The ceiling was higher — the ping showed a reflective surface roughly thirty centimeters above the internal ceiling of the main room.

Someone had built a false wall. A cavity concealed behind the visible surface, accessible — I assumed, though I hadn't found the entry point — from somewhere. The false wall was the same thickness as the original: thirty-two centimeters. But the combined assembly — false wall plus cavity plus real wall — read as forty-seven centimeters on the exterior. The missing two centimeters were mortar joints.

I stood in the center of the seventh floor and looked at the east wall. It looked like every other wall. Plaster. Paint. Nothing. But behind that nothing, something waited.

I shook the thought off. Focused. The wall was the problem, not Thomas. Not yet.

I needed to understand the infrastructure. I moved the contact microphone to different points along the east wall and ran additional sweeps. The cavity wasn't empty — there were linear returns inside it, consistent with pipes or conduit. I mapped them with chalk on the floor: two runs, parallel, running vertically from floor to ceiling near the north edge of the cavity. The diameter was too small for structural supports. Too regular for debris. They looked like cable runs.

Cable runs in a wall that shouldn't contain cable runs.

I cross-referenced the tower's documented infrastructure against my notes. The 1847 construction included no electrical conduit in the walls — the wiring was all surface-mounted during a 1960s renovation. The only in-wall services were cast-iron water pipes on the ground floor, feeding a single tap in the storage room. Nothing on the seventh floor. Nothing that should have been inside an exterior wall on any floor.

Thomas had described the tower's systems in session 7 of his cassettes — a tangent I'd only noticed because of the acoustic analysis. He'd listed the infrastructure with a cataloguer's pride: "original stone, lime mortar, surface wiring, no internal services above ground floor." He'd been lying. Or he'd been describing the tower as it appeared to visitors, not as he'd built it.

The cable runs were undocumented. The cavity was undocumented. The false wall was undocumented.

I pulled out my phone and opened the building survey I'd downloaded before flying out — a structural assessment commissioned by the village trust in 2008, three years after Thomas's death. The surveyor's notes described the east wall of the seventh floor as "sound masonry in good condition, consistent with original construction, no remedial work required." He hadn't found it. Either he hadn't looked carefully, or he hadn't been looking at the same wall.

The plaster showed no signs of recent intervention — the paint layers were consistent with the rest of the room, aging evenly, no fresh skim coat hiding a new joint. Whatever was behind this wall had been here longer than the survey. Longer than Thomas's death. Maybe much longer.

I moved to the window embrasure nearest the anomalous zone and examined the stone surround. The window was original — 1847, hand-dressed sandstone, weathered to a dark honey color. But the wall section immediately adjacent to the embrasure showed a different mortar composition when I scraped a small sample with my pocketknife.

The original tower mortar was lime-based — crumbly, pale, slightly efflorescent, consistent with mid-nineteenth-century construction. The mortar in the anomalous section was denser, grey-white, with a crystalline structure that caught the light differently. I held the scrapings up to the window light. Calcium silicate. Not lime.

Calcium silicate cement wasn't commercially available until the early twentieth century. This mortar was at minimum sixty years younger than the tower. Based on the aggregate size and the density of the matrix, I'd place it closer to the 1990s. Someone had sealed a section of this wall within the last thirty to forty years. Someone with knowledge of the tower's construction, access to the seventh floor, and a reason to hide what lay behind the stone.

Thomas had access. Thomas had knowledge. Thomas had been the tower's caretaker who described this room as "simple" and "nothing hidden."

I wrote the observation in my notebook: "Mortar sample, seventh floor east wall anomaly: calcium silicate, probable vintage 1985-1995. Inconsistent with 1847 construction. Consistent with deliberate concealment." I bagged the sample. Not because I trusted a future lab, but because I trusted my own documentation more than I trusted anyone else's.

The air in the seventh floor had changed. I noticed it gradually — a subtle shift in pressure, the kind you feel descending in an airplane when your ears haven't equalized yet. The cavity behind the wall was sealed, but it wasn't airtight. Air was moving through it. Faintly, almost imperceptibly, drawing from somewhere below and exhausting somewhere above. A draft through a system that shouldn't exist.

I held my hand near the base of the anomalous wall section and felt it: a thin, cold current, barely more than a suggestion against the skin. The air smelled different here. Not the brine-and-mildew perfume of the tower. Something drier. Something that had been sealed away from the sea.

I stood very still. The bell mechanism above me was motionless — the tide was too low for the weights to engage, and no wind was moving through the louvers. The only sound in the tower was the distant, constant murmur of the sea, a hundred feet below. And beneath that murmur, if I held my breath and emptied my mind of everything except listening —

There.

A vibration. Low, so low it was more felt than heard, resonating through the stone floor and up into my legs. Not mechanical in the way a motor is mechanical. Not organic. Something between the two. A pulse with a rhythm that I recognized because I had seen its waveform a hundred times on my analyzer screen.

The double pulse.

The same metallic, compressed coupling that appeared on every recording in the tower's archive. The same artifact I had isolated, measured, and identified as the sonic signature of an edit seam — a bracket marking where two pieces of audio had been stitched together. It was on the tapes. It was in the walls. It was here, vibrating through the stone beneath my feet with a consistency that turned my theory inside out.

If the double pulse was an editing artifact, then something in this tower was being edited. Not digitally. Not on tape. Something was being recorded, cut, and reassembled in real time, using infrastructure hidden behind false walls and inside sealed cavities. The recordings in the archive weren't relics. They were evidence of an active system.

I pressed my palm flat against the center of the anomalous wall.

The stone was cool. Dry. Solid. But through it, like a heartbeat felt through a stethoscope pressed against a chest, I felt the vibration — steady, patient, pulsing in the exact rhythm of the double pulse I had first heard buried beneath Elin's voice. Whatever was behind this wall, it was running. It had been running for a very long time.

And somewhere in the tower, a bell I hadn't struck began to ring.
