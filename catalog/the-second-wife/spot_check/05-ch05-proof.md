The floorboard gave way under my heel. I didn't mean to find it. But there it was — a shirt, stiff with dried blood, the name tag still pinned to the collar. "Clara." The previous housekeeper.

I froze, my hand still gripping the edge of Marcus's closet shelf. I'd been looking for something else — anything else that might connect the watch to something concrete. A receipt, a note, a phone number. Instead, I'd stepped on the one loose board in the entire room, and the corner had popped up like a tongue.

The shirt was folded, almost neatly, wedged into the space between the floor joists. It was a uniform shirt, the same pale blue as the one I was wearing. The same cut. The same fabric. The name tag was fastened with a safety pin, the metal rusted at the edges.

Clara.

I knelt down, my knees pressing into the hardwood. The blood was dark — almost black — crusted in a splatter pattern across the chest and collar. I touched the fabric with one finger. It was stiff, like cardboard. Like it had been soaked and then left to dry without being washed.

The smell hit me next. Metallic, but with something else underneath. Something sharp. Vinegar. Like someone had tried to clean it with a homemade solution and given up.

I pulled the shirt out completely, holding it up to the light from the window. The blood pattern was wrong. Too much on the chest, too little on the sleeves. Like someone had been standing still when it happened. Or like the shirt had been placed on a body that was already lying down.

My hands were shaking. I told myself it was adrenaline, not fear. I'd seen blood before. I'd seen worse than blood. But this was different. This was Clara's blood. The woman who had left a note behind the dryer, warning me to get out before the 15th. The woman who had worked here for six years and then vanished.

I heard footsteps in the hallway.

I shoved the shirt back under the floorboard and kicked the board into place, my heart slamming against my ribs. I stood up, smoothing my apron, trying to look like I was just tidying the closet.

Marcus appeared in the doorway.

"What are you doing in here?"

His voice was calm, but his eyes were scanning the room, looking for something out of place.

"Organizing your closet," I said, keeping my voice steady. "The wife said you had some suits that needed hanging."

He stared at me for a long moment. I could feel the seconds stretching, each one a knife edge.

"I don't remember asking you to do that."

"You didn't," I said. "But I noticed the closet was a mess when I came in to dust. Figured I'd help."

He stepped into the room, and I forced myself not to look at the floorboard. Not to glance down. Not to give anything away.

"Where were you last night?" I asked, the question slipping out before I could stop it.

Marcus's eyes narrowed. "Excuse me?"

"Just curious," I said, keeping my voice light. "I heard you on the phone. Sounded important."

"That's none of your business."

"It is if I'm supposed to keep the house running," I said. "The wife mentioned you had a late meeting. But the car was in the garage all evening."

I saw the flicker in his eyes. The split second of calculation.

"I took a walk," he said. "Cleared my head."

"A walk," I repeated. "At midnight."

"I have trouble sleeping."

"Funny," I said. "So do I. I was in the kitchen around midnight, making tea. I would have seen you come down the stairs."

The silence stretched. Marcus's jaw tightened.

"You're very observant," he said, his voice cold.

"I'm a housekeeper," I said. "Noticing things is part of the job."

He took a step closer, and I forced myself not to back away. "Let me make something clear," he said, his voice low. "You work here. You follow my rules. You don't ask questions about my schedule, my phone calls, or my wife. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good." He turned and walked out of the room, his footsteps heavy on the hardwood.

I waited until I heard his voice from downstairs, talking to someone on the phone again. Then I dropped to my knees and pulled the shirt out from under the floorboard.

I needed to hide it somewhere safe. Somewhere he wouldn't look.

I thought about the watch, hidden in the bottom of my suitcase. The initials E.M. The cracked crystal. The fear in Marcus's eyes when he'd been on the phone.

I folded the shirt as small as I could and tucked it into the waistband of my pants, under my apron. Then I walked out of the study, my heart pounding, my palms sweating.

The wife was in the sitting room when I passed. She looked up from her book, her old-penny eyes catching mine.

"Everything all right, Anna?"

"Fine," I said. "Just finishing up."

She smiled, but it didn't reach her eyes. "You look pale. Are you feeling well?"

"I'm fine," I repeated. "Just tired."

I made it to my room and locked the door. I pulled out the shirt and laid it on the bed, staring at the dark stains. The name tag. Clara.

I pulled out my phone and called Tommy again.

He answered on the second ring. "You again. I was hoping you'd forgotten my number."

"I need information," I said. "Marcus Caldwell. What's his record?"

There was a pause. "Why do you care about Marcus Caldwell?"

"I'm working for him."

"You're what?"

"Long story," I said. "Just tell me what you know."

Tommy sighed. "I told you. He had a case a few years back. A woman died on his property. The official story was an accident, but there were questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind that don't go away," he said. "The body had bruises that didn't match the fall. And there was blood. A lot of it. More than a fall would explain."

I looked at the shirt on my bed. The dark stains. The vinegar smell.

"The case was ruled suspicious death," Tommy continued. "But nothing stuck. No charges. He had good lawyers."

"Who was the woman?"

"I don't know. The records were sealed. But I heard rumors."

"What kind of rumors?"

"That she was a housekeeper," he said. "Just like you."

I closed my eyes. "Tommy, I need to ask you something."

"Go ahead."

"If I find evidence — real evidence — can I get him arrested?"

There was a long pause. "Anna, what are you doing?"

"I'm helping someone," I said. "A woman. His wife. She's trapped here."

"Don't get involved," Tommy said, his voice sharp. "Whatever you think you know, you don't. These people are dangerous. You get too close, you'll end up like the last one."

"I'm already close," I said. "I found the shirt."

"What shirt?"

"Her shirt. Clara's. It's covered in blood. I have it."

Tommy was silent for a long moment. "Burn it," he said finally. "Burn it and get out of there. Before the 15th."

"I can't," I said. "I promised her."

"Promised who?"

"His wife. She's scared. She needs help."

"She's not the only one," Tommy said. "You need to think about yourself for once, Anna. You're not a hero. You're a woman with a record who's one mistake away from going back inside."

I looked at the shirt. The dark stains. The name tag. Clara.

"I know," I said. "But I can't leave her."

Tommy sighed. "Fine. But don't say I didn't warn you."

He hung up.

I hid the shirt in the bottom of my suitcase, next to the watch. Evidence. Proof. Something to use against Marcus when the time came.

I sat on the edge of the bed, my hands trembling. The 15th was two days away. Two days until whatever was going to happen, happened.

I thought about Clara's note. Get out before the 15th.

I thought about the locked room in the east wing. The new bolt. The gardener's warning.

I thought about Marcus on the phone, his voice dripping with venom.

I thought about the wife's trembling hands, her whispered plea, her lies about Clara.

Something was coming. Something bad. And I was right in the middle of it.

I took a deep breath and stood up. I had the proof. I had the watch and the shirt. I could expose Marcus after the 15th, save the wife, and then disappear.

That was the plan. Save her. Then disappear.

I walked to the door, my hand on the handle. I was about to leave when I heard a sound behind me.

Soft footsteps.

I turned.

The wife was standing in the doorway, her face pale. "You found it," she whispered. "I'm so sorry you had to see that."
