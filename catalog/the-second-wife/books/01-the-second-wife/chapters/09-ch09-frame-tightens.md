The footage was grainy, but clear enough. I watched the wife—Lydia—walk into the husband's study, a plastic bag in her hand. She pulled out the bloody shirt, placed it under the floorboard, and smiled. A cold, practiced smile. The smile of someone who had done this before.

I played it again. And again. Each time, the same sequence. Same smile. Same careful, deliberate movements. She knew exactly where the loose floorboard was. She knew exactly how to position the shirt so it would catch someone's attention. She knew exactly what she was doing.

The camera was hidden in a teddy bear on the bookshelf. A baby monitor, repurposed. The lens was the bear's left eye, almost invisible unless you knew what to look for. I had found it by accident, checking for listening devices after the wife's revelation. The bear had been sitting there the whole time, watching her plant her own evidence.

I sat back in the desk chair, my hands shaking. The journal was open beside me, the photographs spread across the desk. The wife had been planning this for six months. She had documented every step, every manipulation, every lie. And now I had footage of her planting the shirt that was supposed to convict Marcus of Clara's murder.

But it wasn't enough. It would never be enough.

Because the footage showed Lydia planting the shirt, yes. But it also showed her walking into the study, opening the floorboard, and placing the shirt with surgical precision. It showed her checking her work, adjusting the angle, making sure the tag was visible. It showed her wiping her hands on her dress, then walking out.

It showed everything except the murder itself.

I checked the file date. Three weeks ago. Three weeks before I arrived. She had planted the shirt three weeks before she even knew I existed. That meant she had been preparing for someone. Not me specifically, but someone. A patsy. A fall guy. A convicted criminal with a record and no one to miss them.

And then I had walked through the door with my fake name and my rehearsed story, and she had recognized me for exactly what I was.

The screen flickered. I had been staring at it for too long. The battery was low, the feed cutting in and out. I needed to save the footage somewhere safe. Somewhere she couldn't find it.

My hand went to my neck. The silver locket. The one Lydia had given me on my second day, a gesture of trust and kindness. She had said it was her mother's, that it had been passed down through generations. She had said it would protect me.

I opened it. The interior was smooth, unmarked. No inscription, no photograph. Just polished silver and a small, almost invisible seam.

I pressed on the seam with my thumbnail. The back panel popped open.

A hidden compartment. Small, but large enough for a memory card.

I looked at the camera. I looked at the locket. I looked at the footage frozen on the screen, Lydia's smile captured in grainy digital perfection.

She had given me the one thing I needed to hide the evidence. She had placed it around my neck with her own hands, smiled, and told me it would keep me safe.

It was almost poetic.

I ejected the memory card from the camera, slipped it into the locket's compartment, and snapped the back panel shut. The locket was warm against my skin, heavy with purpose.

"Good girl," I whispered to myself. "You're learning."

I stood up, stretched my back, and looked around the study. The east wing was quiet, the afternoon light filtering through the heavy curtains. Lydia was in the main house, resting. Marcus was still being interviewed by the police. I had maybe an hour before anyone noticed I was gone.

I walked to the door, my hand on the handle—

"I see you've found my little recording."

I spun around.

Lydia was leaning against the doorframe, arms crossed, a thin smile on her lips. She looked different. Composed. Controlled. The trembling hands were still, the fragile posture gone. She stood like a woman who had never broken a bone in her life.

"Don't worry," she said. "I have the original. And I've already called Marcus back. He'll be here in two hours. Just in time for the signing."

"The signing," I repeated. My voice was steady, but my heart was pounding so hard I could hear it in my ears.

"The will." She tilted her head, studying me. "Did you think I would wait until the 15th? That would be careless. And I'm not careless, Anna. I plan."

"The 15th was the plan. Your journal said—"

"My journal said what I wanted it to say." She stepped into the room, her heels clicking against the hardwood floor. "You were never supposed to find it. But you did. And that changed everything."

"Changed what?"

"Changed the timeline." She stopped in front of the desk, her fingers trailing across the photographs. "I had to accelerate. Move things up. Make sure you didn't have time to think."

"So you moved the signing."

"I moved the signing." She picked up a photograph of me, the one taken outside the prison gates. "Two hours. That's all I need. Marcus arrives, signs the will, and then..."

"And then what?"

She smiled. That cold, practiced smile I had seen on the footage. "And then you do what you came here to do."

"I came here to work."

"You came here to survive." She dropped the photograph back onto the desk. "You came here because you had nowhere else to go, no one else to turn to, and a parole officer who would send you back to prison if you missed a single appointment. You came here because you were desperate. And desperate people are easy to manipulate."

I said nothing.

"But you're smarter than I expected. That's a problem." She walked toward me, her eyes never leaving mine. "I had a plan for you, Anna. A beautiful plan. You were going to be the hero. The woman who saved me from my abusive husband. The woman who finally had the courage to fight back."

"That's not what happened."

"No. Because you found the journal. You found the footage. You found everything I didn't want you to find." She stopped a foot away from me, close enough that I could smell her perfume. "So now I have to change the plan."

"What's the new plan?"

She laughed. Soft, genuine, almost affectionate. "I'm not going to tell you that. You'll figure it out soon enough."

We stood there, facing each other, the silence stretching between us. I could feel the locket against my chest, the memory card hidden inside. She didn't know I had saved the footage. She thought she had found everything.

But she hadn't.

"You should go," she said. "Marcus will be here soon. You don't want to be in this room when he arrives."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm going to tell him you broke into the study. That you've been stealing from us. That you're not to be trusted." She smiled. "And he'll believe me. Because he wants to believe me."

"He knows what you did to Clara."

"He knows what I did to Clara." She nodded, her expression unchanged. "But he doesn't have proof. And proof is what matters, isn't it? Without proof, it's just words. And I'm very good with words."

I stepped back, putting distance between us. "I have proof."

"Of what? The shirt? I planted it. The watch? I put it in Marcus's drawer. The note? I wrote it." She spread her hands, a gesture of surrender. "Everything you found was placed there by me. Everything you think you know was fed to you by me. You don't have a single piece of evidence that doesn't trace back to my hand."

"Except the footage."

"The footage I have the original of." She raised an eyebrow. "Did you think I would leave the only copy in a baby monitor? I'm not stupid, Anna. I made copies. I have files. I have backups."

I touched the locket. "I have the memory card."

"You have a memory card that shows me planting a shirt." She laughed again. "That's not a crime, Anna. That's housekeeping. I was cleaning out my husband's closet, found a shirt I didn't recognize, and put it back where I found it. That's what I'll tell the police. That's what I'll tell anyone who asks."

"Your smile in the footage says otherwise."

"My smile?" She tilted her head, amused. "You're going to convict me based on a smile?"

"I'm going to convict you based on the truth."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she walked to the door, her hand on the handle, and turned back to face me.

"Two hours, Anna. That's all you have. Marcus will be here in two hours. The will-signing will be in three. And then..." She paused, her smile widening. "And then we'll see who the police believe."

She left. The door clicked shut behind her.

I stood in the center of the room, the locket warm against my chest, the silence pressing in on all sides.

Two hours.

I had two hours to find a way out.

I looked at the desk, the photographs, the journal. I looked at the camera, the memory card slot empty. I looked at the door, the handle still warm from her touch.

She had thought of everything. She had planned for everything. She had accounted for every variable except one.

She didn't know I had the footage.

And she didn't know I had a contact named Tommy.

I pulled out my phone, the screen bright in the dim room. No signal. No service. The house was a dead zone, cut off from the outside world.

But there was a phone in the kitchen. A landline. The one I was told not to use for personal calls.

I walked to the door, my hand on the handle, and stopped.

The sound of a car engine, approaching.

I looked out the window. A black sedan, pulling up the drive. Marcus's car.

He was early.

Two hours had become zero.

I pressed the locket against my chest, felt the memory card hidden inside, and started running.
