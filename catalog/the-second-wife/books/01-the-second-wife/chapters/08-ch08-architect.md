The lock was cheap — a simple pin tumbler. I had it open in thirty seconds.

The door swung inward, and I stepped into a room that smelled of paper and dust. And there, on the desk, was a journal with my name on it.

My real name. The one I hadn't used in two years.

Anna.

Not the alias. Not the cover. The name that belonged to a woman who had done three years, four months, and eleven days in a state correctional facility. The name that was supposed to be buried.

I stood frozen in the doorway, my hand still on the knob. The room was small — maybe ten by twelve — with a single window covered by heavy curtains that blocked the afternoon light. A desk dominated the center, wooden and scarred, covered in files and photographs. A bookshelf lined the wall, filled with binders and notebooks. A filing cabinet stood in the corner, its drawers slightly open.

This wasn't a nursery. This wasn't a storage room. This was a command center.

I stepped inside and closed the door behind me. My heart was hammering, but my hands were steady. You learn that in prison too — how to function when your brain is screaming at you to run.

The journal was leather-bound, worn at the edges, with my name written in the wife's handwriting on the cover. Not printed. Handwritten. Like she had practiced it. Like she had written it a hundred times before she got it right.

I opened it.

The first page was dated six months ago.

Candidate profile: Female, early thirties, criminal record (fraud, possession of stolen property). Served three years, four months, eleven days. Released on parole. Uses alias "Anna." Real name: [redacted].

I felt the blood drain from my face.

She knew. She had known from the beginning.

I flipped the page.

Strengths: Desperate, no family ties, no one will look for her. Criminal record means she can't go to police. Willing to do whatever it takes to stay out of prison. Weaknesses: Empathy. She'll bond with the target if I'm not careful. Must control the narrative.

Plan:
- Plant evidence against Marcus (watch, shirt, note).
- Build trust through shared trauma.
- Make Anna believe she's saving me.
- On the 15th, execute Marcus. Frame Anna. Disappear.

I read it three times. Each time, the words rearranged themselves in my skull, trying to form a different meaning. But there was only one meaning.

The wife had planned this. Every single piece.

I turned to the next page. Photographs. Dozens of them. Photographs of me — leaving the prison, getting on a bus, walking into the employment agency. Photographs of Marcus — at work, at the grocery store, in the garden. Photographs of Clara.

Clara's face was familiar now. I had seen it in my nightmares — the bloodstained shirt, the name tag, the vinegar smell. But here she was alive, smiling, standing in the kitchen with her arm around the wife.

They looked happy. Friends. Maybe more than friends.

I turned the page.

Clara was a mistake. I got careless. I let her get too close, and she figured it out. Marcus found her body before I could dispose of it properly. He's been holding it over my head ever since.

But he doesn't know about the east wing. He doesn't know about the room. And he doesn't know about Anna.

Anna is perfect. She's already afraid of Marcus. She's already found the watch and the shirt. She's already lying to the police for me.

By the time she realizes the truth, it will be too late.

I closed the journal. My hands were shaking now.

The room was silent except for the ticking of a clock somewhere in the house. I could hear my own breathing, shallow and rapid, like I had been running.

I looked around the desk. Files upon files. Bank statements. Property deeds. A passport with the wife's photo but a different name. A map of the estate with routes marked in red — escape routes, probably. And a calendar with a single date circled.

The 15th.

Tomorrow.

I picked up one of the photographs. Me, walking out of the prison gates. I remembered that day. I remembered the weight of the sky, the smell of freedom, the terror of having nowhere to go.

She had been watching me for months. Maybe longer.

"You found it."

The voice came from behind me. Soft. Calm. Familiar.

I turned around.

The wife stood in the doorway, a gun in her hand. Not a small one — a serious one, black and heavy, the kind that could put a hole through a person and keep going.

"You were never here to save me," she said. "You were here to take the fall. And now, my dear, you're going to help me kill him."

I stared at her. The trembling hands were gone. The fragile voice was gone. The vulnerable, broken woman who had cried on my shoulder was gone.

In her place was someone else. Someone who had been planning this for a very long time.

"You knew," I said. My voice was flat. Empty. "From the beginning."

"I knew everything." She stepped into the room, the gun steady in her hand. "I knew your real name. I knew your record. I knew you'd be desperate enough to take the job. I knew you'd find the watch. I knew you'd find the shirt. I knew you'd confront Marcus and then lie to the police to protect me."

She smiled. It was the same smile she had given me over tea. The same smile she had given me when she told me about her father.

"I designed every piece of this, Anna. Every conversation. Every tear. Every moment of vulnerability. I knew exactly what you needed to see, and I showed it to you at exactly the right time."

I thought about the locket. The tea. The shared wound of her childhood abuse. The way she had held my hand and told me I was the only person who had ever believed her.

All of it. A performance.

"Why?" I asked. "Marcus said you killed Clara. He said you framed him."

"Marcus is many things — controlling, paranoid, weak — but he's not a killer." She walked closer, the gun still pointed at my chest. "He discovered what I did. He found Clara's body. And instead of going to the police like a good husband, he tried to use it against me."

"Use it how?"

"He wanted a divorce. He wanted the house, the money, everything. He said if I didn't sign over the estate, he'd tell the police what I did." She laughed, a cold, hollow sound. "He thought he had leverage. He thought I was still the fragile little wife he married."

"But you're not."

"No. I'm not." She gestured with the gun toward the desk. "You've seen the files. You've seen the planning. I've been working on this for two years, Anna. Two years of pretending to be sick. Two years of letting Marcus think he was in control. Two years of waiting for the right person to walk through that door."

"And I was that person."

"You were perfect." Her eyes softened, almost affectionate. "A criminal with no family. No friends. No one who would miss you. Someone who would believe anything because she needed to believe in something."

I felt the words like a knife.

The locket. The tea. The shared wound.

All of it had been a mirror. She had held up a reflection of everything I needed to see, and I had fallen for it like a child.

"The note behind the dryer," I said. "The one that said 'HE KNOWS. GET OUT BEFORE THE 15TH.'"

"I wrote it. I planted it before you arrived."

"The watch?"

"I planted it. Marcus never wears jewelry. He wouldn't even notice it was there."

"The shirt?"

"Clara's shirt, yes. I put it there the night before you found it." She tilted her head. "I have to admit, I was impressed you found it so quickly. Most people would have taken a week to search the house that thoroughly."

I looked at the journal on the desk. My name. Her plan. Everything laid out in black and white.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "If I'm just a patsy, why does it matter if I know the truth?"

"Because I need your help." She stepped closer. "The plan was always to frame you. But plans change. Marcus has been talking to the police. He's been telling them about Clara. He's been planting seeds of doubt."

"So you need me to do something."

"I need you to kill him."

The words hung in the air between us.

I stared at her. She stared back. The gun didn't waver.

"You're insane," I said.

"I'm practical." She smiled again, but this time there was something else in her eyes. Desperation. Fear. "Tomorrow is the 15th. Tomorrow, Marcus dies. If you help me, I'll give you the watch, the files, and enough money to disappear forever. If you don't..."

She raised the gun higher.

"Then I'll shoot you now, plant the gun in Marcus's hand, and tell the police you tried to kill me. They'll believe me. I'm the fragile wife with the tragic past. You're the convicted criminal with a history of violence."

I looked at the gun. I looked at the journal. I looked at the photographs of myself, captured without my knowledge, filed away like evidence.

Every step I had taken since I arrived at this house had been predicted. Every decision I had made had been accounted for. I had walked into a trap that had been built specifically for me, and I hadn't seen it until it was too late.

"I need an answer, Anna." Her voice was soft again. Almost kind. "Are you going to help me?"

I thought about Marcus. I thought about Clara. I thought about the locked room in the east wing, the bloodstained shirt, the watch with the cracked crystal.

I thought about the 15th.

Tomorrow.

"Where's Clara's body?" I asked.

The wife's smile flickered. "Why do you want to know?"

"Because if I'm going to help you kill a man, I want to know what I'm getting into. I want to know what happens to people who cross you."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she walked to the filing cabinet and pulled open the bottom drawer.

Inside was a map. A detailed map of the estate grounds, with markings at the edge of the garden, near the old well.

"She's there," the wife said. "Under six feet of dirt and a layer of lime. No one will ever find her."

I looked at the map. I looked at the gun. I looked at the woman who had played me like a violin.

"You have until midnight," she said. "Either you're with me, or you're against me. And if you're against me..."

She didn't finish the sentence.

She didn't have to.

I stood in the center of the room, surrounded by files and photographs and a journal with my name on it, and I realized I had nowhere to go. No one to call. No one who would believe me.

I was trapped.

And the woman holding the gun was the only way out.
