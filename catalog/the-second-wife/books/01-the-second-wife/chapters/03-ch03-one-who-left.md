The note was wedged behind the dryer, yellowed and torn, as if someone had tried to flush it. "HE KNOWS. GET OUT BEFORE THE 15TH." The ink was smudged — by water, or by tears.

I read it three times, my fingers pressing flat against the paper, memorizing every curve of the handwriting. The letters were sharp, urgent, written by someone who was either terrified or running out of time. The paper was damp from the dryer's heat, the edges frayed where they'd been torn from a notebook.

I folded it and slipped it into my pocket, next to the photograph of Clara.

So she hadn't left willingly. She'd left in a hurry. Or she'd never left at all.

I finished folding the laundry — three towels, a bedsheet, two pillowcases — and carried the basket back toward the main hallway. My hands were steady, but my mind was racing. The 15th. That was two weeks from now. What happened on the 15th? A deadline meant something was coming. Something Clara had known about.

I found the gardener in the greenhouse, pruning a dead rose bush with shears that looked older than I was. He was maybe sixty, with leathery skin and eyes that had seen too much to be startled by anything.

"Excuse me," I said.

He didn't look up. "New housekeeper."

"Yes."

"I know who you are." He snipped a branch. "What do you want?"

"The woman before me. Clara. Did you know her?"

His shears paused. Just for a second. Then he resumed cutting. "Knew her well enough."

"What happened to her?"

"She left."

"Just like that?"

"Just like that." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were hard. "One morning she was here. Next morning she was gone. Didn't say goodbye. Didn't leave a forwarding address."

"Did she seem scared?"

The gardener set down his shears and wiped his hands on his pants. "Miss, I don't know what you're looking for, but you won't find it here. This house eats people. Clara was smart enough to leave before it ate her."

"Or someone ate her first."

He stared at me for a long moment. Then he picked up his shears and turned back to the bush. "I don't know anything about that. I just prune the roses."

I watched him for another minute, but he didn't speak again. The conversation was over.

I walked back toward the main house, the note burning a hole in my pocket. The gardener knew something. Everyone in this house knew something. But nobody was talking.

I found the locked door in the east wing just where Marcus had said it would be. The hallway was narrow, the wallpaper peeling, the air thick with dust. The door was solid oak, dark and heavy, with a bolt across the middle that gleamed like new metal.

I ran my finger along the bolt. The metal was smooth, cool, with a faint sheen of factory oil. It was new. Recently installed. Unlike the rusted hinges on the other doors in the house.

I tried the handle. Locked. I pushed against the wood. It didn't budge.

I knelt down and looked at the bolt more closely. It was bolted from the outside. Not the inside.

That meant someone was locked in. Not locked out.

My heart hammered against my ribs. I stood up and took a step back, my mind racing through possibilities. The wife had said it was the old nursery. But nurseries didn't have bolts on the outside. Prisons did.

I walked back to my room and closed the door. Then I pulled out my phone and dialed a number I hadn't called in six months.

It rang three times before a voice answered. "Yeah?"

"Tommy. It's me."

A pause. "Anna. Shit. I thought you were dead."

"Not yet. I need a favor."

"Of course you do." There was a rustling sound, like he was shifting in his chair. "What kind of favor?"

"I need you to run a name for me. Marcus — "

"Don't say the last name. I don't want to know."

"Fine. Just tell me what you can find on him. Criminal record. Anything."

Another pause. "You're working for him, aren't you? That's why you called."

"Yes."

"Anna, I told you. No more favors. Last time I helped you, I almost lost my license."

"I know. But this is different. This is — "

"This is what? Another job you're going to walk away from in three weeks?"

"It's a housekeeping job. That's all."

"Bullshit." But he didn't hang up. I heard him typing on a keyboard. "Marcus. First name only. Let me see what I can pull."

I waited, the phone pressed against my ear, my heart pounding.

"Okay," Tommy said. "I've got something. But it's not good."

"What is it?"

"Marcus had a prior criminal case. Assault. Five years ago. But it was dismissed."

"Dismissed? Why?"

"Witness recanted. Happens all the time. But here's the thing — the case was reopened three months ago."

"Reopened? Why?"

"I don't know. My source cut out before I could ask more." His voice dropped. "Anna, listen to me. Whatever you're doing, stop. Get out of there. That house has a reputation."

"What kind of reputation?"

"People who work there don't last long. They leave in a hurry. Or they don't leave at all."

"Tommy — "

"I'm serious. I can't help you anymore. I've got my own problems. Don't call this number again."

The line went dead.

I stared at the phone, my mind spinning. Marcus had a prior criminal case. It was reopened three months ago. Clara had left suddenly. The note said get out before the 15th.

Two weeks.

I had two weeks to figure out what was happening in this house before something happened.

The wife's face floated in my mind. The locket. The whispered warning. Don't trust the quiet.

I looked at my hands. They were shaking.

I had come here to rob this house. That was the plan. A quick job, a few valuables, and then disappear into the night. But now I was caught in something much bigger. Something I didn't understand.

I heard footsteps in the hallway.

I shoved the phone into my pocket and stood up, my heart racing. The footsteps stopped outside my door.

Then a knock.

I opened the door.

Marcus stood in the hallway, his face unreadable. He was wearing a different sweater than earlier — charcoal gray, cashmere — and his hair was perfectly combed. He looked like he'd just stepped out of a magazine.

"Anna," he said. "I see you've found the laundry room."

My blood went cold.

"Just finished folding the towels," I said, my voice steady.

"Good. My wife used to spend hours in there. Before she got sick." He smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. "She always said the laundry room was her sanctuary. The only place in the house where she could think."

I said nothing.

"She also used to leave notes. Little reminders. Lists of things she needed." He stepped closer, and I smelled his cologne — expensive, sharp, like pine and smoke. "Did you find any notes in there, Anna?"

My hand went to my pocket, where the note was hidden.

"No," I said. "Just towels."

He held my gaze for a long moment. Then he smiled again.

"Good. I wouldn't want you to find anything that might upset you." He turned and walked away, his footsteps echoing down the hallway. "Dinner is at six. Don't be late."

I watched him disappear around the corner, my heart pounding in my chest.

The note was in my pocket. The note that said HE KNOWS.

And I was starting to think he did.
