The cardiac monitor screamed, and Iris’s first thought was that the printer had finally answered.

She was already turning, already scanning the triage desk for the silver casing before her hand reached the alarm button. The monitor kept shrieking—Leads II and III flatlining—but the printer sat dark, silent, its empty paper tray untouched. No slip curling out. No fresh ink.

Iris hit the code button and forced her attention back to the bed. The patient, a woman in her fifties admitted thirty minutes ago for atypical chest pain, had arrested. The rhythm on the screen wasn’t shockable. Iris started compressions, counting aloud to keep her mind anchored. One and two and three—the printer hadn’t moved. She hadn’t seen a slip for this woman. But that was the problem now: every patient who so much as complained of tightness in the chest made her check the machine first, and every check felt like waiting for a verdict.

The code team arrived. Dr. Ovid’s voice came through the overhead, sharp and impatient, telling them to push epi. Iris kept compressions until her arms burned. When they called the time, she stepped back and pressed two fingers to her own wrist. Pulse steady. Alive.

She was alive.

The woman on the bed wasn’t. No slip, no warning, no intervention possible—just a heart that stopped without asking permission. Iris stood at the sink scrubbing her hands long after they were clean, watching the water run.

It had been three hours since Marguerite Vole’s whispered warning. "It only ever prints your handwriting." Three hours since Iris had left the memory-care corridor with that sentence burning in her chest. She’d come back to the ER and worked a full shift, but every time the printer stayed silent, the silence felt heavier. The machine hadn’t printed anything since Arthur Bell’s slip. Not a single label.

Either it had finished what it started, or it was waiting.

Iris dried her hands and walked back to triage. The empty printer sat in its cradle, paper loaded, power cord conspicuously absent. She hadn’t told anyone about it—not Dr. Ovid, not the charge nurse. What would she say? That the label machine had no plug and still managed to print two names that matched dead patients? That she’d shoved a patient into critical care based on a piece of thermal paper? They’d audit her. They’d sanity-check her.

She’d already sanity-checked herself. The answer didn’t change.

At eleven hundred hours, a paramedic brought in an elderly man with confusion and a fever of 102. Vitals borderline, but nothing that screamed imminent arrest. Iris took his history, ordered labs, and assigned him to the observation bay. As she wrote the triage note, her hand hesitated over the printed label on the chart—standard hospital sticker, machine-made, clean sans-serif letters. Nothing like the cramped, uneven font on Lena Zarate’s slip. Nothing like the handwriting-mimicking marks that had appeared on Arthur Bell’s label.

Marguerite had said the machine printed "her" handwriting. That meant the machine was somehow keyed to the nurse at the desk. But Iris had never touched anything inside that printer. She’d never even changed the paper.

She needed to see Marguerite again. The old nurse had been terrified, lucid for a brief moment, then retreated behind a meal-ticket crane. But she’d said something else, something Iris hadn’t fully parsed. "It followed me here."

"It."

Not an it. Her handwriting. The machine.

Iris handed off the observation patient to the next shift and told the charge nurse she was taking a late lunch. She walked the basement corridor, past the archival room where the clerk had forgotten the printer’s existence, and into the sealed wing of memory care.

The door code was the same. She punched it in and stepped through.

The day room was quieter than before. Two residents slept in recliners by the window. A television murmured a soap opera no one watched. Marguerite sat at the same table, a stack of paper meal tickets beside her, her fingers folding and creasing with mechanical precision. A dozen paper cranes sat in a pile. Blue, green, white.

Iris pulled out the chair across from her.

“Marguerite.”

The old woman’s hands kept moving. Crease, fold, turn. Her eyes were fixed on the paper, but they didn’t track the shape she was making.

“I need to ask you about the printer,” Iris said. “The one from 1963. The one that printed your handwriting.”

No response. The crane took form under Marguerite’s fingers, its wings sharp.

Iris reached into her pocket and pulled out the scrap of paper she’d brought—a torn corner from Arthur Bell’s label, the one she’d kept folded in her scrub pocket. She placed it on the table, smooth side up, the printed time facing Marguerite.

“This came from the same machine. Thirty years later. It printed my handwriting. And when a patient died at the time it gave, I couldn’t tell if I’d made it happen or if I’d just watched it arrive.”

Marguerite’s fingers stopped. Her gaze drifted to the paper scrap, then away, then back. Her mouth opened, closed, opened.

“They gave me a whole room,” she whispered. “A whole room with a desk. I used to fold cranes to keep my hands busy. But the paper they gave me had carbon backing.”

Iris’s pulse ticked up. “What do you mean?”

“The meal tickets. They’re carbonless—micro-encapsulated dye, pressure-sensitive. But in the old days, they used real carbon sheets. You’d write on top and the pressure would leave a mark on the copy beneath.” Marguerite picked up a crane and crushed it in her palm, then opened her hand. Blue smudges stained her fingers. “I used to steal the carbon paper. I’d put it behind the triage slips and write the same thing twice. Once on top, once underneath.”

Iris leaned forward. “Why?”

“Because I didn’t want to believe it.” Marguerite’s voice dropped. “When the machine printed, it didn’t use ink. It used pressure. The letters were pressed into the paper the same way my pen pressed through the carbon. I matched them once, under a magnifying glass. The capital letters were identical. Identical. Down to the crack in the upstroke of the V.”

The V. "Vole."

Iris’s breath caught. “The engraving in the archive room—on the bottom of the printer. It said "Vole, 1963".”

Marguerite looked at her with something close to recognition. “I put that there. After I made it stop the first time.”

“How did you make it stop?”

“I stopped writing.” She laughed, a dry, hollow sound. “For two weeks, I didn’t write a single thing. No charts, no labels, no notes. I dictated everything. And the printer stayed dark. So I thought I’d beaten it. But then a patient coded, and I had to chart the code. I wrote her name and time of arrest. That night, the printer spat out the same name and time.”

Iris’s skin went cold. “It copied your handwriting.”

“It copied "through" my handwriting.” Marguerite shoved the pile of cranes aside. Her hands shook as she picked up a meal ticket and flattened it. “I found a way to hide it. I buried the evidence where no one would look. But I kept one thing. One piece of proof.”

She reached into the pocket of her cardigan and pulled out a folded sheet of paper, yellowed, brittle. She slid it across the table.

Iris took it carefully. The paper was thin, with a crinkled texture like old carbon copy paper. On it, in uneven handwritten letters pressed deep into the fiber, was a patient name and a time.

"DOROTHY VANCE — 21:47."

The date at the top read "September 12, 1963." The capital D was twice the size of the other letters, the upstroke cracked and split. Iris turned the paper sideways and saw the faint indentations on the reverse side. Pressure marks, not ink. There was no signature, no nurse identifier, but the handwriting was plain—shaky, human, and somehow wrong. The way a machine might mimic handwriting if it had only ever seen letters through carbon-copy impressions.

“Dorothy Vance,” Iris said.

Marguerite flinched. “Don’t say her name out loud.”

“This is the one you tried to stop.”

“I tore the original slip off the printer and burned it. But the carbon copy stayed in my chart book. I thought if I destroyed the machine’s version, it couldn’t happen. But the patient had already died at 21:47. I couldn’t undo the time.” Marguerite’s eyes were wet. “I didn’t make it stop by burning the slip. I made it stop by never writing the name again. I erased every trace. I told myself the machine followed my hand, not the other way around.”

Iris stared at the capital D. It was identical to the one she’d seen on the bottom of the printer, engraved into the metal. "Vole, 1963." The same cracked upstroke.

The machine hadn’t started with her. It had attached itself to Marguerite first. Then Marguerite stopped writing. Thirty years of silence. Until Iris sat down at that triage desk and picked up a pen.

The printer had found a new hand.

“It’s still printing,” Iris said. Her voice came out flat. “It printed a slip for a child tonight. The child died. Then it printed a man’s name with a time, and I admitted him to critical care early, and I don’t know if that saved his life or if I just made the whole thing faster.”

Marguerite looked at her with something like pity. “You don’t know. And you won’t. That’s the secret it keeps. I spent thirty years asking myself the same question, and the only answer I ever got was a machine that didn’t speak back.”

Iris folded the carbon copy along its creases and tucked it inside her scrub top, against her chest. It felt cold and fragile, like a dead thing.

“I need to go,” she said.

“You need to stop writing,” Marguerite said. “Before it finishes what it started.”

Iris stood, her chair scraping the linoleum. The memory-care hallway stretched ahead, empty and fluorescent-lit. She walked quickly, one hand pressed over the hidden paper, her pulse counting out a rhythm she couldn’t quiet. She felt the absence of someone beside her—a partner, a witness, someone who could confirm she wasn’t inventing the shape of this. The corridor was too narrow. Too bright. She was alone in it.

Her pager buzzed at her waist.

She unhooked it and read the message from the ER front desk: "CALL MAIN. URGENT."

She found a wall phone at the end of the hall and dialed. The charge nurse picked up on the first ring.

“Callahan.”

“Iris, it’s Marlene. Your printer just printed again. A new slip.”

Iris’s throat tightened. “Who’s the patient?”

“That’s the thing,” Marlene said. “There’s no patient name yet. Just a time, and your initials in the margin beside tomorrow’s date.”
