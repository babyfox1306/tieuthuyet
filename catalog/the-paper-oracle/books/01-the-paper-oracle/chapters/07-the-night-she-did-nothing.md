'Your initials are on it,' the ER nurse said over the phone, and Iris nearly dropped Marguerite’s carbon copy into the sink.

She gripped the edge of the stainless steel basin, her knuckles turning the color of old parchment. The carbon copy, a ghostly grey imprints on yellowed paper, trembled in her other hand. The voice on the other end of the line had been young, terrified, a nurse named Sarah who had likely never seen a dead body outside of a cadaver lab. But the accusation in her tone had been sharp. The machine had printed a slip, and on that slip, pressed into the fibre of the paper as if by a heavy, cruel hand, were the initials I.C.

Iris stared at her own reflection in the dark window above the sink. Her eyes were hollows, the skin beneath them bruised by too many nights of fluorescent light. She pressed two fingers to the radial pulse at her left wrist. Thready, rapid, but there. One hundred and ten beats per minute. Tachycardia. Document it. Ignore the root cause.

The bathroom stall behind her was empty, the door swinging slightly on a hinge that needed oil. The hospital was quiet, that deceptive lull between the two a.m. rush and the four a.m. crash. Iris needed to move. Protocol dictated she return to the desk, but protocol did not account for a machine that seemed to know her handwriting before she even picked up a pen.

She folded the carbon copy and shoved it into the pocket of her scrubs, next to the lighter she didn't smoke and the small, sharpened pencil she used for crossword puzzles in the breakroom. She pushed out of the bathroom, the soles of her shoes squeaking on the linoleum. The corridor stretched out before her, a throat of white and grey, swallowing her whole.

The nurses' station was a island of harsh light. Sarah was standing by the admittance desk, clutching a clipboard as if it were a shield. She looked up as Iris approached, her eyes wide, darting to the machine in the corner.

The triage printer sat in its usual spot, a beige box that looked like it had been manufactured in the seventies and never updated. It hummed with a low, electric spite. The tray was empty, save for a single slip of white paper.

Iris didn't run. She walked, forcing her gait to be steady, professional. She was the senior nurse tonight. The charge nurse. Panic was a luxury for patients, not staff.

"Miss Callahan," Sarah whispered, her voice cracking. "It happened again. Just now."

Iris moved to the printer. She picked up the slip.

Patient: Arthur Vance.
Time: 04:12.
Room: 4.

The text was crisp, black typewriter font. But there, in the margin, a series of small, indented dots. Not ink. Pressure marks. Iris pulled the sharpened pencil from her pocket, positioning the medication cup she had snagged from a nearby cart over the paper. She rubbed the side of the lead gently across the surface of the label, shading the indentations like a child making a leaf rubbing.

The graphite settled into the grooves.

I.C.

Iris stared at her own initials, manifested in lead and paper. They were jagged, desperate. It looked exactly like the way she signed off on a difficult chart. Fast. Impatient.

"It's impossible," Sarah said, leaning over the counter. "I didn't see anyone print it. It just... came out. And that patient, Mr. Vance? He's stable. He came in for syncope, but his cardiac enzymes are negative."

Iris checked her watch. 03:45.

"Mr. Vance is in Room 4?" Iris asked, her voice flat.

"Yes. He's sleeping."

"Go check on Mrs. Gable in Room 2," Iris said, turning away from the machine. "Her BP was dropping when you last checked."

"But the slip—"

"Go, Sarah. I'll handle Room 4."

Sarah hesitated, then fled, grateful for the escape from the gravity of the desk.

Iris stood alone in the circle of light. The machine hummed. It was a stupid piece of plastic and gears. It had no brain. It had no eyes. It only responded to input. That was the rule of machines. Garbage in, garbage out. If it was printing her initials, then somehow, she was inputting them.

She looked down at the slip again. 04:12.

In the past, she had fought. She had intervened. Lena in Room 3, the young girl who had died exactly when the paper said she would. Iris had sweated, she had coded her, she had pushed the epinephrine, she had screamed for the doctor. The paper had been right.

Marguerite Vole's warning echoed in her memory. "The machine prints your handwriting."

If the machine was a mirror, reflecting her own intent back at her, then what happened if she stopped looking? What happened if she refused to play the game?

Iris crumpled the slip in her fist, then smoothed it out and tucked it into her pocket. She walked toward Room 4.

The hallway was dim, the emergency lights casting long, skeletal shadows against the walls. She felt a sudden, sharp pang of loneliness, a specific ache that had nothing to do with the hospital. It was the weight of an empty passenger seat, the silence of a phone receiver that only rang with bad news. She looked at the chair beside the nurses' station where a partner might sit, waiting for her shift to end. It was empty. It was always empty.

There was no hand to hold in the dark. No voice to tell her that the shadows were just shadows. She was the only thing standing between the living and the machinery of death, and tonight, she felt woefully under-equipped.

She pressed two fingers to her wrist again. Pulse: 98. Slowing.

You are the mechanism, she told herself. You are the nurse. You are not the victim.

Room 4 was quiet. Mr. Vance, a man in his sixties with thinning grey hair and a complexion like parchment, was propped up in the bed. He was breathing easily, the cardiac monitor showing a steady sinus rhythm at eighty-two.

Iris entered the room and moved to the vitals machine without making a sound. She did not wake him. She did not reach out to touch his shoulder, did not offer the reassuring smile that usually came so naturally, the mask she wore to hide the grinding exhaustion of the job.

She checked the leads. Secure. She checked the IV line. Patent. Fluids running at the prescribed rate. No kinks. No clots.

Standard care. That was the oath. Do no harm. But did doing nothing constitute harm when the alternative felt like participation in a murder?

She looked at the clock on the wall. 03:55.

Seventeen minutes.

Usually, this was the part where she would hover. Where her anxiety would drive her to check his pupils, to auscultate his lungs again, to test his capillary refill. She would be the aggressive advocate, the nurse who caught the drop in blood pressure before the monitor even alarmed.

But the slip had her initials.

If she intervened, if she treated him with the urgency that the printed label demanded in her gut, was she sealing his fate? Was she willing the reality of the paper into existence by acting it out?

Iris stepped back. She moved to the chair in the corner of the room, a stiff vinyl contraption usually reserved for grieving families. She sat down. She crossed her legs. She folded her hands in her lap.

She would do nothing.

She would observe. She would document. She would be a pair of eyes, nothing more. If Arthur Vance arrested, she would respond. That was the law. But until that moment, she would not be the agent of chaos.

She watched him breathe. The rise and fall of his chest. The mechanical hiss of the ventilator-less room.

04:00.

The hospital settled. The distant ring of a telephone at the front desk, muffled by distance. The squeak of a janitor's cart three floors up.

Iris felt the urge to stand up, to pace, to check his pulse manually. The itch was unbearable. It was the clinical instinct, the terror of missing something. What if it was a pulmonary embolism? What if the sinus rhythm was a lie, a mask for a massive MI about to break?

She forced herself to remain seated. She dug her fingernails into her palms, the pain a grounding wire.

04:05.

Mr. Vance shifted in his sleep. He let out a soft snore, turned his head to the left, and settled.

Iris stared at the cardiac monitor. The green line marched forward, steady, rhythmic, indifferent.

Think about Marguerite. The woman had worked this same floor thirty years ago. She had seen the same impossible paper. She had held the same weight. And she had quit. She had walked away and let her memory rot like old fruit. Why? Because she couldn't bear the guilt? Or because she had figured out that the only way to break the chain was to cut the cord?

Iris reached into her pocket and touched the carbon copy. The edges were soft.

04:10.

Two minutes.

The air in the room felt heavy, pressurized, like the inside of a submarine diving too deep. Iris found herself holding her breath. She watched the second hand of the wall clock sweep past the numbers.

Tick.

The monitor beeped. Normal sinus.

Tick.

Mr. Vance sighed, his eyelids fluttering. He did not open them.

Tick.

04:12.

Iris stared.

The clock hand passed the twelve. The minute turned.

The room did not explode. The monitor did not scream. Arthur Vance did not gasp his last. He simply breathed, in and out, the gentle, unremarkable mechanics of a human body continuing its function.

04:13.

04:14.

Iris let out a breath she felt she had been holding for years. Her shoulders dropped, the tension bleeding out of them like air from a tire. He was alive. It had lied. Or she had tricked it. Or the universe was just a chaotic place that occasionally threw a curveball.

She stood up slowly, her legs stiff. She walked to the bedside. She placed her fingers on his radial pulse.

Strong. Steady.

She documented the vitals in the chart. "Patient resting comfortably. No acute distress."

She walked out of Room 4, leaving the door slightly ajar as per protocol.

The hallway felt different now. Less like a throat and more like a hallway. The shadows were just shadows.

She needed to find the source. She needed to understand why the machine had failed when she refused to engage with it. She needed Marguerite's logbook. Not the carbon copy, but the original. The book where the woman had written her thoughts, her fears, the data she had collected before she ran away.

Iris bypassed the nurses' station. Sarah was busy on the phone, her back turned. Iris slipped past, heading for the stairwell. She knew where the old files were kept, the ones that predated the digital system. But she needed something more personal. Marguerite had kept a journal. The carbon copy had been a receipt, but there had to be a ledger.

She thought about the engraving on the cabinet in records. "M. Vole."

She went to the third floor, the administrative corridor where the air was always colder and the carpets were thicker. The door to the storage room was locked, but Iris knew the housekeeping override trick—swipe the badge upward, then tap the handle twice. The lock clicked.

The room smelled of dust and old paper. Rows of metal shelves stretched into the darkness. Iris clicked on her flashlight, the beam cutting through the gloom. She moved to the back, where the personal effects of retired staff were sometimes stored for years before being purged.

It took her twenty minutes of shifting boxes of ancient discharge summaries and rotted payroll sheets. Then she found it. A small, leather-bound notebook, wedged behind a box of rusted staples.

She pulled it free. The cover was cracked, the spine broken. It smelled of vanilla and stale cigarette smoke.

Iris sat on the cold floor, the flashlight propped on a box of files. She opened the book.

The handwriting was crisp, elegant, utterly unlike the rushed scrawl of a modern nurse. Dates from 1964. 1965. Notes on stocks, notes on weather. Then, the entries changed.

"October 14, 1963. The machine printed the name of the boy in Room 2. I tried to save him. I ran. I pushed the crash cart. He died at the exact minute."

Iris felt a chill raise the hair on her arms. It was the same. The exact same pattern.

"October 21, 1963. Another name. I tried to fight it. I called Dr. Ovid—he was just a resident then. He didn't understand. The patient died."

"October 28, 1963. I am tired. I feel like I am writing the script for them. Every time I intervene, the ink becomes real."

Iris turned the page. The handwriting became shakier. Less elegant. More desperate.

"November 5, 1963. The machine printed a name. Elizabeth Small. Time: 03:00. I decided I would not be the executioner. I sat in the closet. I did not go to the room. I let the night float by. I let her sleep."

Iris paused. Her heart hammered against her ribs. This was it. This was the experiment she had just unwittingly replicated.

She moved the flashlight beam to the next line. The handwriting had changed again. It was jagged, deep pits in the paper where the pen had gouged it.

Iris read the last legible entry, the final testament of a woman who had looked into the abyss and decided that blindness was the only mercy.

The printed time passed. The patient was still alive. Iris opened Marguerite's logbook to the final legible line from 1963 — the last words that woman had written before she stopped believing in paper.

"The night I did nothing, the paper lied."

Iris read it three times. The handwriting was shaky, pressed deep into the page, as if Marguerite had been fighting the pen itself.

She had not stopped writing because she was afraid.

She had stopped because she had proven something: the machine only worked if you played its game.

And Iris had just played the most dangerous move of all.
