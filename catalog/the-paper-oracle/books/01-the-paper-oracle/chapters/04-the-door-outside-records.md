The handle turned once from the other side, stopped, then turned again harder.

Iris froze, her hand hovering over the black plastic casing of the machine. The silence in the archives was so absolute that the click of the latch sounded like a bone snapping. The air here was stale, smelling of decomposing paper and the sharp, chemical tang of old laminate, a scent that usually promised safety and order. Now, it just smelled like a tomb.

Heart rate: one-twenty. Palms damp. Respirations elevated.

She scanned the room. No windows. One exit. The steel shelving units rose like canyon walls around her, blocking the path to the door. She had seconds before the intruder realized the lock was jammed or simply decided to force it. Machine safety was the priority. Protocol dictated the protection of hospital equipment, but this was not hospital equipment. This was an anomaly. A liability.

Iris grabbed the device. It was heavier than it looked, a dense, cold brick of metal and plastic that seemed to absorb the heat from her hands. She shoved it into the bottom drawer of the rusted filing cabinet to her right, burying it beneath a stack of rotting policy manuals from the seventies. She slid the drawer shut with a soft snick, just as the handle jiggled a third time.

She backed away, putting a row of shelves between herself and the door. She squared her shoulders, wiping her hands on her scrubs. If it was security, she had a reason. If it was a administrator, she was pulling files for Dr. Ovid. If it was something else—she pressed two fingers to the radial pulse on her left wrist. Thready, fast. Not steady.

The door creaked open. A beam of yellow flashlight cut through the dusty dark, sweeping across the floor. Iris held her breath, her back pressed against the cold metal of a shelf. The beam hit the empty table where she had been standing seconds ago, then danced over the chairs. A shadow stretched long and distorted across the floor.

"Hello?"

A male voice. Gravelly, tired. Not security. The night janitor, likely.

Iris waited. She did not speak. To speak was to reveal location, and location was power.

"Hell of a place to get lost," the man muttered to himself. The light swept the perimeter of the room, missing the cabinet where the machine lay hidden by inches. He coughed, a wet, rattling sound that triggered a clinical assessment in Iris’s head. Smoker. Possible COPD. Early signs of congestive heart failure.

The beam lingered on the far corner, then retreated. The door stayed open for a moment, framing the silhouette of a man in gray coveralls. He looked back into the darkness, his silhouette shrugging, then pulled the door shut. The latch clicked. The heavy footsteps scuffed away down the linoleum corridor, fading into the hum of the hospital ventilation.

Exhale.

Iris stood there for a full minute, counting down her heart rate. One hundred and ten. One hundred and five. Ninety-eight. When she could trust her legs again, she moved to the cabinet and pulled the drawer open. The machine sat there, innocent and mute in the dark. No lights blinking, no power cord visible, just the matte black finish that seemed to swallow the dim ambient light.

She picked it up. The plastic casing was cold enough to burn.

She hadn't come down here just to hide. She had come for context. She needed to know if this black plastic brick had ever existed in this building before, or if it had simply manifested like a tumor in the supply closet.

She turned to the long table against the wall, where the staffing ledgers were chained. It was an antiquated system, even for 1994. The hospital refused to digitize personnel records, citing budget constraints that everyone knew were just a stubborn attachment to paper trails.

Iris pulled the heavy ledger for 1963 onto the table. The cover was faux leather, peeling at the corners. The smell of dust intensified. She needed to find a name, a discrepancy, a signature. The machine had printed Lena Zarate. Then Arthur Bell. Both were patients she had triaged. But the machine itself felt old. The weight of it suggested an era before lightweight polymers. The font on the printed label—sharp, serif, impact-pressed—reminded her of the typewriters they kept in the basement for insurance forms.

She opened the book. Pages crinkled, dry and brittle as dead leaves. She ran her finger down the columns of names. nurses, orderlies, attendants. Shift schedules. 7 to 3, 3 to 11, 11 to 7.

January 1963. February. March.

Her eyes burned from the strain of reading in the low light. She found a small desk lamp and clicked it on. The yellow bulb flickered, casting long shadows that made the handwritten entries look like crawling ants.

She scanned the night shift logs. This was where the ghosts lived.

She stopped at August.

The entries for the ER nurses were neat, precise block letters. Then, halfway down the page, the handwriting changed. It was loopy, frantic, as if written in a hurry.

Nurse M. Vole.

Iris stared at the name. Vole. She turned the page. M. Vole appeared again on the schedule for the following week. Then the next. But in September, the name was crossed out with thick, angry black marker. Not just a line, but a scribble that obliterated the entry beneath it.

She looked at the margin. A transfer note, scribbled in a different hand—administrative, likely. Transferred to St. Jude’s Memory Care Ward. Date: September 14, 1963. Reason: Incident.

No details. Just "Incident."

Iris traced the letters of the transfer note. St. Jude’s was the state facility attached to the hospital, the place where they sent patients whose minds had unraveled beyond the scope of general medicine. But M. Vole was listed as staff, not a patient.

She looked closer at the ledger pages. The old staffing ledger used pasted paper tabs reinforced with yellowed surgical tape to separate the months. Iris touched the tape for August 1963. It was brittle, oxidized to a dark amber. But one tab had been peeled up and re-stuck so many times that lint was trapped under the adhesive.

Someone had checked this month. Repeatedly. Someone had been looking for Nurse M. Vole.

A shiver walked down Iris’s spine, raising gooseflesh on her arms. She checked her pulse again. Steady.

She closed the ledger. The information was circumstantial, a thread dangling in the dark. A nurse from thirty years ago, transferred to a psych ward after an unspecified incident, right around the time this machine might have been in circulation. It wasn't proof. It was a pattern.

And patterns were dangerous.

She looked at the machine sitting on the table. It looked back with its blind, dead eye of a slot.

"Did you belong to her?" she whispered. The sound of her own voice was jarring in the quiet room.

The machine, of course, did not answer. It was a hunk of plastic and gears. It did not have opinions, and it certainly did not have memories.

But the scraped brass rivet on the side caught the lamp's light. Iris frowned. She had noticed the scratch when she first found the device, a jagged line near the carrying handle that looked like wear and tear. But now, under the examination of the yellow light, the scratch looked different. It looked freshly polished. The brass beneath the black coating was shiny, bright, untarnished by the oxidation that covered the rest of the machine's screws.

It looked like it wanted to be found.

She picked up the machine, the cold seeping into her palms again. She needed to get back. The charge nurse at the desk, Maria, would have noticed her absence by now. If Maria came looking, questions would be asked. Questions required answers Iris did not have.

She slipped out of the archives, checking the corridor. Empty. The long hallway stretched out, lined with faded inspirational posters and flickering tube lights. The elevator at the far end hummed.

She walked quickly, her sneakers squeaking softly on the floor. The machine was a heavy weight in her hands, a secret she carried like a pathology specimen.

As she passed the waiting room, she glanced through the glass partition. It was empty, save for a man sleeping in the corner with a jacket over his head. The televisions were muted, casting a blue strobe light over the empty chairs.

She felt a sudden, sharp pang of loneliness. It hit her physically, a hollow ache beneath the sternum. It wasn't a panic attack. It was the specific, agonizing awareness of the empty space beside her.

In the movies, the nurse found the clue, and the handsome doctor stood by her side, his hand on her lower back, grounding her. They would share a look of grim determination, a silent partnership that said they would fight the darkness together.

Iris looked to her left. Empty gray wall.

She looked to her right. A vending machine glowing with soft light.

There was no one.

She thought of the term "Unassigned." It floated in her mind, a clinical placeholder for a bed that hadn't been filled, a role that hadn't been cast. In her life, the slot beside her was permanently unassigned. Dr. Reyes was a good doctor, but he was buried in paperwork and his own divorce. Dr. Ovid was a voice on a phone, a disembodied authority figure who cared about liability, not truth.

There was no partner. There was only the protocol, and now, this machine.

She gripped the machine tighter. The cold metal bit into her fingers. It was a poor substitute for human warmth, but it was solid. It was real. It was a mystery that required her, specifically her, to solve it.

"You and me," she muttered, the words dry in her mouth.

She arrived back at the triage desk unnoticed. Maria was on the phone with the lab, her back turned, arguing about a missing specimen. The ER was in the lull, the strange, deceptive calm that always settled before the dawn chaos.

Iris slid the machine back onto the designated shelf beneath the counter. It fit perfectly, settling into the dust outline of its own presence. She adjusted the cords of the glucometer to obscure it, hiding the black casing behind a tangle of wires.

She sat down on the stool and pulled a clipboard toward her, pretending to check inventory. Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the metal desk to stop the tremor.

It was 04:15.

Arthur Bell was in Room 3. He was stable, sleeping off a dose of nitroglycerin. The label had said 04:12.

Three minutes ago.

Iris spun her chair around and looked at the monitors. Room 3 showed a steady sinus rhythm. Oxygen saturation at ninety-four percent on two liters. He was alive. The time had passed, and he was breathing.

She let out a breath she felt she had been holding since 03:47.

The machine was wrong. Or she was wrong about what the machine meant. Maybe the times were not death times. Maybe they were something else. Admission times? Discharge times? The time of a complication?

It required more data. She needed to wait for the next slip. She needed to watch Bell.

She turned back to the desk. Her eyes drifted to the machine. It sat silent in the shadows.

Then, a sound. A mechanical whir, soft and high-pitched, like the sound of a camera rewinding. It was barely audible over the drone of the ER ventilation, but to Iris, it sounded like a gunshot.

She stared at the dark slot.

The machine vibrated. Just a twitch, a shudder of the plastic casing. Then, with a sharp snick, a small white rectangle of paper was ejected.

It floated down, landing on the Formica counter top.

Iris looked around. Maria was still arguing with the lab tech. The security guard was watching the entrance doors. No one was looking at the desk.

Iris reached out and picked up the slip.

It was blank.

She frowned, turning it over in her fingers. It was the same textured paper as the others. The same perforated edges. But there was no text. No name, no time. Just the stark, empty white space.

Defective? Or a message?

She brought the paper closer to her eyes, angling it under the harsh desk lamp. The blank slip seemed to mock her — a page that refused to confess. She turned it over, tilted it toward the light, held it against the dark backdrop of the counter.

Nothing. Just the faint, almost invisible imprint of pressure marks where the printer's mechanism had struck the paper without ink.

Then she saw it: a tiny indentation, no larger than a pinprick, in the lower right corner. She held the slip at an angle, letting the light graze the surface.

A single word, pressed into the paper without ink:

Soon.

Iris's breath caught. She looked at the machine. It sat quiet, its red light still blinking. She had not fed it paper. She had not touched the power cord. The printer had printed nothing — and yet something had been pressed into the sheet.

She pressed two fingers to her own wrist. Pulse was steady, but her hand was shaking. She didn't know if it was fear or something else.

She folded the blank slip and tucked it into her pocket. She would check it again under the microscope in the lab. She would find the source of the pressure mark. She would figure out who — or what — was leaving messages without ink.

The red light on the printer blinked again.

Slow. Steady.

Like a heartbeat that wasn't hers.
