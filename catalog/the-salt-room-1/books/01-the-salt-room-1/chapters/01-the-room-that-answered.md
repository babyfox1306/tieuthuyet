"Nora," the tape whispered from the speaker after Nora had already pressed Stop.

She pulled her hand back from the deck's power switch and stared at the reels. They were still. The spindles had stopped turning three seconds ago. She had watched them slow. She had watched them stop. Nothing in the room was moving except the dust motes in the afternoon light.

But the voice had kept going.

She waited for it to repeat. The speaker hissed with open-air silence—the kind of sound that meant the tape had ended and the machine was feeding back room tone. No voice. No breath. Just the flat, empty air of Agnes Vance's shuttered hotel.

Nora pressed playback again. The reels caught, spun forward, and the tape resumed its familiar crackle. She had digitized the first four tapes already. Catalogued them. Indexed them. They contained dictations, grocery lists, a radio program from 1983, and twenty-three minutes of what sounded like someone knitting. Nothing unusual. Nothing that explained why a dead woman's voice would say her name after the machine went silent.

She shut off the deck and set her headphones on the table.

The room she was working in had been the hotel's front parlor. Agnes had converted it into a kind of office sometime in the nineties—Nora could tell from the calendar still pinned to the wall, the yellowed edges curling around a date from 1997. The furniture was dust-sheeted. The windows faced the sea, but the salt fog had fogged the glass into translucence. You couldn't see the water. You could only hear it, a low constant push and pull that made the whole building feel like it was breathing.

Nora had driven up from the city that morning with two hard cases full of playback decks, cleaning solution, and a portable workstation. The legal transfer had come through six weeks ago. The lawyer handling the estate had called it an inheritance, but the word felt wrong. Inheritances were things you wanted. Things you had some prior connection to. The only connection Nora had to this building was a handful of summer memories so thin they might have been photographs of someone else's life.

She was here to do a job. Catalog the contents. Determine what was salvageable. Sell the structure if she had to, or burn it, or let the salt air take it back.

She was not here for a homecoming. There was nothing to come home to.

The quiet in the building pressed against her ears. She had noticed it the moment she stepped inside. The front door opened onto a narrow lobby with a registration desk, a framed painting of a ship, and a smell of old carpet and salt. But beyond the lobby, the corridor that ran toward the back of the hotel had a different quality. It was padded. Dead. The sound of her footsteps changed when she crossed the threshold.

Nora picked up her recorder and walked back toward that corridor.

The hallway ran past the kitchen, past a storage closet, past a door with a brass lock and a small sign that read SALT ROOM. She had seen the name on the hotel's original floor plan. A room where salt was stored for curing fish, or perhaps for preserving ice. The sign was hand-painted, the letters faded to a ghost of themselves.

She stopped at the threshold.

The air was cold here. Sharply cold, like the door of a refrigerator had been left open. She could see her breath cloud in front of her face.

She looked down.

There was salt on the floor. A fine white dusting across the threshold, as if someone had poured it from a shaker. The rest of the corridor was dry. The rest of the hotel was damp—everything had the tacky feel of sea moisture—but this spot was dry and white.

Nora crouched. She touched the salt with the tip of her finger. It was cold. Not cold like the floor. Cold like something that had never been warm.

She wiped her finger on her jeans and tried the door. It was locked.

The key was in the lock. Brass, tarnished, with a flat head shaped like a diamond. She turned it. The mechanism clicked, and the door swung inward.

Cold air spilled out like water.

The room beyond was small. Maybe ten feet by ten. The walls were raw wood, stained dark with age and salt. The floor was white—not painted, but coated in a layer of salt so thick it crunched under her shoes. There were no windows. The ceiling was low, and the light from the corridor didn't reach far.

Nora stepped inside.

The cold went through her jacket. Through her shirt. Through her skin, straight into her ribs. She had never felt cold like this. It wasn't the temperature of the air. It was the temperature of something that had absorbed warmth and never given it back.

She turned in a slow circle. The room was empty. No furniture. No shelves. Just the salt floor and the dark wood walls and the cold that kept pressing.

She noticed the brass key still in her hand. She had pulled it from the lock without thinking.

The metal left a white mark on her palm. Powder. Like frost. Like salt.

She stared at her palm. The white patch stayed for seven seconds, then faded into her skin.

Nora set her jaw and stepped back into the corridor. She closed the door, locked it, and left the key in place. She would deal with that room later. She had a job to do.

Back in the parlor, she sat down in front of her equipment and reached for one of the unlabeled tapes. It was the fifth one in the box. No label, no date, just a gray plastic case with a cracked hinge.

She threaded it onto the deck and pressed play.

The first few seconds were static. Then a low hum, the kind of sound that came from recording in a space with no sound treatment. Room tone. She recognized the signature of Agnes's recordings—she had heard it in the other tapes. But this one was different. The hum had a texture to it. A thickness.

Then a voice.

Nora's hand froze on the volume dial.

It was a child's voice. Soft. Distant. As if the child was standing in another room, facing away from the microphone.

"Can you hear me?"

Nora leaned closer to the speaker. The voice was young. Girl or boy, she couldn't tell. There was a wetness to the words, a slight moisture that reminded her of breath on a cold window.

More static. The hum deepened. Then the voice again, closer:

"Are you listening?"

Nora's throat tightened. She moved her hand to the Stop button.

But the tape kept playing.

"You said you would."

A pause. The sound of breathing. Slow, wet, uneven.

Then:

"Nora."

Her name. From the child's mouth. No question in it. No surprise. Just a statement, as if the child knew she would be listening. As if the child had been waiting.

Nora pressed Stop.

The reels stopped.

She sat there, heart hammering, and listened to the silence. The parlor was quiet. The sea was quiet. The whole building was quiet.

But she could still feel the voice. It was inside her ears now, a tiny pressure against her eardrums, as if the word had taken up residence.

She shook her head. Reached for a different tape. Her hand hovered over the case, then stopped.

The phone was in her pocket. She could feel the weight of it. She could call someone. Tell them what she had heard. Get a second opinion.

But there was no one.

That was the shape of her life now. Estangement. Distance. The careful architecture of isolation she had built over the years. She had friends, acquaintances, colleagues—people who knew her professional name, her reputation in the field. But none of them knew this building. None of them knew Agnes Vance. And none of them were allowed into this part of her life, because this part of her life was a door she had kept locked for twenty-two years.

Her hand stayed near the phone, but she did not pull it out.

Instead, she turned back to the deck. She ejected the tape and held it in her palm. The plastic was warm from the machine. It felt ordinary. Harmless.

She put it back in the box.

Then, without fully deciding to do it, she reached for the deck's power cable. She pulled the plug from the wall. The machine's lights died. The reels sat still.

She picked up her headphones and put them on.

Silence. Ordinary silence. The foam padding pressed against her ears, sealing out the world.

She counted to ten. Nothing.

Then, from the headphones, a sound.

Soft. Wet. Irregular. Seven clicks, as if someone was tapping a fingernail against a microphone. Then a breath. One inhale, drawn slowly, the way you would breathe before speaking a name you had waited a long time to say.

Nora ripped the headphones off her head.

But she had already heard it.

The tiny recorded voice was beginning to say her name again, from inside the dead machine, from inside the cold salt room, from inside a silence she had already stopped.
