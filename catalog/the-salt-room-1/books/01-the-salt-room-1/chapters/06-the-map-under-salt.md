"Do not follow Agnes's codes," Ruth said, which was how Nora knew there were codes.

The connection hissed, a long dry exhalation of static that filled the space between them like the salt dust on the floorboards. Nora pressed the phone harder against her ear, her thumb digging into the plastic ridge of the case. The signal here was terrible, a ghost of a bar in the upper corner of her screen, but her mother's voice came through with terrified clarity.

"I haven't found any codes, Mother," Nora said, keeping her tone flat. She used her forensic voice, the one she reserved for clients who insisted their background noise contained EVP. It was a voice that offered no purchase for emotion. "I'm cataloging reels. Agnes labeled everything by date and room number. There's nothing cryptic about it."

"Don't," Ruth whispered. The word cracked, brittle as dried varnish. "She used to hide things in the numbers, Nora. She liked patterns. If you find a sequence that doesn't look like the others, if you find a mark on the map, you leave it alone. You leave it exactly where it is."

"What map?"

"The guest map. The one she kept under the counter. Burn it."

The line went dead.

Nora lowered the phone, the black screen reflecting her own tired eyes back at her. The hotel foyer was silent, the air heavy with the smell of old wood and the cold ocean draft that seeped through the boarded windows. She stood alone in the center of the room. For a fraction of a second, she let herself feel the hollow ache in her chest, the specific, isolating yearning for a partner who would stand in the doorway right now and ask what was wrong. She wanted someone to share the weight of Ruth's panic, someone to validate that she wasn't losing her grip on reality.

But there was no one. The doorway was empty, framing only the darkened corridor leading to the back rooms. She was the sole point of contact in this vast, rotting structure. No hand to hold, no soothing lie to smooth over the jagged edges of the evening.

She exhaled, a sharp sound that seemed too loud in the foyer. Romance was a luxury for people who lived in houses that didn't whisper. She had the tapes. She had the equipment. She would have to be enough.

Nora dropped the phone into her pocket. The silence in the hotel was not peaceful; it was a waiting room silence. She moved toward the corridor, her boots making a dull thud on the carpet that had worn down to the weave. The air grew colder as she approached the Salt Room, the temperature dropping as if she were walking into a meat locker.

The salt crunched under her soles, a fine, crystalline grit that had been tracked out of the room and spread down the hall like an infection. It was physical evidence of whatever emotional catastrophe had happened here. Salt preserved things, but it also burned the earth so nothing could grow.

Nora stepped into the Salt Room. The shelves along the walls were lined with Agnes's collection, a chaotic archive of audio history. Reels of magnetic tape stacked like dark towers, plastic cases yellowed with age, and notebooks filled with her grandmother's jagged, precise handwriting. In the center of the room stood the worktable she had set up, the digital interface glowing softly, waiting for input.

She went straight to the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet. Ruth had mentioned a map. If Agnes had used codes, she needed the key.

The drawer stuck, swollen with humidity, and Nora had to brace her foot against the frame to yank it open. Inside, amidst a tangle of dry rot and paperwork, lay a large, folded sheet of stiff blueprint paper. Nora pulled it out and unfolded it on the table, weighing down the corners with two heavy reel cases.

It was a schematic of the hotel grounds and the coastline, drawn in Agnes's hand. The lines were sharp and black, outlining the shape of the building and the jagged cliffs beyond. But the map was not just topographical. It was annotated. Nora leaned in, squinting under the harsh LED lamp she had clamped to the shelf.

Agnes had divided the property into grids. N1, N2, N3 for the north wing. E4, E5 for the eastern gardens. And then, near the water's edge, the grids shifted. S1 through S10.

Nora traced the lines with her fingernail. S7 was marked on a rocky outcropping below the terrace, a place where the ocean crashed violently against the stone. She remembered that spot from her childhood, vaguely. It was dangerous, a tidal pool that filled and emptied with the violent surging of the tide, cut off from the beach when the water rose.

She looked closer at the S7 grid. The ink there was heavier, layered over itself as if Agnes had drawn it, then tried to erase it, then drawn it again. And there, trapped beneath the final layer of clear varnish that Agnes had used to seal the map, was a physical imperfection.

A brittle flake of dried seaweed was pressed flat beneath the varnish exactly over the rubbed-out pool mark, as if someone had laminated the coastline before admitting the paper had changed.

Nora stared at it. The seaweed was brown and dead, a micro-fossil of a specific moment. Agnes hadn't just drawn the map. She had preserved a piece of the location within it. Why varnish over a weed? Why seal a mistake?

"Coded," Nora muttered to the empty room. "Not dates. Locations."

She turned her attention to the reel cases she had pulled from the S7 shelf in the archive. They were labeled in the same system Agnes used for the linen closets. Reel S7-A, S7-B, S7-C. But the labels on these were different. Instead of "Guest Check-in" or "Weather Report," the text was abbreviated.

S7-A: "Wind/Stone"
S7-B: "Water Level"

Nora felt the hair on her arms stand up. The cold in the room seemed to intensify, pressing against the back of her neck. This wasn't just ambient recording. Agnes had been monitoring the pool. She had been recording the sound of the water, the wind, the stone. And she had hidden those recordings inside a grid system, just as Ruth had warned.

Nora picked up Reel S7-C. The label on this one was faint, nearly rubbed away, but enough remained to read: "Voices/S7".

She checked her watch. Three in the morning. The tide would be high outside, the ocean roaring against the cliffs, but inside here, the silence was absolute, waiting for her to break it.

Nora moved to her playback deck. She needed to hear this. If Agnes had coded the location, and if the damaged tapes from the nursery—the ones with two children—correlated to these codes, then S7 was where the answers lived. She didn't know what happened there, but the cold dread pooling in her stomach told her it was the center of the erasure.

She threaded the tape onto the machine. The magnetic ribbon felt greasy and old. She locked the reels into place and set the tension. The headphones felt heavy around her neck, a tether to the living world she was temporarily leaving behind.

She checked the levels on the computer screen. The waveform was a flat line at first, just the faint electronic hiss of the tape background noise. Nora adjusted the azimuth, fine-tuning the playback head to get the clearest possible signal. She engaged the noise reduction filter, a low rumble that made the speakers hum for a second before settling.

She pressed Play.

The tape dragged, a squeak of rubber against plastic, and then sound bled into the headphones.

It was the roar of the ocean, close and violent. Not the distant crash heard from the hotel windows, but the immediate, churning sound of water filling a hollow space. A wet, sloshing sound. Waves slapping against rock.

Nora closed her eyes, letting the audio paint the picture. She could smell the salt in the recording, the distinctive briny tang of low tide decaying in the sun. It was hyper-real. Agnes had used a high-quality microphone, probably a directional condenser, pointed right at the water.

Then, beneath the waves, a human voice cut through.

"Counting," Agnes said. Her voice was calm, clinical, completely detached from the violence of the ocean noise. "Marker one. Water rising. Wind from the northeast. Twelve knots."

There was a pause. The sound of pebbles grinding together underfoot.
