"Play past the break," Nora told the machine, and the waveform split into two recorded breaths.

She had extracted the fragment from the damaged splice three times, each pass running through a different restoration filter. The first pass removed the low-frequency rumble from the hotel's old wiring. The second pass pulled out the mechanical scrape of the reel. The third pass left only the breath sounds, isolated and raw. She watched the amplitude graph on her screen: two distinct inhales, offset by half a second, occupying different frequency ranges. The first breath rose and fell with a nasal quality she recognized as her own voice at seven. The second breath was shorter, the inhale catching at the peak, as if the child had smaller lungs and a thinner throat.

Nora selected the second breath and ran a spectral comparison against a sample of her own childhood voice from a home movie. The formants did not align. The second breath belonged to a different child.

She turned to the photograph propped against the monitor. The scratched-out face. She had measured the relative heights using the photo software. The erased child stood two inches shorter than Nora, her shoulder overlapping Nora's, their elbows touching. They had been arranged close together, the way children are placed when they belong to the same household, same care, same matching dresses.

Nora picked up the photograph and held it under the desk lamp. The scratched child's dress had the same blue-and-white trim as Nora's. Same fabric, same hem. Someone had sewn two identical dresses. She turned the photograph over.

Agnes's handwriting: "Girls, first week."

Girls. Plural. No names.

Nora set the photograph down and walked to the salt room. The cold pushed against her as she crossed the threshold. She knelt beside the first bed frame and examined the wall above it. Two sets of pencil height marks. One at her own seven-year-old height, with the initial N scratched into the plaster beside it in what looked like Agnes's hand. The other set two inches lower, the initial sanded away, leaving a patch of raw gouged plaster. She pressed her fingertip into the groove. It was precise, deliberate, the work of an adult who did not want any trace of the letter to remain.

She crossed to the second bed. The floor beneath it showed a lighter rectangle the size of a small rug, the wood protected from dust for years before the rug was pulled. The floor near the bed legs was worn smooth, the finish polished by a child's body sliding in and out of bed. She touched the wall behind it. Three parallel scratches, made by a small hand dragging nails repeatedly. She pressed her palm to the plaster. It was colder than the rest of the wall by several degrees. She exhaled deliberately and watched her breath fog more densely over that spot, as if the air remembered the child who had slept there.

The quiet wrongness settled into her chest. The cold was not uniform in the room. It clung to the second bed, the second hook on the wall, the scratched plaster, the worn floor. As if the absence had weight.

Nora recorded a voice memo: "Second bed shows floor wear, rug outline, three parallel scratches at child height. Cold concentrated at second bed location. Air fogging more heavily there. Room clearly arranged for two children. One child erased from all surface evidence."

She stepped over the salt ridge and pulled the door closed. The cold released her gradually, as if reluctant.

Back in the parlor, she sat down and pulled the tape tin from the box. The masking tape on its face was studded with tiny salt crystals that had grown through the adhesive, forming raised white beads exactly along the outline of a vanished handwritten initial. The first initial was clear: N. Next to it, the salt clustered thicker, obscuring a second letter. Nora tilted the tin under the magnifying lamp. The salt crystals were translucent, layered over a dark smear of graphite or ink. She dabbed a tiny drop of water onto the edge of the salt mass. The crystals dissolved slowly. She blotted the residue with a lint-free cloth and leaned forward.

A curved stroke became visible. The top of a D or a P. She could not determine more without damaging the tape.

She matched the tin to the log entry in Agnes's notebook. Tape 14. "Girls."

Nora pulled the digitized file onto her editing software. She checked the full waveform. Two clusters of voice activity in the first minute, separated by silence. She highlighted the first cluster and cleaned it gently.

She pressed play.

Agnes's voice came through the speakers, slightly warped by the degraded magnetic stripe: "Testing, one, two. Levels good. This is Tape 14, recording on July 9, 1994."

A pause. Papers shuffling.

Then Agnes, farther from the microphone: "Now both of you, one at a time."

Nora leaned forward. The silence before the children spoke stretched like held air.

A child's voice—high, careful, nasal in the way she remembered her own voice—said, "Nora."

Herself at seven. She remembered the stretch she used to put on the vowel, the way she liked to hear her own name fill a room. The tape held it perfectly.

A second child's voice began to answer, smaller and slower, the first syllable pushing into the air, and a harsh scraping noise cut across the sound exactly where the name should have completed.

The scrape was metallic, deliberate, three strokes in rapid succession. Nora did not need to compare it to the photo on her phone. She already knew the scratches in the plaster behind the second bed had the same pattern.

The tape continued. Agnes said something muffled. The hiss swallowed whatever came next.

Nora stopped the playback. She did not replay it. She sat back in her chair, the cold from the salt room pooled in her knees, and stared at the static waveform on the screen. The second child's name was a gap in the sound, a violence in the tape that mirrored the violence in the plaster.

She thought of what it would feel like to have someone beside her right now. An arm around her shoulders, steadying her. A voice that said I'm here instead of don't sleep in the salt room. She imagined the weight of another body close, the simple heat of a hand on her back. The thought hollowed her chest because there was no one. The hotel held only her and the salt and the tapes. She reached for the tape case and pressed her thumb into the salt crystals until they bit deep. The ache focused her. She let it.

She had two breaths, two height marks, two sets of hooks, two child bodies in a photograph, a log entry that said "girls," a salted initial, a voice that spoke her own name, and a scrape that murdered the second child's name before it could reach the air.

It was not proof of identity. It was not proof of relationship. It was proof of presence. Presence was enough to ruin the story her family had told her.

Nora opened the notebook and wrote down the frequency of the second breath, the measurement of the height difference, the pattern of the scratches. She wrote circling the gap where the name would be.

She played the tape once more, from the top.

Agnes: "Testing, one, two. Levels good. This is Tape 14, recording on July 9, 1994."

Pause.

"Now both of you, one at a time."

Her own voice: "Nora."

The second child began to answer.

The scrape cut across the syllable, and the name disappeared into three parallel strokes of erased sound.

Nora stopped the tape at the exact frame of the scrape. She zoomed in on the waveform. The damage was not a tape crease or a dropout. It was a deliberate scratch, the same three-fingered mark that a child had dragged into the plaster of the salt room wall.

She looked at the salt-encrusted tin. She looked at the photograph. She looked at the notebook entry that said only "girls."

She did not know the name of the child who had stood beside her in a matching dress, who had breathed into the same microphone, who had slept in the second bed, who had scratched her anger into plaster and then into the tape itself. But the erased child was no longer invisible to her. The space beside her in the archive was no longer empty.

It was waiting for a name.

The hotel settled around her. A floorboard creaked in the back corridor. The cold from the salt room seeped under the parlor door, pooling around her chair.

Nora pressed the tape case harder into her thumb and listened to the silence of the house that remembered two children, even if she could not.
