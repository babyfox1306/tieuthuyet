"Burn it," Ruth said, and Nora knew her mother had not asked whose name was on the tape.

The phone was warm against her ear. The voice message from moments ago hung in the archive room like a second layer of cold, and now Ruth had called back, live, as if the voicemail had been a warning shot and the real negotiation was about to begin.

"You heard me," Ruth said. "Burn whatever you found. The tape, the labels, the index cards. Everything. You can keep the equipment, but the evidence has to go."

Nora pressed her thumb to the ridge of the empty tape case on the desk. The plastic edge bit into her skin. "You knew her name."

A pause. The kind that carried weight.

"Nora."

"You knew Della's name. You knew she existed. You knew she was in that room with me, and you taught me to forget her. You taught everyone to forget her." Nora's voice stayed flat, forensic, the same tone she used when she calibrated azimuth alignment. "I need you to say it out loud. I need you to stop hiding behind the weather and the market prices and the long silences on the phone."

Ruth's breath came through the speaker, shallow and measured. "What did you restore?"

"Tape three. The one with the two-girl recording. Agnes labeled it corrected, but she didn't erase the voices. She just buried them under new labels. I found the original splice."

"Who else has heard the name?"

The question landed like a door slamming. Not an answer. A deflection. A mother who had spent twenty-two years controlling what her daughter could remember, still trying to control the perimeter of the discovery.

"I'm asking the questions now," Nora said. "Did you help remove her from the photographs?"

"You don't understand what you're doing."

"Did you write the instructions to delete her from the hotel records? Did you tell Agnes to stop writing the name in the logbooks? Did you—"

"Nora." Ruth's voice cracked, and for a moment she sounded old, tired, frayed around the edges like a damaged reel that had been played too many times. "I was trying to protect you."

"Protect me from what?"

"From carrying a dead twin through the rest of your life."

The words hit like a physical blow. Nora's hand tightened on the tape case until the ridges pressed white lines into her palm. The archive room was very cold, and the cold was very patient, and the cold had been waiting for this conversation to happen inside it.

"I don't remember her," Nora said. "I don't remember drowning, I don't remember a funeral, I don't remember losing anyone. But the room remembers. The tapes remember. The photographs remember where someone used to stand before you scratched her out. I remember nothing, and that nothing has a shape, and the shape has her name."

Ruth was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had shifted. Softer. More careful. The tone of a woman who had spent decades learning how to wrap hard truths in soft cloth.

"I can make this easier for you," Ruth said. "I can tell you it was an accident. That it was quick. That she didn't suffer. I can give you the pieces that fit together cleanly, and you can close the archive and walk away from the hotel and never have to hear her voice in your sleep again."

Nora closed her eyes. The offer hung in the air like a hand reaching toward her from across a salt ridge.

Comfort that falsified evidence. A story that had been polished smooth by years of repetition, with all the rough edges of truth sanded away until it looked like mercy.

That was Ruth's gift. The gift of elegant forgetting.

"I don't want the clean version," Nora said. "I want the tapes. I want the labels. I want the names you tried to erase. If the truth is broken, I want the broken pieces. I don't need you to smooth them down for me."

"Nora."

"No."

The word landed like a lock engaging.

Ruth exhaled slowly. "Then you're going to find things you aren't ready for."

"I already have."

"That was just the beginning."

Nora opened her eyes. The tape deck sat on the desk, its reels still, its counter frozen at 011. The index card with Della's exposed name lay beside it. The evidence of a life that had been deliberately unmade.

"Tell me one thing," Nora said. "Did you write the instructions? Did you tell Agnes to stop writing her name?"

Ruth's silence was the answer.

"I found blank spaces in the logbooks," Nora continued. "The same spacing. The same missing name. It wasn't casual forgetting. It was procedural. Someone went through every record after the lost summer and removed her from the sequence. Was it you?"

"The hotel was going to be sold. We couldn't have a dead child in the records. It would have raised questions."

"Ruth."

"The family had already decided. Once Agnes was gone, everything would be cleared out. Nobody would ever know there had been two Vance girls in that room."

"But Agnes kept the tapes."

"Agnes kept everything," Ruth said bitterly. "She kept the evidence, and she kept the silence, and she kept the cold in that room exactly the way Della left it. Your grandmother was a collector of secrets, and she never once gave me the chance to burn them before they could find you."

The word again. Burn.

Nora looked at the tape deck. At the cold equipment. At the window that let in the sound of the tide pulling against the shore below the hotel.

"I'm not burning anything," she said. "I'm preserving everything. That's what the archive is for. That's what I do."

"Then you're going to lose what's left of your memory of that summer."

"I don't have any memory left to lose. That's the point. You already took it."

Ruth made a sound that might have been a sob or might have been a laugh. It was hard to tell through the static of the connection.

"Promise me one thing," Ruth said.

"No."

"Promise me that if you hear something on those tapes that makes you want to stop, you'll stop. You'll put the reel down and walk away and never open it again."

"I can't promise that."

"Nora."

"I can't. If I stop, I'm still her. I'm still the girl who stood in the hallway and let her sister be erased. I'm still the daughter who learned silence before she learned speech. The only way out of this room is through the tapes."

Ruth's breathing had become ragged. "You sound exactly like Agnes."

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"It wasn't meant as one."

The call ended. Ruth had hung up without saying goodbye, the same way she had ended every difficult conversation Nora could remember.

Nora set the phone down on the desk beside the index card. Her hand was shaking slightly, the way it always shook after she had spliced a complex repair and the tension of concentration released. She pressed her palm flat against the desk surface until the tremor stopped.

For a brief moment, in the silence that followed Ruth's absence, Nora felt the lack of something she had never had. A presence she could turn to. Someone who would stand between her and the weight of what she was uncovering. A shoulder. A hand. A voice that said not to worry, that the darkness was manageable, that the cold in the room was just weather.

There was no one.

There had never been anyone.

Nora looked at the tape deck and let the evidence protect her instead. The metal was solid. The reels were honest. The hiss on the recordings was not a lie.

She picked up her handheld recorder and pressed Record.

"This is Nora Vance. My mother asked me to burn the evidence of my twin sister's existence. She offered me a clean version of the story in exchange for the truth. I refused both offers. The archive remains open. I am continuing the restoration."

She stopped the recording and set the device down.

The archive room felt larger now, emptier. The cold had settled into the floorboards and the equipment and the paper labels. Nora pulled on fresh cotton gloves and turned back to the shelves.

She had the name now. She had the evidence of the erased child in the salt room. But Ruth's reaction had confirmed something deeper: the erasure had been active, deliberate, and ongoing. Ruth had maintained it. Ruth had instructed Agnes. Ruth had trained the entire family into a silence that lasted twenty-two years.

Nora needed to find the physical trace of that instruction.

She moved through the archive shelves systematically, pulling boxes of records from the year after the lost summer. Hotel ledgers. Maintenance logs. Label inventories. Each volume had the same problem: gaps where names should have been, blank spaces three characters wide, consistent spacing that matched the length of Della V before the last two letters were added.

The pattern was unmistakable. Someone had gone through every document from August of that year forward and removed the name. The blanks were not tears or water damage. They were deliberate erasures, executed with the same blade and the same pressure and the same intent.

Nora opened a maintenance log from September. The handwriting in the margin was Ruth's. She recognized it from years of birthday cards and instruction notes left on the kitchen counter.

The note read: Remove extra name from all guest and family records effective immediately. Use correction tape where needed. Negative reprocess if photograph shows pair. — R.V.

Nora stared at the note. The ink had faded to brown, but the letters were still legible, still sharp, still carrying the authority of a woman who had decided that her daughter would grow up alone.

She photographed the page with her phone. Then she photographed the surrounding pages to establish context. Then she recorded a voice memo describing the note's location, condition, and content.

The evidence was clean. The handwriting was Ruth's. The instruction was explicit.

Nora set the log aside and kept searching.

In the deepest corner of the bottom shelf, behind a stack of unlabeled boxes, she found a single reel case that did not match the others. It was older, heavier, made of the kind of pressed cardboard that had been used in the early 1980s. The label on the spine was handwritten in Agnes's script, but the ink had been partially scratched away, leaving only fragments.

Nora lifted the case carefully. The cardboard was brittle with age. She carried it to the desk and set it under the work light.

The label read: TIDAL POOL. LAST DAY. The last word was partially smeared, as if someone had tried to scratch it out but had not finished the job. Below the location was a note in smaller handwriting: Fragile. Do not fast-wind. Do not play without full degaussing.

Nora's hands were steady as she turned the case over. The flaps were sealed with an old strip of masking tape that had yellowed and curled at the edges. She did not open it yet. She wanted to document the case before she disturbed the seal.

She photographed the label, the spine, the tape seal, and the edges of the cardboard. She recorded the dimensions and the approximate weight. She noted the smell of salt and oxidized plastic.

Then she turned the case over again and noticed the inner flap. A piece of paper had been tucked inside, visible through a gap where the cardboard had separated. She opened the flap carefully, using the edge of a cotton glove to lift the paper without tearing it.

It was a note. Handwriting she knew.

Ruth's.

If Nora asks for this one, call me before she hears it.

The ink was fresh. Not decades old, like the rest of the archive. This note had been written recently, within the last few years, and placed inside the case as insurance.

Nora sat back in her chair. The cold in the room had become personal. It pressed against her shoulders and her neck and the exposed skin of her wrists, as if it were reading the note over her shoulder.

She had not asked for this reel yet. She had not even known it existed. But Ruth had anticipated that she would, sooner or later, and had left instructions for someone to intercept her before she heard whatever the Tidal Pool recording contained.

The clock on the desk read 11:47 PM.

Nora looked at the sealed case. At the note. At the cold that had settled into the tape deck and the index cards and the exposed name of her twin sister.

She had found the next thread.

She had found the thread her mother had tried to hide.

And she had no intention of calling anyone before she played it.
