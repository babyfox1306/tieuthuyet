Day nine. 06:14. Lena. Status: aware. Choice: pending. I wrote the words and stared at them until the ink dried. The notebook had become a confession I was writing in real time, and I still did not know the final line.

The morning light crept across my desk, illuminating the edge of the external drive where the deleted footage lived. I had watched it again at 03:00, then again at 04:30. Grant's body halfway through Lena's door. The kitchen drawer gaping behind him like an accusation.

I closed the notebook and pressed my palms flat against the cover. The weight of Lena's offer from the stairwell pressed against my chest — not a demand, not a threat, but a question dressed in calm. Keep the originals. Preserve the truth. Walk away.

I had not walked away.

I opened the private archive one last time. The deleted interval. The honeytoken footage — Lena's scissors cutting the corner of the envelope with that surgical precision. The note I had written after she looked directly at my window on day five. I let each file load, watched each frame, then closed every window.

The courtyard was quiet when I checked the live feed. Lena's kitchen light flicked on at 06:33. Gray leggings. Black jacket. Running shoes. The same precise routine, but now I knew each movement was a word in a sentence I was only beginning to read.

She left at 06:41. Locked the door. Tested the handle. Turned toward the courtyard exit.

I grabbed my jacket and notebook and followed.

The morning air was cold against my face, the kind of cold that sharpens everything. I kept distance — two blocks, then three, letting her pull ahead. She did not look back. Did not slow at intersections. Her stride was longer than a morning walk pace, head up, scanning the street ahead.

But she did not take the route to the postal kiosk today. She turned left at the corner, crossed the street, and walked directly into the courtyard.

My courtyard. The one beneath my dark window.

She sat on the bench. The bench where I had watched her pause, adjust her shoe, check her phone. The bench that had always been in my frame.

I stopped at the courtyard entrance. She did not turn. Did not wave me over. She simply sat with her hands resting on her thighs, looking straight ahead at the empty fountain.

I walked to the bench and sat beside her.

She did not look at me. Her profile was sharp against the morning light — jaw set, eyes steady, the slight furrow between her brows that I had catalogued from fifty feet away but never seen this close.

"You didn't walk away," she said.

"No."

"You watched me mail the envelope."

"Yes."

"And you kept the footage."

"I did."

She exhaled slowly, a sound that was not quite a sigh. "Then we need terms."

I did not speak. The space between us on the bench was exactly the length of my forearm. Her hand rested on the wooden slats, palm down, fingers loose. Close enough that if I extended my hand, I could reach it.

I did not.

She spoke to the fountain. "Grant is manufacturing evidence to force me out before the settlement. The displaced objects — he moves things when he gets in. The broken doorframe seals. The missing mail. It's all designed to create a pattern. Instability. A wife who can't manage her own life."

She paused. The courtyard was empty. No Mae Torres watering plants. No delivery trucks. Just the two of us and the sound of traffic three blocks away.

"I've been documenting everything," she said. "Every object out of place. Every seal broken. Every piece of mail that disappears before I can retrieve it. But I don't have what you have."

"Grant entering your apartment."

"Grant entering my apartment without my knowledge or consent." She turned to face me for the first time. Her eyes were not angry. They were measuring. "That footage is the proof I need. But only if you agree to my rules."

"I'm listening."

She listed them like she had rehearsed them, like each one had been tested before it reached her tongue.

"No recording device inside my apartment. Not one. Not hidden, not visible, not passive. The courtyard camera never records nudity or sex. You disable it and cover the lens before you enter. No physical contact without a direct yes. No private image goes to Grant. No decisions made on my behalf. Consent is withdrawable without negotiation. You preserve evidence — you never fabricate it. And I decide when this alliance ends."

She paused. Her hand had not moved from the bench.

"The certified envelope stays sealed," she said. "I don't tell you what's inside until I decide you need to know. That's non-negotiable."

I thought about the footage. The four minutes of Grant's body halfway through her door. The kitchen drawer gaping. The envelope with the clipped corner. The tracking number she had recorded.

I thought about what I would lose if I said no. What she would lose. What Grant would win.

And I thought about the word I had written in my notebook at 06:14. Choice: pending.

"I agree," I said.

She did not smile. Did not relax. She simply nodded once, as if she had expected this outcome and was already moving to the next step.

"You keep sending Grant edited reports," she said. "Staged behavior. Me doing the things he expects me to do. The performance continues. He needs to believe the surveillance is working."

"He'll want more footage. Longer intervals."

"Then you give him longer intervals. Of me performing stability. I'll give you material to work with." She finally looked at her hand on the bench, then back at me. "Can you do that? Lie to him directly, every day, knowing what he's doing?"

"I've been lying to him since day three," I said. "When I stopped sending him the footage of you looking at my window."

Something shifted in her expression. Not trust exactly — trust was too soft a word for what passed between us. Recognition. The acknowledgment that we had both been playing a game and had only just discovered we were on the same side.

She stood. The movement was fluid, unhurried. "Then let's begin."

I stood with her. My notebook was in my jacket pocket, the cover warm against my ribs.

"One more thing," she said. "When you cross my threshold, you leave the observer behind. You're not watching me anymore. You're witnessing me. There's a difference."

I opened my notebook. The pen felt heavier than it had at 06:14. I wrote:

'Day nine. Lena. Status: ally. Terms accepted.'

I closed the notebook and followed her across the courtyard.

The walk to her building took thirty seconds. Thirty seconds of her keys in her hand, her shoulders straight, her stride measured. Thirty seconds of me counting the steps and feeling the weight of every choice I had made since day one.

She stopped at her door. The lock looked new — a different model than the one Grant had bypassed. She inserted the key, turned it, and paused with her hand on the knob.

"You understand what this means," she said. "You step inside, and you're no longer an observer. You're accountable."

"I understand."

She opened the door.

I stepped across the threshold into Lena Hart's apartment. The courtyard camera was disabled, the lens covered. She stood at the kitchen counter, watching me enter the space I had only ever seen through a lens.

The air smelled different here. Coffee and paper and something floral I could not name. The kitchen drawer that Grant had left gaping was closed now. The utility envelope was gone from the counter.

She did not speak. She simply watched me absorb the space — the bookshelves, the photographs, the small clay bowl on the windowsill filled with keys.

I opened my notebook and wrote:

'Day nine. 14:37. First entry. Not observation. Permission.'

The word felt heavier than any lie I had ever told Grant.
