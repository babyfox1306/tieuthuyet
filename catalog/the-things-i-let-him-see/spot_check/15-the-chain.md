Day thirteen. 07:03. I woke in Lena Hart's apartment with the weight of her planner still pressed into my memory and the knowledge that I had never been watching — I had been watched. She was already at the kitchen table, the paper planner open, Mae's printed audit beside it. She did not look up. She said, "It's time to make this unkillable."

I sat up on the couch, the blanket pooling at my waist. The morning light cut across the table in a sharp rectangle, illuminating the edges of the planner pages. Lena's finger traced a line of her own handwriting — dates, times, observations I had never seen her make.

"How?" I asked.

She lifted her gaze. "We reconstruct the chain. Every piece. In front of witnesses. Then we distribute it so no single person can bury it."

I reached for my field notebook, but Lena shook her head. "Not yet. Wait for Mae."

I checked my watch. 07:11. The apartment felt different in daylight — smaller, less like a stage and more like a workshop. The kitchen counter held the utility envelope, still pristine, propped against the backsplash like a bookmark. The door-seal planner sat beside it, the one with the grid of times and dates she had shown me the night before.

I said nothing. I watched her organize the table.

At 07:23, three knocks came at the door. Lena crossed the room, checked the peephole, and opened it. Mae Torres stepped inside, the cookbook from the board secretary's office tucked under her arm. She was still in her morning clothes — navy sweater, reading glasses pushed into her hair, the same anxious energy she'd carried through the board meeting.

"Sorry," Mae said, pulling the cookbook free. "I didn't sleep. Kept thinking about what happens if someone finds it before we're ready."

She set the book on the table and opened it to the middle pages. The printed master-access audit slid out — three pages of paper, the ink slightly curled at the edges. Mae placed them beside the planner with the same care a crime scene tech would use on evidence.

"All there," she said. "Every time Grant's credential accessed Lena's unit. Every time the system logged an override. The deleted intervals match the gaps in your footage."

Lena nodded once. "Good. Let's start."

She pulled a chair out for Mae and gestured for me to sit across from her. I took the seat, my back to the window, the morning light now falling across the papers between us.

Lena began with the tracking number. She recited it from memory — a string of digits I had watched her input at the postal kiosk on day seven. She wrote it on a fresh sheet of paper, then slid the planner toward me. "You saw me clip the corner. Describe it."

I hesitated. "Lower-right corner. Small folding scissors. You cut with surgical precision."

"Good." She reached into her bag and retrieved the envelope itself — the certified envelope she had mailed, now opened along the top seam. She pulled out the contents: a single sheet of paper, folded in thirds. She unfolded it and placed it on the table.

The honeytoken phrase. "Friday key return."

I felt the weight of the words settle in my chest. Grant had used that exact phrase in his message on day ten, asking if Lena seemed ready for a Friday key return. I had repeated it to Lena, and she had shown me the false packing checklist sealed in the envelope. Now the phrase sat on the table in Lena's handwriting, dated, time-stamped by the postal machine.

"He used those words," I said. "Grant. When he called me. He wanted to know if you were ready to hand over the keys."

Mae flinched. "He said that?"

"He did." I pulled out my phone and opened the message log. The screenshot I had taken of Grant's instruction — the exact wording, the timestamp, the contact name — was still saved. I placed the phone on the table. "He wrote it. Not just said it. There's a record."

Lena studied the screen without touching it. "Then that's the second link. Third, if you count the clipped corner."

We worked through the rest of the chain in silence, each piece laid flat and verified by at least two of us.

The original lobby footage from my hard drive — the unedited files showing Grant entering Lena's unit on the four deleted intervals. I pulled them up on my laptop, each timestamp matching the gaps in Mae's audit. Lena watched the playback without expression. Mae turned away after the first few seconds.

The notebook timestamp of the first deleted interval — 08:12:47 to 08:16:03 — written in my field notebook on day six, before I knew the footage had been tampered with. Lena compared it to Mae's audit. "Three-second variance," she said. "That's the system clock drift. Acceptable."

The door-seal planner with its grid of dates and times — the physical record of every time I had entered Lena's apartment to set or check the cameras. Lena had recorded each visit in the planner, noting the seal condition before and after. She slid it across the table. "Your handwriting matches the notebook."

I flipped through the pages. I had never seen her write those entries. She had been documenting my movements the same way I had been documenting hers.

The original surveillance contract — the one Grant had signed, the one I had signed, the one that specified the scope of observation and the prohibition on entering the subject's unit. Lena had a copy in the planner's back pocket, printed on the same day she had first detected the camera in her kitchen.

"Four minutes of deleted footage," Lena said, tapping the paper. "Grant's credential accessing my unit. My kitchen drawer open. Your notebook timestamp. Mae's audit showing the same deletion. The contract he violated."

She looked at me. "Now we make it unkillable."

Mae cleared her throat. "What does that mean exactly?"

I answered before Lena could. "It means I make one encrypted duplicate. Right here. Right now. And I give it to someone who doesn't work for Grant."

Lena's eyes stayed on me. "Who?"

"Detective Imani Cross."

The name hung in the air. Mae looked between us. "You know a detective?"

"From before," I said. "My father worked with her. She knows how evidence gets buried. She also knows how to keep it alive."

Lena considered this. "You trust her?"

"I trust that she doesn't trust me. That's better. She'll verify everything before she acts. She won't move on my word alone."

Lena nodded. "Then do it."

I pulled the black USB drive from my bag — the one with the scratched label, the cross-hatch pattern I had marked over the factory serial. I connected it to my laptop and began the encryption process. The software I used was open-source, auditable, the kind that left a paper trail anyone could verify.

Mae watched over my shoulder. Lena sat across from me, her hands flat on the table.

I copied the lobby footage first — the unedited files, the deleted interval archive, the timestamps that matched Mae's audit. Then the surveillance contract, scanned and timestamped. The screenshot of Grant's instruction message. The evidence index I had compiled overnight: every piece, every witness, every timestamp.

The encryption progress bar crept across the screen. I felt the weight of each file leaving my sole custody.

Lena's hand landed on my wrist.

Not hard. Not stopping me. Her palm rested against the inside of my forearm, her fingers wrapping just enough to ground me. I stopped breathing for a second.

"You're sure," she said.

It was not a question.

I met her eyes. The morning light caught the edge of her face, the line of her jaw, the calm she wore like armor.

"If I disappear, the evidence doesn't. That was the point."

She held my gaze for a long moment. The encryption bar reached ninety-three percent. I watched her watch me, and I knew — she was testing whether I meant it. Whether I would break the chain the moment she looked away.

I did not.

She removed her hand. The warmth of her palm stayed on my skin for another three seconds, then faded.

The encryption completed. I ejected the drive and held it up. "One copy. No backups. No second drive."

Lena reached for the USB. I passed it to her without hesitation. She turned it over in her fingers, studying the scratched label, the cross-hatch pattern.

"How does Detective Cross verify this is the only copy?" she asked.

"She doesn't. Not by looking at it. She verifies by the chain — the witnesses, the timestamps, the audit trail. The drive is just the container. The evidence is the content."

Lena slid the drive into an envelope. "Then we need her here."

We called Cross at 08:14. I used my phone, speaker mode, Lena and Mae silent beside me. Cross answered on the second ring.

"Reed. You're on speaker."

"I know. I'm at Lena Hart's apartment. There's something you need to see."

A pause. "Is this about your father's case?"

"No. This is separate. New. I have evidence of a crime — manufacturing evidence, tampering with a surveillance system, breaking into a unit under observation. I have witnesses, physical records, and one encrypted duplicate."

Another pause. Longer this time.

"Anyone else in the room?"

"Mae Torres, condominium board secretary. Lena Hart, the subject of the surveillance."

Cross let out a breath. "I'll be there in twenty minutes. Don't move anything."

She arrived at 08:33. I met her at the door — a woman in her late forties, dark hair pulled back, coat unbuttoned, badge visible on her belt. She scanned the room before she entered, cataloging exits, windows, faces. Professional habit.

Lena stood by the table, arms loose at her sides. Mae sat in the corner, hands clasped in her lap.

Cross did not sit. She stood at the edge of the table, looking at the papers spread across it. "Walk me through it."

I did. The surveillance contract. The honeytoken envelope. The clipped corner. Grant's instruction using the phrase "Friday key return." The unedited lobby footage. The notebook timestamp of the deleted interval. The door-seal planner. Mae's printed audit. The evidence index.

Cross listened without interrupting. When I finished, she picked up the evidence index and read it cover to cover. Then she set it down and looked at Lena.

"You knew," Cross said. "From the beginning."

"Yes."

"And you let him keep watching."

"I let him see what I wanted him to see. Everything else I kept for myself."

Cross turned to me. "And you gave her your footage."

"I gave her what she needed to verify her own chain."

Cross picked up the envelope containing the encrypted duplicate. "This is the only digital copy?"

"It's the only encrypted duplicate. My originals are on a hard drive in my apartment. Mae's audit is physical. This drive is the third point in the triangle."

Cross weighed the envelope in her hand. "One copy. One witness. One detective. That's not a triangle, Reed. That's a thread."

"Then add more witnesses," Lena said. "Keep the drive in a safe you control. Don't open it until you've verified the chain independently. The evidence survives even if Calder disappears or changes sides."

Cross studied Lena for a long moment. Then she nodded.

"I'll verify the chain of custody. I'll preserve the duplicate. I'll remain available as a witness. But I'm not making an arrest based on one encrypted drive and a stack of paper. You understand that."

Lena nodded. "I don't want an arrest. I want the evidence to outlast any single person."

Cross slipped the envelope into her coat pocket. "Then I'll be waiting for the phone call."

She did not say whose phone call. She did not have to.

She walked to the door, paused, and looked back at me. "Your father would have done it the same way. One copy. One witness. One detective who doesn't trust anyone."

She left.

The door clicked shut.

Mae let out a breath she had been holding since Cross arrived. Lena stood motionless, her eyes on the door, her hands still flat on the table.

I picked up my field notebook. Opened it to the next blank page.

I did not write anything.

I closed it and set it down.
