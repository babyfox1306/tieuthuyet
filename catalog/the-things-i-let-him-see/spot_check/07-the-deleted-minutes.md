Day six. 06:14. I wrote: "Lena. Status: still unaware of what I saw last night, still performing for a watcher I'm no longer sure she needs to convince." I crossed out the last twelve words and wrote: "Status: unaware." The notebook didn't need to know I'd stopped believing my own reports.

I closed the notebook and opened the private archive instead of the live feed. I knew I shouldn't. The day hadn't started yet. Lena's bedroom light was still off. I had twenty minutes before her routine began, and I spent them watching the clip I'd saved from yesterday—her face turned toward my window, three seconds of deliberate stillness, the morning light catching the edge of her jaw.

I watched it three times. Then I forced myself to close the file and switch to the live cameras.

The courtyard was empty. Gray light. No movement. I checked the camera status lights—all green—and ran my thumb along the edge of the notebook, waiting.

06:32. Lena's bedroom light turned on.

I watched the shadow move across the window. Shoulder. Arm. Head tilted to the side. She was getting dressed, and I looked away out of habit, focusing on the empty courtyard until I heard the soft click of her apartment door through the hallway monitor.

06:41. She stepped into the hallway.

Gray leggings. Black jacket. Same running shoes. She locked the door, tested the handle, and turned toward the courtyard exit. I tracked her through the common-area camera, watching her feet land exactly where they'd landed yesterday. Same stride length. Same arm swing. Same head height.

She reached the entrance threshold and stopped.

I held my breath. Yesterday she'd paused for five seconds. Today she paused at the threshold—the same spot where the camera status light had reflected three days ago—and did not look up. She adjusted her bag strap. She shifted her weight. She stood there for three full seconds, one foot in the courtyard light, one foot in the hallway shadow, and did not turn her head toward my window.

Then she stepped through and walked across the courtyard without looking back.

The absence of her gaze felt heavier than her direct look had yesterday. I stared at the empty threshold, waiting for her to turn, to pause, to give me something, but she kept walking, head down, hands in her jacket pockets, until she disappeared through the building's main exit.

I exhaled. I hadn't realized I'd been holding my breath again.

06:47. I checked the common-area dashboard feed out of habit—the compressed version Grant received, the one with the timestamp log running along the bottom edge.

Something was wrong.

I scrolled back. 08:12:47. Then 08:16:03. A gap. Four minutes missing from the log. No footage between those timestamps. No explanation.

I checked the feed again, refreshing the portal, thinking it was a loading error. The gap remained. 08:12:47 to 08:16:03. One hundred and ninety-six seconds. Deleted.

My thumb stopped moving along the notebook edge.

I opened my local recording system—the unedited originals, the files that never touched Grant's dashboard, the archive I kept for myself. I found the timestamp range and pulled up the full feed.

The footage was there. All four minutes. Preserved.

I started the playback.

08:12:47. The common-area camera showed an empty hallway. The courtyard beyond was gray and still. Lena had been gone for several minutes by this point, already well into her walk.

08:13:02. A figure appeared at the far end of the hallway.

I leaned closer to the monitor.

08:13:14. The figure resolved into Grant Hart. He was wearing dark slacks and a blue button-down, the same clothes he'd worn when he hired me. He carried nothing in his hands. No bag. No papers. No excuse.

08:13:29. Grant stopped at Lena's apartment door. He pulled a key card from his pocket—the master credential, the one that opened every unit in the building, the one only the condominium board president carried.

I stopped breathing.

08:13:31. Grant swiped the master credential. The lock clicked open. He pushed the door, stepped inside, and closed it behind him.

I rewound the footage and watched it again. Then again. The third time, I froze the frame on his face—his hand on the master credential, his body halfway through the door, the kitchen drawer visible behind him.

He was inside her apartment. While she walked. While I watched the empty courtyard, staring at the threshold where she'd paused, not seeing what I should have seen.

I fast-forwarded through the next two minutes. The hallway remained empty. No sound from the monitor inside Lena's apartment—she had no cameras there, only the kitchen unit that covered the common areas. Grant was in the kitchen, off-camera, doing something I couldn't see.

08:15:47. The door opened. Grant stepped out, closed it behind him, and walked back down the hallway. His hands were empty. His expression was neutral. He looked exactly like a man who had every right to be there.

08:16:03. The hallway was empty again. The timestamp jumped to 08:16:03 in the dashboard feed.

Grant had deleted the interval. He had removed the evidence of his own entry, knowing the dashboard log would show a clean gap, knowing I might not notice, knowing the four minutes would simply disappear.

He hadn't counted on my local archive.

I saved the unedited clip to a new folder in my private archive. I labeled it: "Day Six—Grant Entry. Credential: Master. Duration: 08:13:31–08:15:47. Omitted from dashboard log."

Then I opened the kitchen monitor feed from Lena's apartment and scrolled back to the same time window.

08:13:45. The kitchen camera showed the counter, the stove, the refrigerator. No movement. But when I checked the drawer position—the one beneath the counter, the one where Lena kept her utility bills and receipts—it was slightly open. Not fully. Just a centimeter, a gap I would have missed if I hadn't been looking for it.

Grant had opened that drawer. He had moved something inside it. He had closed it, but not completely.

I saved that frame too.

I ran my thumb along the edge of my notebook, pressing hard enough to feel the cardboard bend. I opened the page where I'd written "Status: unaware" and stared at the words.

I wasn't documenting instability. I was documenting a crime.

Grant was manufacturing the evidence. The displaced mug. The missing utility notice. The crumpled envelope in the recycling bin. Every piece of "proof" that Lena was forgetting things, losing things, becoming unstable—it was him. He was entering her apartment while she walked, moving objects, planting doubt.

And she knew.

She knew someone was watching. She knew someone was inside her space. She knew, and she had been telling me, and I hadn't seen it until now.

I looked at the frozen frame of Grant's entry—his hand on the master credential, his body halfway through the door, the kitchen drawer open behind him. I looked at the timestamp. 08:13:31. She had left at 06:41. She had paused at the threshold, adjusted her bag strap, shifted her weight, and stepped through without looking back.

She hadn't looked back because she knew he would come. She had timed her walk. She had given him the window. She had been trying to show me what he was doing.

I whispered into the empty apartment: "She knew. She was telling me, and I didn't see it until now."

The words hung in the air. I stared at the frozen frame, at Grant's face, at the evidence I now held in my private archive.

I couldn't give it to Grant. He was the one who deleted the interval. He was the one who entered her apartment without permission. He was the one manufacturing the instability I was supposed to document.

I couldn't give it to the police. Not yet. Not without understanding what I was holding. One clip of a man entering his own separated wife's apartment while she walked—it wasn't enough. Not without context. Not without knowing what he had done inside.

I could only keep it. Preserve it. Wait.

I closed the private archive, opened the dashboard feed, and pulled up Grant's daily report template. The cursor blinked in the empty text field.

"Day six."

I typed the words. I stared at them.

"06:14. Subject's morning routine showed no notable deviation from established baseline."

I typed the lie. It felt different today. Not automatic. Not convenient. Deliberate.

"Subject left for morning walk at 06:41. Returned at 07:19. No notable activity during absence."

I saved the report. I closed the dashboard. I opened my field notebook and wrote a single line beneath the morning's crossed-out words:

"Dashboard interval 08:12-08:16 deleted by client. Local archive preserved. First confirmed entry. Omitted from report."

I closed the notebook and stared at the frozen frame of Grant entering Lena's apartment. His hand on the master credential. His body halfway through the door. The kitchen drawer open behind him.

I saved the clip to the private archive, closed the dashboard, and opened Grant's daily report template.

I typed: "Day six. No notable activity."

The lie sat on the screen, and for the first time, I didn't feel guilty. I felt righteous.
