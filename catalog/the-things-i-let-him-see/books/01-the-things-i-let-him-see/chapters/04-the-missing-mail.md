Day three. 06:14. Lena. Status: still unaware, still watching me without knowing she's watching. I crossed out the last five words before I could read them again. The notebook didn't need to know what I was starting to feel.

I set the pen down and leaned back in the chair. The monitor showed Lena's apartment in grayscale—kitchen dark, bedroom curtains drawn, living room empty. Standard pre-dawn stillness. Everything where it should be.

Except I couldn't stop running the previous day through my head. That moment in the courtyard. The way she'd stopped at the threshold, turned her head at an angle that shouldn't have caught my reflection, and held perfectly still. Three seconds. Maybe four. Long enough for me to kill the zoom and drop the angle, heart hammering against my ribs like I was the one being watched.

I pulled up the footage again. Frame by frame. 15:42:13—she steps into the courtyard. 15:42:16—her head rotates approximately fifteen degrees to the left. 15:42:19—her eyes track something in the upper window line of the building across from hers. My building. My window.

I told myself it was nothing. A bird. A cloud reflection. The natural scan of someone checking their surroundings.

But I'd written it in the notebook anyway, and I couldn't cross out every word.

I saved the clip to the external drive and switched to the morning feed. 06:32. Lena's bedroom light clicked on. I watched the shadow move behind the curtain—her shape, the way she paused at the dresser, the habitual sweep of her hand across the surface before she picked up anything. I'd noticed that on day one. She never grabbed. She assessed first.

Six minutes later she emerged in her walking clothes: leggings, fitted jacket, running shoes. Her hair pulled back tight. She walked through the living room, touched the edge of the kitchen counter as she passed, and disappeared into the hallway. I switched cameras to track the building corridor.

The lobby feed showed her approach the mailboxes at 06:41. Not part of her usual route. The past two mornings she'd gone straight through the courtyard door and into the neighborhood loop. This was different.

I leaned forward, adjusting the focus.

She stopped at her mailbox, slot 4C, and pulled the small door open. Her hand went inside, came out empty. She frowned—a small thing, barely a tightening around her mouth—and reached into her jacket pocket. Keys came out. She sorted through them, selected a smaller one, and opened the parcel box beneath the mail slot.

Empty.

She stood there for a moment, her hand resting on the open box door. Then she pulled her bag around, unzipped the main compartment, and searched through it with methodical precision. Wallet, phone, sunglasses case. She checked every pocket, every fold. Nothing.

I zoomed in on her face. The frown was still there, but it wasn't confusion. It was the expression of someone checking a box they were certain they'd already marked.

Then she looked up.

Not at the lobby door. Not at the hallway. Up, toward the second-floor window that overlooked the lobby—the window I'd used as a reference point for camera placement. The one that caught the morning light at this hour and threw it back like a mirror.

Her eyes hit the glass. Held.

My hand froze on the keyboard. I couldn't move. Couldn't breathe. Her face filled the monitor—not perfectly centered, not posed, but there. Present. Looking at something she couldn't possibly see but somehow knew was there.

Three seconds.

Then Mae Torres walked through the lobby door, coffee cup in hand, and the moment broke.

Lena turned, her expression shifting smoothly into something social and neutral. She gestured toward the open mailbox, said something I couldn't hear through the lobby mic's limited range. Mae stepped closer, peering into the box, then back at Lena.

I adjusted the audio gain. Static crackled, but the voices surfaced in fragments.

"...was supposed to be there. Utility notice..." Lena's voice, calm.

"...sure it's not in your bag? Sometimes I put things..." Mae's voice, uncertain.

"...checked three times. It's not here."

Mae nodded, but her face told a different story. That particular look—the one people get when they want to believe someone but the evidence is working against them. She touched Lena's arm, said something I couldn't catch, and shrugged.

Lena's jaw tightened. Just a fraction. Then she smiled—a practiced smile, the kind you use to end a conversation—and closed the mailbox. She said something else, short, and walked toward the courtyard door.

I watched her go. Watched Mae stand there for an extra beat, staring at the closed mailbox, before she shrugged to herself and headed for the elevator.

I saved the clip. Labeled it: Day3_Lobby_MailInteraction.

My phone buzzed at 09:17. Grant's voice came through in a text message: "She tell you about the missing utility notice? Found it in the recycling bin this morning. She must've dropped it there and forgot. Did she seem confused? Accusatory?"

I read the message twice. Then a third time.

The recycling bin. The utility notice was in the recycling bin. Not lost. Not stolen. Not misplaced in the mail system. In the recycling.

I opened the notebook and wrote: "Subject reported missing mail to neighbor Mae Torres. Grant claims to have found the item in shared recycling. Possible confirmation of forgetfulness pattern."

The words sat there, clean and professional. Correct.

I closed the notebook.

I spent the afternoon replaying the lobby footage. Lena's gestures. Her calm. The way she'd searched her bag with the precision of someone who knew exactly what she was looking for. And Mae's expression—that flicker of doubt that crossed her face before she could hide it.

I wrote Grant back: "No mention of confusion. She seemed organized. Methodical."

His reply came fast: "That's how she hides it. You'll see."

I didn't answer.

The evening feed loaded at 19:00. Lena's kitchen light was on. I zoomed in, expecting the usual routine—dinner prep, a book at the counter, maybe a call I couldn't hear. Standard evening pattern.

She was at the counter. The red receipt from yesterday lay flat in front of her, creased along its precise thirds. She picked it up, refolded it with the same exacting care, and placed it to one side.

Then she reached for something new. A utility notice envelope. White. Rectangular. Pristine—no creases, no stains, no bends.

She set it beside the receipt. Aligned the edges with the counter's tile grid. Stepped back.

I zoomed in tighter, adjusting the focus until the envelope's text was nearly legible. Condominium utility notice. Current date. Her unit number.

The one Grant said he'd found in the recycling.

She didn't open it. Didn't read it. Just placed it there, like a piece of evidence on a table.

And then she turned.

Not toward the window. Not toward the courtyard. Toward the kitchen cabinet where I'd hidden the camera—the one she couldn't possibly know the exact coordinates of. Her face filled the monitor, eyes tracking directly into the lens, and she held perfectly still.

No expression. No smile. No fear. Just a woman looking at something she knew was there.

I grabbed the notebook. Wrote: "End of day three. Subject's behavior increasingly difficult to categorize."

My hand was shaking. I didn't know why.

I looked back at the monitor. She was still there. Still watching. The receipt and the envelope sat beside her like a question I couldn't answer.

I killed the feed.

And then I turned it back on.

She was gone. The counter was empty. The receipt and the envelope were nowhere in frame.

I searched the footage. Forward. Backward. Frame by frame. She'd left the kitchen at 19:14, and the items hadn't moved for another six minutes. Then she'd returned, picked them up, and carried them out of frame.

That was it.

I closed the notebook. Locked the external drive in the safe. Sat in the dark for a long time, staring at the monitor's blank screen.

I didn't sleep well that night.
