Day eight. 06:14. Lena. Status: aware. I wrote the words and did not cross them out. The lie had become a confession, and the confession had become a choice I was not ready to make.

I sat in the dark of my apartment, the laptop screen the only light in the room. The private archive was open. Three files. The deleted-interval footage. The honeytoken clip. And now, the frozen frame of Grant's face as he opened the envelope she had sent to herself.

I had watched each file four times since yesterday. Each viewing tightened something in my chest. Not guilt. Not fear. Something sharper. Something that felt like the moment before a door opens and you don't know what's on the other side.

I closed the archive and opened the live feed. Lena's bedroom light was off. The kitchen was dark. The living room curtains were still drawn. Her morning routine wouldn't start for another twenty minutes—if she followed the pattern. But I had stopped assuming she would follow anything.

I ran my thumb along the edge of my field notebook. The spine was worn now, the pages soft from repeated folding. I opened it to the entry from yesterday. "Day seven. Honeytoken confirmed. Lena: aware. Architect. Not victim."

I read the words three times. Then I wrote beneath them:

"Day eight. 06:14. Waiting."

The cursor blinked. I added:

"She knows. She has always known. The question is what she wants me to do with what I have."

I closed the notebook and watched the feed. The minutes crawled. 06:27. 06:31. 06:35.

At 06:39, Lena's bedroom light flicked on. I adjusted the camera angle, zooming in on her window. The shadow moved across the glass. Gray leggings. Black jacket. Running shoes. The same precise routine. But something was different today. She didn't pause at the window. She didn't adjust the blind. She moved through the room like she had already finished her performance.

I leaned closer to the monitor.

At 06:46, she stepped into the hallway. I watched her lock the door, test the handle. She turned toward the courtyard exit, and for a moment I expected her to take her usual route—the path that curved past the mailboxes, the one that took her past the second-floor window where she had looked up on day three.

She didn't.

She turned right at the courtyard's edge and walked toward the basement stairs.

I grabbed my jacket and my field notebook and moved.

The basement stairs were narrow, the lighting dim. I took them two at a time, keeping my footsteps light. The laundry room was at the end of the hall, past the storage units and the boiler room. I had never seen her go there before. I had never seen anyone go there.

I stopped at the corner and peered around the edge. The laundry room door was propped open with a cinder block. The light was on.

I found a position near the storage units, where the shadows were thick and the angle gave me a clear view of the door. I pulled out my phone and opened the common-area camera feed. There was a camera in the laundry room—a small dome mounted near the ceiling, pointed at the washing machines. I adjusted the feed and waited.

Lena walked into the frame.

She was carrying something in her left hand. A piece of paper. Unlined. Folded once horizontally. She stopped in front of the washing machines, turned, and faced the camera.

I froze.

She held the note up to the lens. Her hand was steady. Her expression was calm. Deliberate. Waiting.

I read the words on the paper. Handwritten. Ink bleeding slightly at the edges of the letters. One sentence:

"You keep the originals. Good."

My hand froze over the keyboard. My breath stopped.

She held the note for three seconds. Then she lowered it, folded it again, and slipped it into her pocket. She turned and walked out of the laundry room.

I stared at the frozen frame on my screen. Her face. Her eyes. The note. "You keep the originals. Good."

She knew.

She had always known.

And she had just confirmed it.

I saved the clip. I labeled it: "Day eight. Note in laundry room. 'You keep the originals. Good.'"

I opened the private archive. I placed the clip next to the honeytoken footage. Three pieces of evidence. Three moments that belonged to me, not Grant.

I sat back in my chair and stared at the screen.

"You keep the originals. Good."

She was telling me she knew I had the deleted footage. She was telling me she knew I had the honeytoken clip. She was telling me she trusted me to keep them.

But she hadn't told me why.

---

At 09:17, I heard footsteps on the stairs. Not the heavy tread of a neighbor going to work. Something lighter. Deliberate.

I closed the laptop and stood up. The footsteps stopped outside my door.

Three knocks. Pause. Two more.

I opened the door.

Lena Hart stood in the hallway. She was wearing the same gray leggings and black jacket from the morning walk. Her hair was pulled back. Her face was expressionless.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, "We need to talk."

I stepped aside. She walked past me into the apartment. She didn't look around. She didn't comment on the setup. She walked to the window and stood with her back to me, facing the courtyard.

"You're wondering how I knew," she said.

"Among other things."

She turned. "Grant hired you. I don't know when. I don't know how much he's paying. But I know he hired you to watch me."

I didn't answer.

"I've known for weeks," she continued. "The first time I saw you in the courtyard, I knew. The way you watched. The way you didn't look away when I looked back."

"You looked back."

"I wanted to see if you would break eye contact. You didn't."

I felt the weight of her gaze. "Grant receives selected reports," I said. "Not everything."

"Selected reports. You mean you keep the originals."

"Yes."

She nodded. "I know. I saw the note."

"I know you saw it. You wrote it."

"I wrote it because I needed you to know that I know. And I needed you to know that I trust you to keep them."

"Why?"

She walked toward me. She stopped three feet away. Close enough to see the exhaustion in her eyes. Close enough to see the calculation.

"Because I'm building something," she said. "And I need a witness who sees the whole picture. Not just the parts Grant wants him to see."

"What are you building?"

She shook her head. "Not yet. When I know you've made your choice, I'll tell you."

"What choice?"

She held my gaze. "Three options. Keep serving Grant. Preserve the truth without taking control of my plan. Or walk away."

"What's the difference between the first two?"

"Service is following instructions. Preservation is choosing what to keep and what to release. Grant wants you to follow instructions. I want you to choose."

I felt the weight of the private archive in the other room. The deleted footage. The honeytoken clip. The note.

"How do I choose?"

"You don't. Not yet. Think about it. Review what you have. Decide what kind of witness you want to be."

She turned and walked to the door. She paused with her hand on the handle.

"I'll be in the stairwell for another two minutes," she said. "If you want to ask anything else."

She left.

I stood in the middle of the apartment, staring at the closed door.

---

I found her on the landing between the third and fourth floors. She was sitting on the steps, her arms wrapped around her knees. She looked up when I approached.

"One question," I said.

"One."

"Why me?"

She studied my face for a long moment. "Because you saw the envelope. You saw Grant take it. And you didn't tell him you saw."

"How do you know I didn't tell him?"

"Because Grant would have mentioned it. He can't help himself. He would have said something about the certified mail, about the tracking number, about how he found it before you did." She shook her head. "He didn't. Which means you kept it to yourself."

I sat down on the step below her. The distance between us was less than three feet. The stairs creaked under my weight.

"You clipped the corner," I said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I needed to know if he would open it. If he would tamper with the mail. If he would take something that was addressed to me."

"And he did."

"Yes."

I looked at her. Her eyes were steady. Unblinking.

"What was in the envelope?" I asked.

She smiled. A small, controlled smile. "When you make your choice, I'll tell you."

I opened my mouth to respond, but she stood up.

"Think about it," she said. "Review what you have. Decide what kind of witness you want to be."

She walked up the stairs and disappeared onto the fourth floor.

---

I returned to my apartment and opened the private archive. The deleted-interval footage. The honeytoken clip. The note.

I watched each file again. Grant's body halfway through Lena's door. The kitchen drawer gaping. The clipped envelope in his hand. The note held toward the camera. "You keep the originals. Good."

I opened my field notebook. I wrote:

"Day eight. Lena: aware. Architect. Witness. Choice pending."

I closed the notebook. I opened the frozen frame of Lena's face in the stairwell—her eyes holding mine, her voice still in my ears.

I ran my thumb along the edge of the notebook.

"She asked me to choose," I whispered. "And I already know the answer."

But I didn't say it out loud. Not yet.

I froze the frame on the note—her handwriting, deliberate and precise, held toward the camera like a witness statement. "You keep the originals. Good."

I saved the clip to the private archive, closed the laptop, and sat in the dark.

She had known before I knew. She had chosen me before I chose her.

And now she was waiting for my answer.
