Day seven. 06:14. Lena. Status: aware.

I wrote the words and did not cross them out. The lie had become heavier than the truth, and for the first time, I let the truth sit on the page. She knew. She had always known. And I had just become the witness she needed.

The private archive was still open from last night. The deleted-interval footage sat in its labeled folder, Grant's body halfway through her door, the kitchen drawer gaping behind him. I had watched it four times since yesterday. Each viewing tightened something in my chest.

I closed the archive and pulled up the live feed. Her bedroom light was still off. 06:17. She would leave at 06:41. She had left at 06:41 for the past six days. But today felt different. Today, I was watching for something specific.

I opened my field notebook and wrote beneath the first line:

"06:17. No movement from subject's unit. Anticipating departure at 06:41 ± 2 minutes."

I ran my thumb along the edge of the page. The paper was warm from my hand. I had been writing in this notebook for seven days, and every entry felt like a confession I hadn't finished.

At 06:33, her bedroom light flicked on. I watched the shadow move across the window—her silhouette, the same precise arc of her arm as she pulled on the gray leggings, the black jacket, the same running shoes. She was performing the routine, but I was no longer sure the routine was for Grant.

I was no longer sure it was for me, either.

At 06:41, she stepped into the hallway. But today, she was carrying something. A white envelope. Legal size. Certified—I could see the green return receipt sticker from the angle of the common-area camera.

I leaned forward. She had never carried mail out of her apartment before. She had never carried anything but her bag and her phone.

She locked the door, tested the handle, and turned toward the courtyard exit. But she didn't take her usual route. Instead of crossing the courtyard and exiting through the main gate, she turned right at the courtyard's edge and walked toward the street.

I grabbed my jacket and my field notebook and moved.

I took the stairs two at a time. The lobby was empty. I hit the street at 06:44 and spotted her three blocks ahead, walking east with the certified envelope held flat against her chest, both hands cupped around it like she was carrying something fragile.

I followed at a distance. She didn't look back. She didn't slow at intersections. She walked with purpose, her stride longer than her morning walk pace, her head up, scanning the street ahead.

She was going somewhere specific.

At 06:49, she stopped at a postal kiosk three blocks from the condominium. It was one of those automated machines—self-service, credit card payment, tracking labels printed on demand. She stepped inside the glass enclosure and set the envelope on the metal counter.

I stopped across the street. I could see her through the kiosk's glass door. She was methodical. She pulled a small pair of scissors from her bag—silver, compact, the kind that fold into themselves. She clipped the lower-right corner of the envelope with surgical precision. Not a tear. Not a fold. A deliberate excision.

I wrote in my notebook: "06:49. Subject clips lower-right corner of certified envelope before mailing. Intentional. Signature detail."

She fed the envelope into the machine, tapped the screen, inserted her card. The machine whirred, printed a tracking label, and she pressed it onto the clipped envelope with the flat of her palm. She watched it seal. She watched the machine accept it.

Then she paused.

She pulled a red receipt from the machine—the certified mail receipt—and folded it into precise thirds. She opened her paper planner—the same one I had seen on her kitchen counter—and tucked the folded receipt into the page for today's date. She wrote something next to it. A number. The tracking number.

I was writing all of it. Every movement. Every detail. My hand was moving faster than my brain could process.

And then she looked up.

Not at me. At the glass door of the kiosk. At her own reflection. And behind her reflection, at the glass of the opposite storefront. And behind that, at my reflection.

I was standing across the street, my notebook half-open, my pen frozen above the page. Her eyes caught my reflection in the kiosk glass. She didn't turn. She didn't acknowledge me. She simply held my gaze in the glass for one second.

One second.

I forgot to breathe.

Her lips didn't move. Her expression didn't change. She just looked at the reflection of the man who had been watching her for seven days, and she didn't look away.

Then she packed her scissors, closed her planner, and stepped out of the kiosk. She walked past me without turning her head. She walked back toward the condominium, her hands empty now, her stride steady.

I stood on the sidewalk for three full seconds before I could move again.

I crossed the street and walked to the kiosk. The machine had already printed her receipt. I looked at it. I didn't touch it. I wrote in my notebook:

"06:51. Subject mails envelope to herself. Envelope: certified, return receipt requested. Lower-right corner clipped. Tracking number recorded in personal planner. Red receipt folded into planner. Subject holds gaze at reflection of watcher in kiosk glass for one second. Does not acknowledge. Does not turn. Holds gaze. Leaves."

I stared at the words. They looked like evidence. They felt like indictment.

I walked back to the condominium at 07:03. The lobby was quiet. The mail hadn't arrived yet. I took the stairs to my apartment, closed the door, and sat down at my desk.

I opened the lobby camera feed. I set it to record. I waited.

At 14:22, the mail carrier arrived.

I watched the lobby feed as the carrier sorted the mail into the slots. I watched as a certified envelope—white, legal size, lower-right corner clipped—slid into slot 4C.

Lena's slot.

I watched the timestamp tick forward. 14:23. 14:24. The envelope sat in her mailbox, visible through the small glass window of the slot.

At 14:31, the lobby door opened.

Grant Hart walked in.

He was wearing a suit. He was carrying a briefcase. He walked directly to the bank of mailboxes, pulled a key from his pocket, and inserted it into slot 4C.

The duplicate key.

He opened the mailbox. He pulled out the certified envelope. He held it up to the light, turned it over, examined the clipped corner. His expression didn't change. He slid his thumb under the sealed flap and opened it.

I watched the feed. I watched him pull out a folded piece of paper. I couldn't see what was written on it. The angle was wrong. The camera was too far.

But I saw his face.

He looked at the paper for a long moment. Then he folded it, put it back in the envelope, and slipped the envelope into his briefcase. He closed the mailbox, pocketed the duplicate key, and walked out of the lobby.

I saved the clip. I labeled it: "Day seven. Grant removes clipped envelope from 4C. Duplicate key. Contents unknown."

I opened the private archive. I placed the clip next to the deleted-interval footage. Two pieces of evidence. Two moments Grant didn't want me to see.

I stared at the frozen frame of Grant's face—the clipped envelope in his hand, the duplicate key still in the lock, his expression unreadable as he opened something Lena had sent to herself.

She had sent it to herself.

She had clipped the corner.

She had recorded the tracking number.

She had folded the receipt into her planner.

She had held my gaze in the kiosk glass.

She knew.

She had always known.

And I had just become the witness she needed.

I opened my field notebook to a fresh page. I wrote:

"Day seven. Honeytoken confirmed. Lena: aware. Architect. Not victim."

The words felt like a confession I had been writing for seven days.

I saved the clip to the private archive, closed the dashboard, and opened Grant's daily report template. The cursor blinked in the empty text field.

I typed: "Day seven. 06:14. Subject's morning routine showed no notable deviation from established baseline."

I paused. I stared at the words.

I deleted them.

I typed: "Day seven. 06:14. Subject left unit at 06:41 carrying certified envelope. Returned at 07:19. No further notable activity."

I saved the report. I closed the dashboard. I opened the frozen frame of Lena's reflection in the kiosk glass.

I ran my thumb along the edge of my field notebook.

"She knew I was there," I whispered. "She wanted me to see."

The words hung in the empty apartment.

I closed the notebook and stared at the frozen frame—her eyes in the glass, holding mine for one second, not turning, not acknowledging, just letting me know that she knew.

I had been watching her for seven days.

She had been watching me back.

And I had just become the witness she needed.
