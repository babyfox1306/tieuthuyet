"Listen to the end," the child's voice said from the reel in Nora's hands, and behind her Ruth began to scream.

Not a shocked sound. Not the tidy little gasp Ruth used when confronted with a bill, a stain, a question she did not intend to answer. This was a torn sound dragged up from somewhere too deep to have manners.

Nora stood on the threshold of the salt room with the final reel pressed against her coat, and counted.

One.

The tape box was damp against her fingers.

Two.

Ruth's scream broke into her name.

Three.

Nora walked away.

"Nora!" Ruth's shoes slapped the corridor behind her. "Stop. Please. Give it to me."

That was new. Ruth Vance did not plead. Ruth instructed, corrected, ended conversations by turning her back. Hearing please in her mother's voice should have felt like victory.

It felt like stepping on a rotten stair and hearing it give.

Nora did not look back. If she looked back, Ruth would have a face. A grieving face. A mother's face. Something inconveniently human attached to every lie.

The final reel was heavier than it had any right to be. Metal, tape, cardboard, salt. Evidence, finally with mass. Her thumb found the old scar across her palm and rubbed once, hard enough to sting.

"Where are you taking it?" Ruth demanded.

"The place it belongs."

"Nora, listen to me."

That nearly made her laugh. It came out as a breath, sharp and ugly. "Bit late to become a fan of listening."

The hotel seemed to lean around them as Nora crossed the lobby. The chandelier above trembled though nothing had touched it. Salt crusted the baseboards in pale ridges, thicker now than when she had arrived, as if the house had been quietly exhaling all week and nobody had done it the courtesy of noticing.

Behind her, Ruth stumbled. A hand hit the wall. Nora heard the brittle scrape of nails against old paper.

Good. Let the house catch her, too.

The front doors groaned open under Nora's shoulder. Outside, the path to the cove cut down through rock and coarse grass, silvered by late light. The sea was not dramatic. That was the offensive part. It moved below with patient indifference, folding and unfolding around the black teeth of the tidal pool.

Seven-year-old Nora had loved that pool.

No. That was not evidence. That was a feeling trying to sneak in wearing a witness badge.

She took the path down.

Ruth followed, breath ragged. "You don't understand what that tape is."

"Then define it."

Silence.

Of course. The Vance family specialty. Say nothing until silence hardened into architecture.

Nora kept walking. Halfway down, she saw the portable recorder tucked beneath the leaning post beside the old steps, where she had left it after the last impossible playback. Its cracked handle was crusted white. The machine looked less like a device than an offering the tide had rejected.

She crouched and opened the lid.

The smell inside was metal, dust, and brine. The take-up spool sat crooked but intact. Beside it, in the recorder case, was the torn strip of leader she had used before, stiff with salt. Agnes had known someone would bring the tape here. Agnes had known the truth would need the right mouth.

Nora set the final reel on the spindle.

Ruth reached the bottom of the path and stopped several feet away from the pool's edge. Her face had gone colorless, but not blank. Blank would have been kinder. This was a face with every locked door opened at once.

"Don't," Ruth whispered.

Nora threaded the tape.

Her hands were steadier than they felt. Across the pool, water shifted inside the rock basin with a low suck and release, suck and release, like the sea practicing breath. The spool turned once under her finger with a faint scrape like a fingernail under shell grit.

The tape box lay open beside her knee.

A white crescent of dried salt marked one corner. Nora stared at it, irritation flashing bright because her brain picked now to care about shape. Not a stain. Not random. The crescent matched the exact curve of a child's small bite mark.

Her stomach tightened.

A child had bitten the box.

To mark it? To hold it? To keep someone from taking it away?

"Nora," Ruth said. "If you play that, you can't make it untrue again."

Nora looked up at her mother. "That implies you made it untrue once."

Ruth flinched.

Excellent. A confession, if confessions wore expensive coats and collapsed slowly in public.

Nora pressed play.

At first there was only hiss.

Then a hum, low and intimate, the recorder's old motor pulling years through its teeth.

A child's laugh burst out, bright enough to make Nora's body jerk.

"Stop copying me," one girl said.

"I'm not copying. This is my voice, too."

Two voices. Same age. Same quick rise on too, same little scrape at the edge of the r. Not identical, not quite, but close enough that Nora's skin recognized the problem before her mind did.

The tape crackled.

"You sound like me because you came after," the first child announced.

"By seven minutes," the second said, offended. "Agnes says seven minutes doesn't count."

"Ruth says it counts."

"Ruth says everything counts when she wants to win."

Nora's throat closed.

Ruth made a sound behind her, not denial. Pain.

On the tape, one of the girls sang two lines of a nonsense tune, off-key and fearless. The other joined in half a beat late, turning the song into a wobbling braid of sound.

Nora knew the tune.

She had hummed it in hotel rooms without knowing why. Under her breath while inspecting broken pipes. In elevators. Once at her grandmother's funeral, before Ruth had touched her elbow so hard it hurt.

"Say it properly," the first girl demanded.

"No."

"Say my name."

A pause. Static flickered like breath over the microphone.

"Della," the second child said, making the name into a dare.

"All of it."

"Della Vance," the second child said. "Now you say mine."

"Nora Vance," Della said on the tape. "Nora Vance, Nora Vance, Nora Vance."

The tidal pool vanished.

Not visually. Nora did not see ghosts, because apparently the family horror had standards. But the present loosened its grip, and memory came up through sound.

Bare feet slapping wet rock.

A gull screaming once overhead.

Della's laugh ahead of her, too close to the edge.

Nora dug her nails into her palm. The scar burned under her thumb.

On the tape, seven-year-old Nora shrieked, "Don't go past the black line. Agnes said."

"Della, don't," the child Nora said, but she was laughing. She was always laughing when Della made rules bend. That was the first cruelty of the memory. It had joy in it.

A splash cracked through the speaker.

Not large. Not cinematic. A small body entering water awkwardly. A stumble, a skid, a slap of palms on stone, then the pool taking what gravity gave it.

The tape warped.

"Della?" child Nora said.

Water answered. Slosh against rock. A cough. Or the recorder's distortion pretending to be one.

"Della, get up."

Ruth dropped to her knees behind Nora.

The sound hit the ground softly, fabric and bone and grief.

Nora could not turn. If she turned, she might stop the tape. If she stopped the tape, she would be exactly what they had trained her to be.

On the recording, Della gasped.

"Nora."

One word. Her name. High, wet, startled.

Then water.

Seven-year-old Nora did not scream.

Nora remembered that most of all. Not because she had been calm. Because terror had turned her into furniture. She had stood on the flat rock above the pool, toes curled against the slick surface, mouth open, soundless.

Della's head had gone under.

Come up, Nora thought now, uselessly, viciously. Come up, come up, come up.

The child on the tape breathed fast, tiny panicked pulls. The recorder had been close. Too close. It captured everything Nora had done and everything she had failed to do.

"Nora?" Della's voice came again, thinner.

Then the scrape of a knee. A sob swallowed before it became sound.

Seven-year-old Nora whispered, "Agnes."

Not loud enough.

The pool gurgled.

"Nora," Ruth choked behind her. "She was gone so quickly."

The tape kept running.

That was Agnes. Nora understood it with sudden, cold clarity. Agnes had left the recorder on. Agnes had preserved not just proof but duration. The terrible length of a child's silence. The exact measurement of failure, if anyone was cruel enough to measure it.

Nora was cruel enough. Analytical to the end. Very professional. Very composed. Counting seconds beside the place where her sister died.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

Five.

Too many.

On the tape, a distant adult voice shouted. Agnes, from the hotel steps.

"Della? Nora?"

The child's breathing broke then. Nora began to scream on the recording. Not words at first. Just a high ripping sound that made Ruth cover her mouth with both hands.

Nora's eyes stayed dry. Her body had apparently decided tears were inefficient.

The tape snarled.

The reel jerked, the right spool stuttering as the damaged final section caught. Della's last call dissolved into a muddy drag of sound. Nora lunged before the tape could tear.

"No."

She hit stop, opened the lid, and found the kink with her fingertip. The strip had folded where salt had stiffened the edge. One bad crease. One last sabotage by time or by Ruth or by the house itself, because why should this family let even a dead child finish a sentence?

Nora pulled the tape loose by inches.

Her hands shook now. Fine. Evidence could be handled with shaking hands. Crime scenes had been processed by people with worse tremors and better coffee.

She took the torn leader from the recorder case and used it to brace the damaged section, smoothing the crease across her thumbnail. Not perfect. Stabilized. Enough to pass through the heads without snapping.

Ruth watched her from the stones, face wet. "You always did that."

Nora did not look up. "Repaired magnetic tape at seven?"

"Fixed things with whatever was near. Della broke them. You fixed them. She hated that. Loved it, too."

The words landed like stones placed carefully on a grave.

Nora threaded the tape again.

"Say it," she said.

Ruth's breath caught.

"Say what you did."

Ruth bowed her head. For one wild second Nora thought her mother would refuse, even now. That she would choose the old family script and crawl inside it until it killed her.

But Ruth looked at the tidal pool, and something in her broke without noise.

"Della was your twin sister," she said.

The recorder hummed between them.

Ruth swallowed hard. "She was born seven minutes before you. She drowned here the summer you were seven. You saw it happen. You froze. You were a child, Nora. You were a child."

That last part came too fast, too desperate, trying to outrun blame.

Nora let it pass. Not accepted. Not rejected. Filed.

"And after?" Nora asked.

Ruth pressed a fist to her chest. "After, you stopped speaking. For weeks. When you did, you asked for her until you made yourself sick. Every room had two beds. Every drawer had two sets of clothes. Every photograph had her face beside yours. I couldn't..." Her voice cracked, and there she was, not villain, not victim, but something worse. A person who had chosen harm because grief offered it a clean shirt. "I couldn't live in a house where she kept being almost there."

"So you erased her."

"We all agreed."

"Don't launder it."

Ruth shut her eyes. "I erased her. I burned photographs. I cut her out of albums. I told people not to say her name. Agnes fought me, then stopped fighting where I could see. I thought she had finally understood."

Nora glanced at the reel. "She understood perfectly."

"She kept the tapes."

"She kept Della."

Ruth's mouth trembled.

The salt room. The cold door. The frost, thick as refusal. Nora saw it now with brutal simplicity. They had removed a child from every visible surface and expected the space she occupied to politely close. But a body leaves more than objects. A sister leaves air shaped around her. A bed beside another bed. A second voice in a song. A name the house had been forced to swallow.

The room had stayed cold because it held what they denied. Not a ghost in a sheet. Not a trick. Space with grief packed into it so tightly it changed the temperature.

Nora pressed play.

The repaired section passed with a rough hiss. The spool scraped again, fingernail under shell grit, and then the distortion thinned.

Della's recorded voice emerged.

"Nora."

Clear.

No water over it now. No static chewing the edges. Just her name, spoken by a little girl who had trusted her sister to hear.

Nora closed her eyes.

There she was. Not an imaginary friend. Not a cousin. Not a guest's child caught by accident on Agnes's sentimental tapes. Della Vance, seven minutes older, rule-breaker, singer of stupid songs, biter of tape boxes, drowned in the tidal pool while Nora stood frozen on the rock and silence stretched too long.

Love arrived like pain because apparently the body was not picky about delivery.

Nora bent over the recorder, her forehead nearly touching the lid. "Della Vance," she said.

The name did not echo.

It settled.

The pool gave one soft slap against the rocks. Behind them, from high inside the hotel, something shifted. Not a crash. A release. The kind old wood made when a locked room warmed enough to remember it had hinges.

Ruth began to sob.

Nora did not comfort her. Not yet. Maybe not ever in the way Ruth wanted. There were consequences, and Nora had no intention of wrapping them in lace because her mother had finally located honesty at the bottom of a cliff.

But Nora reached back without looking and set one hand on the stone between them.

An offering. A boundary. Ruth did not touch it.

Good. She was learning.

They sat until the tape ran out.

The end was not peaceful. It clicked and clicked on the empty spool, stupid and mechanical, long after the voices stopped. Nora let it go for ten clicks before shutting it off.

Ten. Not three.

When she stood, her knees objected. Her face felt carved. Ruth remained on the ground, smaller than Nora had ever seen her, staring at the pool as if apology could travel underwater twenty-two years late.

Nora carried the recorder back up the path.

The hotel waited with its front doors open.

Inside, the air had changed. Not warm, exactly. The Saltwood was not about to become charming because one woman said one name. But the vicious cold had loosened. The lobby felt damp, stale, almost ordinary until Nora saw the salt on the walls tremble in a fine white shiver.

Ruth followed at a distance.

Nora climbed to the west corridor.

The door to the salt room stood ajar.

No frost sealed it now. Water ran down the panels in clear threads. Inside, the room wavered at the edges, warmer than before but unstable, as if the hotel had stopped holding its breath and had no idea what breathing required.

The second bed was there.

Nora had not seen it clearly before. Or had refused to. Small iron frame. Bare mattress. A hollow in the dust beside it where another child's shoes might have waited.

On the floor, salt had melted into shallow puddles. In one of them, a faint vibration moved outward in rings, though nothing had dropped.

Ruth stopped behind Nora. "What happens now?"

Nora looked at the room that had kept her sister when no one else would.

"Now," she said, "nobody says she wasn't real."

Ruth nodded once, ruined by the simplicity of it.

Nora should have been done. Mystery solved. Childhood horror named. Dead sister restored to the family record whether Ruth liked the paperwork or not. A clean ending, if stories were designed by committees and cowards.

Then the ceiling above them creaked.

Three slow knocks.

Nora looked up.

Not from the salt room. Higher.

The attic.

Ruth went still. "Nora."

There it was again. Not the voice from the tape. Not Della speaking live. Wood, weight, the hotel rearranging its secrets overhead.

Nora's hand tightened around the recorder handle. "Agnes preserved more than one truth."

"Don't go up there tonight."

Nora turned. "You are spectacularly bad at predicting what will stop me."

The attic stairs were at the end of the service hall, behind a narrow door Agnes had once painted the same color as the wall. Nora found the latch by memory she should not have had. The stairs rose steeply, coated in dust except for a single dragged line down the center.

At the top, the attic smelled of dry salt and old cloth.

Sheets covered furniture in pale, rigid shapes. The air was warmer than the salt room but not safe. It pressed against Nora's ears with the dense quiet of a held recording.

She moved slowly, counting under her breath because fear hated arithmetic and she enjoyed being annoying.

One.

A stack of hatboxes.

Two.

A cracked mirror turned to the wall.

Three.

A low trunk beneath a sheet stiff with salt.

Nora pulled the sheet away.

The trunk was black, iron-banded, and locked. Agnes's initials had been scratched into the lid with something sharp, not decorative, not neat. A key hung from a loop of red thread tied around the handle, as if Agnes had wanted the lock to look more final than it was.

Nora opened it.

Inside, more reels waited in tight rows, each in a labeled box.

Her breath stopped.

The top label was not from the summer Della died. It was not from Nora's childhood at all.

In the attic, beneath a sheet stiff with salt, Nora found Agnes's locked trunk; inside, more reels waited, and the top label was dated one week after Agnes died.
