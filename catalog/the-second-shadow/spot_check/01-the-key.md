"I'm not afraid of you," Mara whispered as the key turned with a click like a door sealing shut. The sound was too loud in the afternoon stillness, a confession of intrusion. The lock was stiff, unused to the ritual of opening.

The door swung inward, exhaling a breath of dust and dried lavender and something else—something mineral and sour that clung to the back of her throat. The foyer was dark, the wallpaper a faded pattern of climbing vines that looked, in the dim light, like cracks in porcelain.

"Home sweet home," she muttered, stepping over the threshold.

The house settled around her, a groan running through the old bones of the floorboards. It was testing her, she decided. Waiting to see if she would flinch. She ran a hand over the banister. Dust came away on her fingers. The wood was scarred, gouged by decades of use.

This is how horror movies start, she thought. I should have sold this place sight unseen.

But she was broke. And desperate. And the letter from Jules Delacroix had arrived at the exact moment her bank account hit zero, like Aunt Ilse was reaching out from the grave to remind her that blood mattered whether you wanted it to or not.

"You held her secrets," Mara whispered, pressing her palm flat against the wall. "You can hold mine."

She left her single bag by the door and walked deeper into the belly of the house. The parlor was a museum of a life she barely remembered. Ornate furniture draped in white sheets, ghost furniture. A piano with a silent mouth. The air was thick, undisturbed.

The stairs groaned beneath her weight as she climbed to the second floor. The attic door was at the end of the hall, a small inconspicuous rectangle in the ceiling with a cord hanging from it. She pulled the cord. The ladder came down with a screech that raked against her sanity.

The attic was suffocating. Airless. Thick with the heat of generations. Boxes stacked in the corners, labeled in a flowing hand—Christmas, Summer 89, M's Things. She found the bear in a box labeled Playroom.

He was smaller than she remembered. Not that she remembered much. The summer she turned eight was a blank page in the book of her life, a chapter someone had torn out.

Wick. The one-eyed bear.

His fur was threadbare, moth-eaten in patches. One button eye was missing, replaced by a dark piece of glass cut like a jewel, catching the thin light filtering through the dirty attic window. It looked real. Watching. A charm hung from his remaining ear, a small silver key no longer than her thumbnail.

She touched the glass eye.

Vertigo.

The attic tilted. The smell of rain. The sound of a man's voice, low and pleading. A woman's sharp reply. The creak of a floorboard. A fall. A terrible, wet thump that vibrated through her bones.

She snatched her hand back, gasping. Her heart hammered against her ribs. The bear stared up at her, innocent, inanimate.

"What the hell was that?" she whispered to the silence.

The house offered no answer. Just the gentle groan of old wood settling. She grabbed Wick by the paw and fled the attic, the memory of the fall clinging to her like a spiderweb.

That's where the lawyer found her.

He was standing in the parlor when she came down, a silhouette against the dirty window. Tall, broad-shouldered, dressed in charcoal gray that probably cost more than her car. He turned when he heard her footsteps, and she was struck by the calm of him. The deliberate stillness.

"The door was open," he said. His voice was low, even. "I'm Jude Croft. I was your aunt's... representative."

"Mara Vale." She didn't offer her hand. She was still clutching the bear, a talisman against the cold that had settled in her bones.

"I have the keys to the outbuildings. The property." He paused, his gaze dropping to her hand. "You found Wick."

It wasn't a question.

"You know him?"

"I know your aunt kept him in the attic. She told me if you ever came, to make sure you found him."

Unease crawled up her spine. "Why?"

He stepped closer. The air in the room seemed to thicken. "I think that's a question you have to answer for yourself, Mara."

He held out a key. The brass was warm. His fingers brushed hers as he pressed it into her palm. The touch was brief, electric. A flicker of something that had nothing to do with ghosts or bears. Her eyes met his. The silence in the parlor stretched, taut as a wire.

"Thank you," she said, her voice steadier than she felt.

"You shouldn't go into the attic alone," he said. "The floorboards in the back section are rotten. You could fall through."

Fall. The word echoed in her skull.

"I'll keep that in mind."

He didn't move. The space between them was charged, humming with a tension she didn't understand. He was close enough that she could smell him—soap, clean sweat, the papery scent of old documents.

"You're shaking," he said.

"I'm fine."

"No. You're not."

His hand rose. He didn't touch her. He simply held it in the air between them, a question. She didn't pull away. His fingers brushed the curve of her jaw, featherlight, and the touch was a match to dry tinder.

She didn't know who moved first.

They collided against the parlor wall. The bear—Wick—fell to the floor, forgotten. His mouth was on hers, and she wasn't thinking about the attic or the fall or the sound of a voice she couldn't place. "Tell me to stop," he said, his voice rough.

"Don't you dare."

Mara whirled around.

The hallway was empty.

The air was frigid. The radiator knocked once, a sound like a heartbeat.

She looked back at the mirror. Her reflection stared back, alone.

But the girl's handprint was pressed against the glass, small and perfect, slowly fading away.
