The girl in blue lifted her hand, and Mara felt the weight of every forgotten summer settle into her bones.

The memory of the pond came back not as a story she'd been told but as a physical thing—the cold water closing over her head, the panic in her chest when she'd surfaced alone, the way she'd stood on the bank and watched bubbles rise and then stop. She'd been eight. She hadn't meant to push him. They'd been fighting over the bear, and his foot had slipped on the muddy bank, and she'd grabbed his arm, but her grip wasn't strong enough.

The girl in blue stood three feet away, her face soft in the dim light filtering through the attic's single window. She wore the same dress Mara had worn that summer—a pale blue sundress with a torn hem. Her hair was plaited in two braids, just like Mara's mother used to do.

"You remember now," the girl said. Her voice was high and clear, a child's voice, but there was nothing childlike in her eyes.

Mara nodded. Her throat felt raw, as if she'd been screaming for hours. "I remember."

"You've been running for twenty-one years."

"I know."

The girl stepped closer. Her bare feet made no sound on the dusty floorboards. "I've been waiting in the dark. Holding it for you. Every night, every summer, every birthday when you tried to forget—I was here, keeping it safe."

Mara's phone buzzed in her pocket. She pulled it out blindly, not taking her eyes off the girl. The screen showed her mother's name.

She answered without thinking. "Mom?"

Lydia Vale's voice crackled through the speaker, thin and brittle. "Mara. I need to tell you something."

Mara's hand tightened on the phone. "I already know."

"No." Her mother's breath came in short, ragged gasps. "You don't. I know what you found in Ilse's house. I know about the bear. I've been waiting for this call for twenty years, dreading it."

"Then why didn't you tell me?"

"Because I was trying to protect you." Lydia's voice broke. "I knew what happened that summer. I found you at the pond, soaking wet, clutching that ratty bear. You wouldn't speak. You just stared at the water. I knew something terrible had happened, but you wouldn't tell me, and when you finally did—"

Mara's vision blurred. "When I finally did what?"

"Nothing. You never told me what happened at the pond. You said you forgot. But I knew you didn't forget. I could see it in your eyes. So I took you to a therapist, and she said the best thing was to let the memory surface naturally, or you'd break."

"And you believed her?"

"I believed what I needed to believe." Lydia's voice hardened. "I was a single mother, Mara. I was barely holding it together. And Ilse—your aunt—she knew. She knew because she'd been through the same thing. She was the one who told me the bear was dangerous, that you'd stored something in it. So I kept you away from her house. I kept you away from everything that could trigger it."

The girl in blue touched Mara's hand. Her fingers were cold, impossibly cold, and Mara felt the memory of the pond water rising around her ankles.

"Why didn't Ilse tell me herself?" Mara asked.

"Because she was too ashamed. She had her own shadow, Mara. Her own split. She couldn't face yours. She thought if she kept the bear hidden, you'd heal. She was wrong."

Mara closed her eyes. The weight of all those years pressed against her chest, heavy and suffocating. "What about the man? Lachlan. Did you know about him too?"

Silence. Then, so quietly Mara almost missed it: "He was your father."

"I know."

"He was a good man, Mara. He wasn't what people said. He loved me, and he loved you. But that night, when he came to the attic to confront Ilse about the bear, he fell. And I didn't stop it."

"You pushed him."

"No." Lydia's voice hardened. "I didn't push him. He slipped. The floor was rotted. But I didn't catch him either. I stood there and watched him fall because I was too afraid. And I made you swear to keep it secret. I made you carry that guilt."

The girl in blue was crying now, tears streaming down her pale cheeks. But she was smiling too.

"I made you split," Lydia continued. "I made you create her. And I've spent every day since hating myself for it."

Mara let out a breath she didn't know she'd been holding. "I forgive you."

"Mara—"

"I forgive you, Mom. But I need to fix this now."

She ended the call and slipped the phone back into her pocket. The girl in blue was watching her, waiting.

"So," the girl said. "Now you know everything."

Mara nodded. "Everything."

"And now you have a choice. You can take me back, become whole again, and live with the truth. Or you can run, leave me here in the dark, and pretend none of this happened."

Mara looked at the bear still clutched in her hand. Wick, with his single button eye and his matted fur, holding twenty years of repressed memory in his stuffing.

"What happens to you if I take you back?"

The girl smiled. "I become you. I merge into your shadow, and we carry the memory together. I won't be separate anymore. I'll just be part of you."

"And if I leave you here?"

"Then I stay. Forever. In this house, in the dark, waiting for someone else to find me. But you'll never be whole."

Mara's fingers found the scar on her left hand, the one she'd had since childhood, the one she'd always rubbed when she was anxious. She'd gotten it the day of the drowning, pulling herself out of the pond, cutting her hand on a broken branch.

She reached out and took the girl's hand. Cold fingers laced through hers.

"I'm done running," Mara said.

The girl's smile widened, and then she dissolved, like smoke in wind, like ink in water. Her form unraveled into threads of blue light, and those threads wove themselves into Mara's shadow, stretching and reshaping until the shadow was whole again, no longer missing a piece.

Mara felt it—the weight of the memory settling into her spine, the cold of the pond water in her lungs, the guilt and the grief and the terrible, crushing relief of finally knowing. She gasped. Her knees buckled.

Jude caught her. His arms wrapped around her, strong and steady, pulling her against his chest. He smelled like coffee and old wood, and his heart was pounding against her ear.

"I've got you," he whispered. "I've got you."

She stayed in his arms, shaking, as the last of the blue light faded from the room. She felt different. Complete. Like a puzzle piece she hadn't known was missing had finally clicked into place.

"I remember everything," she said, her voice muffled against his shirt. "Every detail. Every second."

Jude's hand came up to cup the back of her head. "And you chose to take it back. You chose to be whole."

"I didn't want to run anymore."

He pulled back just enough to look at her. His eyes were dark and serious, and there was something raw in them that made her breath catch.

"I love you," he said.

The words hung in the air, simple and terrifying.

Mara stared at him. "You barely know me."

"I know you chose to face your worst fear instead of running from it. I know you carried guilt you didn't deserve for twenty years and still found the strength to forgive yourself. I know you're standing here, whole, after everything."

He took her face in his hands, his thumbs brushing her cheekbones. "I love you, Mara."

She wanted to push him away. She wanted to laugh and say he was being ridiculous. But instead, she leaned into his touch and let herself believe.

"I don't know if I can say it back yet," she whispered.

"Then don't. Just let me hold you."

He pulled her into a tight embrace, his arms wrapped around her like a shield. She pressed her face into his shoulder and breathed.

Later, when the sun had set and the house had gone dark, Mara found Ilse's diary on the nightstand. She opened it to the final entry.

The page was written in blue ink that smudged when she touched it, as if Ilse had been crying as she wrote.

I see her now. My second shadow. She's been with me since I was twelve, since that summer I can't remember but can't forget. She stands in the corner of my bedroom at night, watching me. I've never told anyone. I've never let her in. But she's still there, patient, waiting for me to be brave enough to take her back.

I'm not brave.

I hope Mara will be.

Mara closed the diary. Her hand was shaking.

She looked at Jude, who was sitting on the edge of the bed, watching her.

"Aunt Ilse had one too," she said. "A second shadow."

Jude's face went pale.

The radiator in the corner knocked three times.

Mara and Jude both froze. The sound echoed through the silent house, rhythmic, deliberate—like a heartbeat.

Jude stood up slowly. "Was that—"

"It's just the pipes."

But she wasn't sure she believed it.

They stood together in the quiet, listening. The house settled around them, creaking and groaning like an old ship. The shadows in the corners deepened and shifted.

And then, just for a moment, Mara saw something flicker past the window.

Not her reflection.

Not Jude's.

A shadow, dark and fast, moving against the moonlight.

The radiator knocked three more times.

Mara's hand found Jude's. He squeezed back, hard.

She didn't know what she'd seen. She didn't know if Ilse's shadow was still in the house, waiting for someone to help her. She didn't know if the memory was truly over.

But she knew one thing for certain.

They weren't alone.
