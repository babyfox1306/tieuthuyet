Every carved name split open, and the moss beneath them began pulling toward the exposed ribs.

The graveyard had become a dissection table. The ground parted along the incised letters, the black mat of the moss contracting and curling away from the fresh stone beneath. The names stood out sharp and clean, as if the dead were rising to read their own headstones.

Marget pressed her numb palm against the central stone. The pulse was there, stumbling, skipping, racing into something desperate. The entire network was in spasm.

Arthur had not moved from where he stood, ten feet away, staring at the spreading fissures. His bag was on the ground. The shattered receiver lay in pieces at his feet. He had taken the maul. He held it like a walking stick, one hand gripping the head, the other on the shaft.

She did not wait for him to recover.

She dropped to her knees and grabbed a piece of broken slate. Her right fingers curled around it. She could not feel the stone, only the pressure of her grip transmitted through the tendons in her wrist. The left hand was going. The ring finger and the little finger were already stone. The middle finger was fading.

She scuttled to the nearest exposed filament. It lay across a split grave, pulsing with the same frantic rhythm she had felt in the central stone. It was pale, almost translucent, the color of drowned skin.

She brought the slate down.

The edge severed the filament cleanly.

The recoil was immediate. The network snapped taut from the center to the perimeter. Every open grave seized at once. The ground shuddered under her knees. The cold in the air stuttered, flickered, and for a half-breath the fog thinned and the graveyard warmed.

She looked at the wound in the filament. The two ends writhed, searching for each other, but the gap held. The tissue was dying.

The outer graves went silent.

The feeding had stopped.

She had proved it. The network was a single organism. Damage at one point was damage to all. The central stone was not just the heart—it was the brain, the lungs, the spine. Hurt the center, hurt the whole.

Arthur's feet crunched on the gravel.

She looked up.

He was moving toward her, the maul dragging in his grip. His face was gray. The youth she had seen drain from him was gone, replaced by the deep lines of a man who had been hollowed out.

"You've ruined it," he said. Not shouting. His voice flat, factual.

She stood. Her legs were unsteady. The numbness had climbed past her left wrist. She could feel the bones in her forearm moving, but the skin was dead.

"It was eating the dead," she said.

"It was keeping order."

"It was feeding you."

He did not deny it. He just kept walking.

She turned. She needed to see the center.

The central stone was tilting.

It had shifted in the convulsion, leaning eastward by the width of a finger. The base of the stone was dark with moisture. The bone fragment she had extracted was inside, visible through the fracture, but the fracture had widened. The stone was broken and she had not shattered it.

No. The network had done this. The spasm had cracked it from within.

The raven stood on the leaning face.

It was not still.

Its head was low, its wings half-spread, its body tensed. It was trying to anchor itself against the tilt. Its claws were sunk into the carved surface of the stone, the keratin tips scraping slow grooves in the granite as the stone shifted beneath them.

The raven tried to fly.

It sprung upward. Its wings beat once, wide and black against the fog. Its body lifted by a handspan, claws clearing the stone for a fraction of a heartbeat. Then the claws caught. They hooked back into the granite, pulling the raven down. It smashed into the stone chest-first, wings flailing, beak clicking.

The raven could not leave.

It was bound to the stone the way the moss was bound to the graves. The anchor held it. The cold held it.

She understood: the raven was the seal. It was not a guardian. It was a lock. Break the lock, break the network.

Arthur was three steps away.

She scrambled past him.

The tools were where he had dropped them. The iron wedge lay on the moss, its tip dark with moisture. The maul was in Arthur's hand. She needed it. She grabbed the wedge first.

Arthur swung the maul.

Not at her. At the ground. He drove the head into the earth beside the wedge, splitting the soil, blocking her reach. The vibration hummed up the handle.

"You will not touch that stone," he said.

"Oswin Kerrow is in there."

"Oswin Kerrow is dead. What is in there is the machine he became."

She looked at the fracture. The bone was visible. The date was visible. 18 October 1912. He had been in the soil for over a century, and the moss had eaten him from the inside out, and he had let it happen so the other graves could rest.

Arthur pulled the wedge from the ground and threw it into the mist. It landed somewhere in the open graves, clattering against stone.

She did not have the wedge.

She had the broken slate.

It was not enough.

Arthur lifted the maul and set the head against his shoulder. He was guarding the central stone. He was waiting for her to leave.

She pressed her left hand against the central stone.

The cold soaked into her skin. She could feel it at the base of her palm, at the joint of her wrist, but the fingers were gone. She could not feel the grain of the granite. She could only feel the temperature, the deep chill of a man who had been buried alive for a hundred years.

The cold did not speak.

It never spoke.

She could not ask Oswin if he wanted release. She could not feel his will in the temperature, only his presence. A wound held open. A hunger that was not his.

But she knew what he had done. He had made himself the anchor so the network would consume him instead of the others. He had believed the dead deserved peace. He had sacrificed his own.

She could not ask him if he was tired.

She had to trust what he had been.

Arthur shifted his weight. The maul came off his shoulder.

"Marget." His voice was quiet. "Step away."

She stepped toward him.

Her right arm hung dead at her side. Her left was dying. She could feel the numbness climbing through the meat of her forearm, swallowing the muscle, the nerves. She had maybe an hour before her hands were completely gone. Maybe less.

"Give me the maul," she said.

"No."

"He made himself the anchor so the network would feed on him. You turned his body into a pipe. You're prolonging a horror."

Arthur's grip on the maul tightened. "You don't know what you're talking about."

"I know the network stopped when I cut the filament. I know the raven cannot leave the stone. I know that if I shatter the anchor, the network dies. The feeding stops. Oswin Kerrow is released."

Arthur's face did not change. But his hand moved to the handle, adjusting his grip.

"You do not understand what you are breaking," he said.

"Then tell me."

He said nothing.

She had known he would not. Arthur Penhaligon never explained himself. He gave commands. He took what he wanted. He left the rest in silence.

She let her right arm go limp.

It dropped across the shaft of the maul, the dead weight of it falling between Arthur's hands and the head of the hammer. Thirty pounds of meat and bone swung on the pivot of her shoulder. The weight snagged the haft against her hip.

Arthur pulled.

The arm did not move. It was dead. It had no resistance, no muscle tone. It was a sack of meat hooked over the handle.

She twisted.

The maul wrenched in his grip. He had not expected the angle. The head swung free of his control. She caught it with her left hand.

Her left fingers closed around the haft.

She could not feel them.

She felt the pressure in her palm, the strain in her wrist, but the fingers were gone. The last nerve in the left hand had died while she was reaching. The numbness was through her elbow. It was climbing into her shoulder.

She pulled.

Arthur held.

She pulled again, and the weight of her body, the dead drag of her arms, the leverage of her stance—it was not strength. It was gravity. It was the refusal to let go.

The maul came free.

Arthur stumbled back, his hands empty, his face a mask of disbelief.

She held the maul.

The handle was slick with the moisture from her palms. She could not feel it. She could only feel the balance, the heft, the weight of the iron head swinging against the air.

Arthur took a step forward.

She raised the maul.

He stopped.

"Don't," she said.

"Marget—"

"Don't."

She turned.

The central stone was three steps away. The raven was watching her, its head cocked, its claws still sunk in the broken granite. The fog was thickening around the base. The cold was deepening.

She walked.

Arthur did not follow.

She reached the central stone. The fracture was open. The bone fragment was inside. The pulse was gone. The network had stopped feeding. It was waiting.

She raised the maul above her head.

The raven spread its wings.

Its claws did not lift. They stayed buried in the stone. The wings beat once, twice, stirring the air around her, ruffling her hair. The raven strained. Its feet did not leave the rock. It spread its wings wider, and the cold rose around her like a held breath.

Marget Halloway raised Arthur Penhaligon's maul above the central stone, and the raven spread its wings without lifting its trapped feet.
