The white filaments did not enter the nearest coffin; they bent around it and ran toward the central stone. Marget knelt in the mud, tracing the path with her eyes. The tendrils were thicker here, braided into cords that moved with purpose.

She followed them on hands and knees, her boots sinking into the saturated earth. The lower plot still drained from Arthur's flooding, and the water had exposed deeper layers of the network. Filaments surfaced from the disturbed soil, gleaming like tendons in the grey light.

Marget paused at the second row of graves. The white cords wrapped around the headstone of a Sarah Pender, dated 1892, but they did not penetrate the coffin. Instead they hugged the stone casing and continued forward. She pressed her palm against the grave—cold, but the familiar creeping burn of the outer network was absent. The moss had left this corpse alone.

She crawled further, her knees numb through the wool of her trousers. The filaments thickened as she neared the central stone, weaving into a mat that pulsed faintly. They avoided the newer plots entirely, skipping over undisturbed earth to converge at a single point six feet east of the central stone's base.

Marget dug her fingers into the soil there. The ground was looser, as if it had been turned recently. Her left hand struggled to grip—the numbness had spread past her wrist, and she could only feel pressure through her thumb and forefinger. She used her right hand instead, scooping away the mud until her nails scraped something hard.

The bone fragment lay in a shallow depression, wrapped in oiled cloth. She recognized the wrapping. She had placed it here four days ago, after reading Oswin's notebook, after pressing the torn page between the roots of the alder.

She unwrapped the cloth with her right hand. The fragment was a section of femur, about six inches long, taken from the oldest part of the graveyard. The bone had been cleaned and carved with a date and initials: O.K. 1901. The cut of the chisel was unmistakable.

Marget turned the fragment toward the weak light. The stroke had a stagger—the tool had skipped twice before biting deep. That was Oswin's signature. He had taught her that exact technique, standing over her workbench while she tried to match the rhythm. "Again, Marget. The cut is your name on the stone. Do it until your wrist burns."

Her wrist no longer burned. She held the bone in her left hand and felt nothing.

Cold bloomed from the fragment, so sharp it stole her breath. The air around her contracted, and the mist seemed to solidify into droplets that clung to her skin. But at the same time, the graves behind her grew warmer—the familiar bite of the outer network softened, receding inward like water draining from a basin.

Marget held the bone against her chest and waited. The cold concentrated, pressing into her sternum, and she understood: the entire network was a web that drew its power from a single source. The filaments bypassed the other coffins because they were not the destination. They were conduits, and the bone was the terminus.

She set the fragment on the oiled cloth and pulled a small notebook from her coat pocket. She had started it after Arthur confiscated her ledger, a replacement she kept hidden inside her lining. She wrote: "C010 confirmed—Oswin Kerrow's bone, dated 1901, damage-chisel pattern matches apprenticeship records. Network converges on fragment location approx 1.8m east of main stone. Outer graves 14°F warmer at point of contact."

She tested her left hand again, pressing her ring finger against the notebook's edge. Faint pressure—barely a whisper. Her little finger registered nothing at all.

The sodden earth behind her crunched.

Marget turned. Arthur Penhaligon stood at the edge of the lower plot, his boots dry despite the mud, his gloves immaculate. In his right hand he carried a stone maul—the heavy, iron-bound type used for splitting rock. The head rested against his shoulder, and his fingers wrapped around the handle with the ease of long familiarity.

He did not speak. He walked forward, his steps measured, and stopped three feet from where she knelt.

"Mr Penhaligon." Marget kept her voice flat. "I found the bone."

"I see that." His eyes moved from the fragment to the exposed filaments to the spread of her notebook. "You're digging where you shouldn't."

"The network centers here." She gestured to the bone. "Oswin's unmarked plot. You knew."

Arthur shifted the maul, letting the head drop a few inches. The iron thudded against the grass. "Leave it. Walk away. I'll fill the hole and you'll go back to your cataloguing."

"I've been cataloguing. That's how I know you flooded the lower graves to hide the pattern. The water slowed the network but it didn't break it. The filaments just went deeper, and they all lead here."

"Clever girl." He said it without warmth. "But you don't understand what you're protecting."

"I'm not protecting anything. I'm proving it."

"Then you've proved it. Congratulations. Now put the bone down and leave."

Marget picked up the fragment, cradling it in her numb left hand. The cold surged again, and she felt the outer graves release another degree of chill. "You don't want me near this spot. The ledger confiscation, the flooding, the stones you removed—all of it points to this single patch of ground. So either you explain why, or I keep digging."

Arthur stepped forward, the maul rising. He stopped with the iron head level with her chest. "I'm not explaining anything. The bone goes back in the ground, and you leave the graveyard. If I see you here tomorrow, I'll break your hands."

"With what?" She looked at the maul. "That's a splitting tool. It works on stone, not bone."

"It works on anything I swing it at."

Marget inhaled the cold air. Her lungs burned. She could feel the numbness climbing her left arm now, spreading past her elbow, but she kept the bone pressed against her palm.

Arthur did not lower the maul. "You think this is a game. You think there's a secret you can write down and publish and walk away from. There isn't. This ground eats secrets. It eats people." He pointed at the bone with his free hand. "That fragment used to be a man. You want to become the same? Stay."

"I'm not afraid of you."

"You should be." He adjusted his grip. "The moss doesn't care about your courage. It cares about warmth and time, and you're giving it both."

Marget looked past him. The central stone had absorbed the mist, its surface slick and dark. The raven hunched on top, its head tucked, watching with one flat eye.

"The raven never leaves," she said. "It's been there since I arrived. Even when you flooded the graves, it stayed."

"The bird is a symptom."

"What does that mean?"

Arthur's jaw tightened. He had already said too much. He raised the maul higher, positioning it for a swing. "Last warning. Drop the bone. Leave. Or I put you in the ground myself."

Marget did not drop the bone. She tightened her grip—what remained of her grip—and waited.

The ground beneath her shifted.

It was a small movement at first, a tremor like a lorry passing on a distant road. But it grew, a flexion of earth that rolled from the central stone outward, cracking the mud in radial spokes.

Arthur staggered. His boots slid on the wet grass, and the maul dropped to his side.

Marget planted her feet as the ground bucked again. The central stone wobbled—no, it tilted. The base had softened, the earth turning to slurry around it. The monolithic block leaned, its surface groaning against the roots that held it fast.

The raven shrieked and launched itself into the air, circling once before landing on the chapel roof.

Behind Arthur, the central stone tipped further, and the roots tore free in a spray of mud. The hole it left was dark and deep, and from that darkness came a pale shape—a cage of bone, tangled in green-white filaments.

A moss-bound human rib cage rose into view beneath the raven's feet, the sternum still intact, the left side collapsed where the soil had crushed it. The filaments wrapped around each rib like thread, pulsing with a slow, wet rhythm.

Arthur turned. He stared at the rising corpse, his face empty of all expression. The maul slipped from his fingers and landed with a dull thud.

Marget didn't move. She held Oswin's bone against her chest, and the cold was absolute, and she understood that she had only found the room before the door.
