The waterline crossed every cold mark on Marget Halloway's torn map and missed every grave she had left unmarked.

She crouched at the edge of the lower plot, the damp paper spread across her knee. The flood had receded hours ago, leaving a dark tideline on the granite slabs and a film of silt across the grass. She had torn this fragment from her ledger the night before Arthur Penhaligon confiscated it, working by candlelight, cutting away the section that held the cold marks she had mapped over six days. The rest of the book was in his pocket now. This piece was all she had left.

She traced her thumb along the pencil lines. Seven graves marked with her coldest readings. Seven crosses surrounded by circles where the temperature dropped below anything the surrounding soil could explain. She looked up. The water stain on the stones matched the outline exactly. The flood had risen precisely to the edges of her coldest marks and stopped, as if someone had drawn a boundary and poured water only inside it.

Marget pressed her palm flat against the paper. The numbness in her left hand had spread overnight. She could feel the grain of the page only as a vague pressure, the way she might feel a stone through a thick glove. Her index finger was gone. Her middle finger was gone. Her thumb registered only when she pressed hard enough to bend the nail. And the outer half of her palm—the meat below the pinky—had turned to something that did not belong to her.

She pulled out her ledger—the spare, the one Arthur did not know about—and made a note.

"Left hand: index, middle, thumb, outer palm—no sensation. Remaining: ring, pinky, inner palm—partial."

She dated it and closed the book.

The flood was no accident. Arthur had taken her map, seen which graves she had marked as coldest, and opened the sluice to submerge only those. He had not wanted her to examine them. He had wanted to bury the evidence under mud and standing water.

But he had missed the one grave she had not marked.

The central stone stood sixty feet away, the raven perched on its peak, its head cocked as if listening. Marget had never taken a reading from that stone because she had never touched it. She had walked around it, mapped its perimeter, but the cold there was so dense it felt like walking into a wall. She had marked it on the map with a different symbol—a hollow square—and Arthur had not flooded it. Either he did not know it was the source, or he wanted her to think it was not important.

She had a different test now.

Marget stood and walked to the shed where she had stashed Oswin Kerrow's notebook. The pressed feather was still inside, tucked between the last pages—a crow feather, black and curved, flattened by years inside the book. She had found it when she first discovered the notebook, and something about its placement had bothered her. Oswin had pressed it with the same care he had used for his botanical specimens. He had meant it to be found.

She carried the notebook to the central stone and sat on the damp ground, the feather resting on the open page.

The air was still. No wind.

She watched.

For three minutes, nothing happened. The feather lay motionless, its vane pointing toward the eastern edge of the graveyard, away from the stone. Marget pulled her collar tighter and waited. Her breath fogged. The cold that radiated from the stone was not the cold of winter or the cold of wet ground. It was a dry, architectural cold, as if the stone were drawing heat out of the air around it and storing it somewhere below.

Then she saw it.

A bead of frost formed on the shaft of the feather, near the quill. It spread slowly along the vane, not across the whole feather but only along the half that faced the central stone. The frost traced the barbs in silent lines, creeping from the base to the tip, and when it reached the end, it stopped. The other half of the feather remained dry.

Marget turned the notebook in a slow circle. The frost receded and reformed on whatever side of the feather now pointed toward the stone. She tested it four times. Each time, the frost appeared only on the vane oriented toward the central stone. The feather did not write. It did not speak. It was not a message. It was a compass, and its needle was the cold.

She set the notebook aside and looked at the ground between her knees. The flood had softened the earth here, turned it to dark mud. The water had drained away, but the soil was still loose, still shifting.

Arthur had closed the sluice.

She heard the mechanism engage—a metallic groan from the direction of the main gate—and then the wet sound of something heavy settling. The water level in the lower plot had dropped, but the ground beneath her had not stabilized. It was sinking in slow motion, the mud crawling toward the center of the graveyard like a basin tilting toward a drain.

Marget pushed herself upright and walked to the edge of the lower plot where she had laid out four glass dishes that morning. Each dish held a sample of moss scraped from a different grave—one from the eastern perimeter, one from the western, one from the northern, and one from the grave closest to the central stone. She had sealed them with lids, trapping the moisture inside.

She knelt beside the dishes and watched.

The moss from the eastern grave was still. The western sample was still. The northern sample showed no movement. But the sample from the grave near the central stone—the one she had marked as coldest—lay flat against the glass, its tendrils pressed to the surface as if listening.

Marget lifted the dish and tilted it. The moss did not slide. It held its position, anchored by something she could not see.

She set the dish down and tapped the glass with her fingernail.

The moss in the other three dishes flattened at the same instant.

Every strand, every frond, every tiny branch lay flush against the glass, leaving branching prints in the condensation. They moved as one, as if a signal had passed through the ground and reached them all simultaneously.

Marget's breath caught. She tapped the dish again, harder. The moss nearest the central stone twitched, and the others twitched in response, half a second later, like echoes.

They were connected.

She sat back on her heels and stared at the dishes. The moss was not separate. It was one organism, spread across the graveyard, and the grave nearest the central stone was its heart. Whatever pulsed inside the stone fed the network. Whatever fed the network made the moss grow. And whatever made the moss grow had consumed Oswin Kerrow fourteen months ago.

She pulled out her ledger and wrote:

"Moss samples from four graves show synchronized response when the sample nearest the central stone is disturbed. The network is continuous. The cold is not random—it is the network's respiration."

"Arthur Penhaligon flooded only the graves I marked. He knows what the network is. He is protecting it."

She closed the ledger and looked at the central stone. The raven had not moved. It stood on the peak, its beak slightly open, its black eye fixed on the horizon.

Marget stood and walked toward the stone. Her left hand was useless now, hanging at her side like a dead weight. She would need to record the numbness progression again tonight. She would need to measure the loss in millimeters and accept that soon she would not be able to feel the stones at all.

She stopped at the base of the central stone and pressed her right hand against its surface. The pulse was still there, low and steady, a heartbeat that did not belong to anything living.

The ground beneath her knees trembled.

She looked down.

The mud was cracking. Fine lines radiated from the base of the stone, spreading outward like a web. The cracks widened, and the soil slumped.

Marget dropped to her knees and watched the ground split open.

White filaments rose through the fissure, thick as rope, pale as bone. They stretched between the graves like tendons under peeled skin, connecting the cold marks she had mapped, following the same perfect circle she had drawn in her ledger. The network was not beneath the stones. It "was" the stones, woven through the earth, pulling everything inward.

The mud split again, and Marget saw the filaments pulse.

They were alive.

The raven opened its beak and made no sound.

And Marget Halloway knelt in the center of the web, her dead hand pressed to the mud, counting the beats of something that had never been human.
