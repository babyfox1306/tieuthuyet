"Give me the ledger," Arthur Penhaligon said, and closed his gloved hand around the arm Marget Halloway could no longer feel.

She watched his fingers indent the wool of her sleeve. The fabric dimpled. Her shoulder registered the stretch of the coat. The muscles of her back tensed against the pull. But the space between his glove and her elbow was a dead zone, a corridor of absence.

She did not flinch. She met his eyes.

"You cannot do this."

"Your contract is void," he said. His voice was flat, like the surface of the stagnant water in the ditch behind the yard. "You withheld primary documentation. I am taking possession of all data collected on my property."

"The data is my work."

"Which makes it mine."

He pulled the ledger from her satchel with his free hand. The spine creaked. The pages rustled. She did not resist. Her right arm dangled, useless, a dead weight in his grip.

He released her. She could not feel the release, only saw his hand lift away.

He turned and walked out of the tool shed. His boots were clean, dry. He did not look back.

The door clicked shut.

She heard his car door open, close. The engine started. The sound faded into the fog.

She stood up slowly. Her right leg was fine. Her left hand worked.

She reached into the pocket of her coat. The cloth was still warm from her body heat. Her fingers closed around the notebook.

Oswin Kerrow's field journal. The pages of soil data. The pressed feather slipped between the last pages.

Arthur had not searched her. He had the ledger, the cold-map. He did not know about the notebook.

She looked around the tool shed. The floor was packed earth. In the corner, a loose kiln brick, cracked from age.

She pried it up with the toe of her boot. The cavity beneath was dark and dry.

She wrapped the notebook and the feather in a square of oilcloth she kept in her satchel. She placed them in the hole. She pressed the brick back into place. The dust on the floor matched the dust on the brick. Invisible.

She wiped her hands on her trousers. A grey smear. She could not feel the smear on her right palm.

She stepped outside.

The fog was thick. The light was wrong, grey and directionless. She could not see his car, but she knew the road. She knew the moor.

She did not take the road. She took the path along the cut, where the peat was black and wet. The ground sucked at her boots. Her right arm refused to swing, a dead weight that threw her balance. She stumbled twice, catching herself on clumps of heather with her left hand. The spiny stems scraped her palm. She felt the scrape. She welcomed it.

She reached the low stone wall at the southern boundary of the graveyard. His car was parked at the bottom gate.

He was not in the car.

She saw him fifty yards below the wall, standing at the old sluice gate that bled into the drainage ditch for the lower plots.

She ducked behind a gorse bush. The thorns pressed her coat. She watched.

The sluice gate was set into a concrete housing thick with green slime. A rusted iron lever, vertical, locked in the down position by a heavy padlock. The lock was not broken. It was open.

The housing bore a fresh crescent of mud, pressed into the metal grain, proof that someone had opened it with a narrow tool rather than by accident. The tool had scraped the lock plate clean. The mud was still damp.

He had been here before. This was planned.

He pulled a thin iron rod from his coat pocket. He inserted it into the eye of the lock. The metal clicked. He pulled the lock free.

He braced his leg against the concrete and pulled the lever. The metal screamed. The gate below the waterline lifted.

A wall of dark water, thick with debris, erupted from the channel. It did not trickle. It surged. A wave two feet high rolled across the lower plots.

The moss lifted from the stones like a tablecloth shaken by invisible hands.

The grave markers vanished.

The ones she had touched. The ones she had mapped. The coldest points of the perimeter, the data she had bled for.

The cold circle she had drawn became a drowning.

Arthur stood at the lever, watching. His pocket watch was open in his gloved hand. He did not look at the watch. He looked at the water.

He was timing something.

She wanted to rush forward. To scream at him. To demand what he was doing.

But she stayed silent. She stayed still.

He remained there for a long minute. The water settled into the ditch, flat and dark. The moss floated on the surface, pale and stringy.

Then he turned. He walked back to his car. He drove away.

She waited until the sound of the engine was completely absorbed by the fog.

Then she moved.

She slid over the wall. Her boots hit the wet grass. She ran to the sluice.

The grate. The large iron drain grate at the corner of the wall, where the runoff from the flooded plots went underground.

Something was caught on the iron bars. A piece of paper.

Her paper.

The corner of her cold-map.

She knelt. The water was frigid. It soaked through the knees of her trousers.

She reached with her left hand.

Her fingers were stiff. They felt thick, clumsy. She could not feel the edge of the paper, only the pressure of her grip.

She pinched. The paper was wet, soft. It tore.

She pulled it free.

The rest of the ledger was gone. Pages, covers, everything. Swept into the dark hole beneath the earth, carried away by the runoff.

She was holding the only piece that survived.

She pressed it flat against her coat. The ink was smudged, bleeding into the fibers. But the pencil marks were still visible. The circle. The coldest points. The names of the graves now underwater.

She sat on the wet ground, her legs weak. She pulled out her own personal journal from her coat. The one Arthur did not know about.

She clicked the pencil. She pressed the point to the paper.

Her left index finger could not feel the edge of the page. She tried to write.

Left index: measurable numbness.

The letters were too large. The line wobbled. She could not feel the pencil in her grip. She could only see it move.

Left middle: measurable numbness.

The words were worse. The direction of the line was a guess, not a command.

Right arm: complete loss.

She flexed her right hand. The fingers curled. She saw the movement. But the signal did not arrive. The limb was a foreign object attached to her shoulder.

She looked back at the flooded plot.

The water was dark, still.

But something was moving across the surface.

A black line.

The moss.

It was rising.

It climbed out of the water, a thick, wet, fungal thread. It did not float with the current. It did not sink.

It crawled.

Across the mud. Past the submerged markers.

Toward her.

The raven on the central stone watched. Its head was unmoving.

Across the flooded plot, the submerged grave markers vanished one by one, but a black line of moss rose through the water and continued crawling toward Marget Halloway.
