The name Oswin Kerrow was written in the margin, in ink that had faded to brown, as if the pen had been dipped in rust.

Marget Halloway traced the letters with the pad of her thumb. The grain of the old paper caught on her calluses. The document was the keepers' log she had found in the locked cabinet behind the vicar's desk, and the marginal note was a single line, written thirty years ago by a clerk who had no idea what he was sitting on: "For Kerrow's full records, see the tin under the chapel floor."

She read the line again. Then she folded the log, tucked it into her satchel, and walked out into the fog.

The chapel was a ruin. The roof had collapsed in the center, and the floor was littered with slates and bird bones. The pews had been pushed against the walls, their wood warped and splintered. She walked the perimeter three times before she found the loose slab. It was hidden beneath a fallen pew, covered in a drift of dead leaves and dried moss. The mortar had crumbled to sand. She worked her fingers into the gap and pulled.

The stone lifted with a sucking sound, and a puff of cold air rose from the cavity beneath.

She scraped her knuckles on the edge of the flagstone and felt the sting only as a distant alarm, a fact reported from a province that was losing contact with the capital. She did not look at the blood. She looked at the tin.

It was a biscuit tin, the paint blistered and peeled into floral shapes that no longer matched. It had been wrapped in waxed cloth and sealed with twine. She carried it to the altar step, where the light was better, and sat down on the cold stone.

The twine was brittle. The knot gave when she pulled. She unwound the waxed cloth and lifted the lid.

The notebook lay inside, nestled in a bed of dry moss. She touched the moss. It crumbled to dust. It was dead. Dead, and not regrowing. She filed that fact away and pulled out the notebook.

The cover was oilcloth, stained with dark patches. She opened it. The first page was dated fourteen months before Oswin Kerrow had vanished. The entries were dense, meticulous, obsessive. Soil moisture at ten depths. Air temperature at dawn and dusk. The exact length of the moss fringe on each stone. The position of the raven on the central stone, recorded as a compass bearing.

She turned the pages. The handwriting changed over time. It started as neat block letters, then loosened into a hurried scrawl. The pauses got longer. The blank space between entries grew. The data became erratic. He stopped measuring air temperature. He stopped measuring moisture. He measured only the moss, and then only the cold.

The last page was different. The paper was crisp, unwarped, pressed into the back of the book as if added in a different season. The ink was darker, the hand shaking.

"The root reaches the surface tonight. I have confirmed it by touch. It feeds on the warmth of the dead. I will take the root. The raven will remember. Arthur must not find this book."

She read it three times. The root. Not the moss. Something under the stone that fed on warmth. And Arthur—Arthur must not find it. But Arthur already knew. He was harvesting something. The notebook had just given her a name for it.

She turned the notebook over and shook it. A feather fell out.

A raven's feather, the shaft snapped near the quill. She held it up to the light. The barbules were intact, perfectly preserved. He had kept it as a marker. As a witness. She turned it over in her fingers. The feather was weightless, but it felt heavy with meaning.

She slid it back between the last two pages of Oswin's notebook, where he had pressed it — the crisp leaf among the warped ones. It would stay there.

The door to the chapel groaned open.

She looked up. Arthur stood in the doorway, silhouetted against the grey sky. His shoes were clean. His gloves were buttoned at the wrist. He had not come to help. He had come to take.

Before he reached her, she closed the notebook and slipped it flat inside her coat, against the lining over her ribs. The cold-map ledger stayed in the satchel at her side — the one she had drawn every day since she arrived, marking the boundaries of the numbness, the temperature of the stones, the behavior of the moss. Let him find that. Let him think that was all of it.

"Miss Halloway."

His voice was flat, the voice of a man who had already decided how the conversation would end.

"I heard the stone scrape," he said. "You found something."

He did not ask. He stated. She did not stand. She stayed on the floor, empty hands visible on her lap.

"Oswin Kerrow's field notes," she said. "Fourteen months of data. He knew what was happening to the moss."

"I'm sure he did."

"He called it a root."

"You don't know what you're reading."

"Then tell me."

Arthur walked forward. He did not hurry. His steps were measured, precise. The stone floor was damp, but his shoes made no sound. He stopped in front of her and held out his hand.

"The notebook."

She kept her coat closed. The book pressed one thin edge against her ribs.

"I haven't got it on me. I read what I needed."

"You are finished with everything on this site. Give me what you found."

"It's evidence."

"It is my property."

"He wrote 'Arthur must not find this book'. Why?"

Arthur's eyes were cold, flat, unreadable. He did not answer. He looked at the biscuit tin, the empty waxed cloth, the satchel. He was stronger, and he was faster, and she had no rights on this land — but he did not put his hands on her coat.

Then he looked at the satchel at her side.

"Your ledger."

She felt the color drain from her face.

"It's my personal record."

"It contains measurements of my graveyard."

"Which I took with my hands and my ledger and my time."

"Which is my property under the terms of your contract. You agreed to submit all documentation."

She shook her head. "Arthur. Please."

"You are dismissed, Miss Halloway. The ledger."

She sat frozen. He reached into the satchel and pulled out the ledger. The leather of the spine creaked. The pages rustled. She watched him hold her cold-map in his gloved hands, the map she had drawn with fingers that were slowly dying, the map that contained every mark she had gathered since she arrived.

He opened it. He looked at the first page. He saw the lines, the temperatures, the notes. He flipped farther, hunting for the book she had already denied. There was no feather. No Oswin. Only her own hand.

He closed the book.

"You will leave the site by evening."

"I haven't finished."

"You have. Your contract is void if you withhold primary documentation from the landowner. You withheld. The contract is void. You will leave."

He turned and walked out.

The door clicked shut behind him. His footsteps crossed the yard. The gate clanged. The engine of his car turned over, caught, and faded into the fog.

She sat in the silence.

The ledger was gone — every cold mark, every temperature line, every stone she had measured with dying fingers. But Oswin's notebook was still against her ribs, the pressed feather still between its last pages. She had lost the map. She still had the book. And the cold was creeping up her arm.

She flexed her right hand. The fingers curled, but she could not feel the curl. She looked down and saw the movement, but the signal did not arrive.

She rolled up the sleeve of her right arm.

The skin was pale, bloodless, hard to the touch. The veins were faint, as if the blood had receded. She placed her left hand on her right elbow. The joint was a smooth, informationless sphere. She could feel her own fingers pressing, the pressure of her left hand against the wool of her sleeve, but the elbow itself was silent. It did not report back.

She moved her hand down to the forearm. The muscle beneath was a distant shape, muffled, as if she were touching it through a thick curtain. She pressed harder. The pressure deepened, but it did not become a feeling. It remained an idea.

She moved her hand down to her wrist. The bones were a cold geometry. She pressed her palm flat against her knuckles. Nothing. The contact was a fact in space, not a sensation in her body.

She pressed her left hand to her right and felt nothing at all—not the cuff, not the skin, not the bone beneath.
