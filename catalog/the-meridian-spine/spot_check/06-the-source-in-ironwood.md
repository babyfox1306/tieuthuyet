"You think you caught me," Renn says, his voice echoing in the Ironwood visitation room. "But you didn't catch the people who paid me. They're still out there, and now they own the referee."

Iris studies him through the scratched partition.

Aldous Renn has aged badly and maintained himself carefully, which is a difficult combination to achieve. His prison shirt is pressed. His gray hair is cut close. The flesh beneath his eyes has loosened, but his gaze remains sharp enough to invoice by the minute.

"You were convicted on forty-three counts," Iris says. "That qualifies as caught."

His mouth moves at one corner.

Renn places both hands on the steel table. His thumb taps against his middle finger twice, pauses, then taps once more.

Iris remembers the gesture from the trial videos. He used to do it while attorneys waited for answers, buying himself three seconds at a time and pretending the silence belonged to him.

"You came a long way to insult a man behind reinforced glass," he says.

"I can insult people closer to home. I came because a custom override used at Kessler has appeared in the Meridian Spine certification chain."

The tapping stops.

Not surprise. Control.

Renn leans back, and the chair legs scrape the concrete floor. A guard looks over from the wall, then returns his attention to the door.

"National Integrity Directorate," Renn says. "Independent certification. Meridian Spine verified clean."

The polished phrase lands between them with the sterile perfection of text approved by six committees.

"You've seen the campaign," Iris says.

"Everyone has seen the campaign."

"I asked whether you recognized the method."

"No, you told me it appeared."

"Petra Lund used it."

That earns Iris half a second of stillness.

Renn's attention drops to the document sleeve in front of her. It contains no originals. After the slide vanished from Directorate storage, Iris had become less generous with evidence and more imaginative about duplication.

She removes a printed micrograph of the Batch 743 fracture and places it against the glass.

Renn does not bend closer.

"You expect me to identify tool damage from a photocopy?"

"I expect you to recognize the reason I drove two hours to a prison that serves coffee capable of stripping paint."

"The coffee is better in the east wing."

"Then your punishment lacks consistency."

His smile is brief and bloodless.

Iris places the Kessler-era comparison image beside the first. Seven compression points. The drag at the fifth position. The skewed bite where the guide plate had shifted during loading.

Renn's eyes move from one image to the other.

He taps his thumb against his middle finger again.

"What happened to the original imaging?" he asks.

There it is.

Not what is that. Not how certain are you. He asks about the evidence she has not shown him.

Iris keeps her face flat. "Why assume something happened to it?"

"You brought paper into a room where paper is the least reliable object."

"You built companies out of paper."

"And you helped bury them with steel."

The old anger flashes, but it has nowhere useful to go. Renn had been guilty. His conviction remained supported by test records, diverted payments, fabricated approvals, and the physical remains of a bridge that had failed exactly where the falsified load coupons said it could not.

Justice had not made him innocent. It had only made him available.

Iris slides a second page from the sleeve. It is a copy of the Aegis Fabrication requisition bearing Alan Torvik's signature.

"Do you recognize the vendor?"

Renn reads it without touching the glass. "No."

"The coding?"

"Standard consumable."

"It purchased a custom jig."

"Then someone wanted it to pass through a budget line that didn't trigger technical review."

"That answer came quickly."

"Prison gives a man time to appreciate accounting."

Iris watches his eyes. They remain on the bottom of the requisition, not on Torvik's signature or the Aegis name.

The footer contains the procurement template number and a small boxed address for service notices. Nothing dramatic. Fraud had always been offended by drama. It preferred eight-point type.

"What are you reading?" she asks.

"Nothing."

"Your nothing is at the bottom right."

Renn looks up.

Iris takes out another page, this one from the public acquisition notice showing Veridian Trust's purchase of Aegis Fabrication. She sets it beside the requisition.

"Aegis entered the certification chain before Veridian acquired it," she says. "But its legal service address remained unchanged through the acquisition."

Renn's fingers start their rhythm.

Two taps. A pause. One.

Iris puts her thumbnail against the edge of the acquisition notice. The paper catches slightly at one corner where the prison scanner had fed it crooked.

"Who used that address during Kessler?"

Renn says nothing.

She lets the silence collect.

The visitation room has been designed to make silence uncomfortable. Hard surfaces return every cough, every chair scrape, every distant lock engaging. Even breathing becomes public.

Renn had once used that effect to control courtrooms. Now Iris could see him counting the seconds.

"You didn't come for Lund," he says at last.

"I came for a legal address."

"You already have one."

"I have a box in a commercial tower used by twelve registered entities."

"Then you have eleven distractions and one invoice."

"Which invoice?"

His gaze shifts toward the guard. Not fear. Calculation.

"I don't keep records here."

"No. You kept them in offices registered under holding companies."

"And those records were seized."

"One page wasn't recovered."

The tapping stops again.

Iris had seen that page only in an index from the Kessler discovery archive: co-ownership schedule, supplemental folio, restricted circulation. The document itself had never appeared in the recovered material. Every surviving reference to its contents had been redacted.

Renn lowers his voice. "Careful, Ms. Kane."

"I was careful at Kessler. You went to prison anyway."

"You found the mechanism. You found the people operating it. Then you stopped where the evidence stopped."

"I don't sign conclusions that outrun the material."

"That must be comforting."

"It isn't. That's why it works."

His eyes return to the acquisition notice.

"Veridian didn't prepare its own papers," he says.

That is not evidence. It is a direction.

"Who did?"

Renn reaches one hand toward the glass, his index finger hovering opposite the tiny service address.

"Look at the filing folio, not the registered agent. The folio prefix belongs to the firm that assembled the structure."

"Name the firm."

"You won't trust the name."

"Correct."

"So why ask?"

"To measure whether you lie before I obtain the filing."

The muscle along his jaw tightens. He respects precision only when he is the one charging for it.

"Vale Soren Legal," he says.

The name sits cold in Iris's mind. Familiar, but not from the Meridian file.

Renn watches recognition arrive despite her attempt to deny him the satisfaction.

"They represented your companies," she says.

"They incorporated some of them."

"How many?"

"Enough to bill aggressively."

"Did they establish Veridian Trust?"

"I didn't say that."

"You told me to read the folio prefix."

"I told you where to look."

"And if the prefix matches?"

Renn leans closer to the glass. "Then the firm behind Veridian's structure is the same firm that built the corporate partitions around my contractors."

Not an answer to the larger question. Not proof of ownership. A legal origin shared by entities that were supposed to belong to separate eras.

Iris looks down at the papers.

Aegis supplied the custom jig before Veridian acquired it. Lund used the override method. Torvik's requisition disguised specialized tooling as a consumable. If Veridian's incorporation trail ran through Vale Soren, then the new chain did not merely resemble Kessler's.

It had been assembled at the same workbench.

"Who instructed Vale Soren?" she asks.

Renn smiles without warmth. "There it is."

"What?"

"The question that was missing from your first investigation."

"The instruction would be privileged."

"The payment wouldn't."

"Whose payment?"

His thumb meets his middle finger.

Two taps.

The guard announces that five minutes remain.

Renn glances at the clock. "They'll have replaced the file you lost by now."

Iris feels the words strike, but she gives him nothing.

"I didn't say I lost a file."

"You didn't need to."

"How would you know anything was removed from Directorate evidence storage?"

"I know what happens when competent people get close to expensive facts."

"That sounds polished enough to mean nothing."

"It means you should stop carrying the only copy."

"I stopped doing that before your trial."

"And yet you came with photocopies."

The guard takes one step toward their table.

Iris gathers the pages into the sleeve. "Did Vale Soren handle the hidden co-ownership schedule?"

Renn's expression closes.

The question has found a locked surface. She runs her thumbnail along it anyway.

"That page was missing before seizure," she continues. "Someone removed it while your records were still under private control."

"I have nothing else for you."

"That wasn't a denial."

"It wasn't testimony either."

"Good. Testimony is soft."

For the first time, real irritation cuts through his composure.

"You still think material tells the whole truth."

"No. It tells the part people can't revise after lunch."

The guard signals the end of the visit.

Renn stands. "Get the certified folios, Kane. Not the online abstracts. The abstracts were designed for people who enjoy being wrong quickly."

Then he turns away.

Iris remains seated until the secured door closes behind him.

On the table, one of her photocopies has shifted beneath the edge of the sleeve. The Vale Soren name is not printed anywhere on it, but she can already see where it should appear: in the filing metadata, beneath the public layer, attached to the hands that assembled Veridian before Veridian acquired Aegis.

A connection was not a conclusion.

It was enough to choose the next document.

Three hours later, Iris is back in her Directorate office with the blinds closed and two certified corporate folios spread across her desk.

She had requested them from the commercial registry before leaving Ironwood. The courier envelope arrived at 4:18, its red wax authentication strip pressed with a ridged seal that left a tiny crescent missing from the lower edge.

The first folio belongs to Veridian Trust.

The second belongs to a Renn-controlled engineering company dissolved after Kessler.

Iris aligns the pages by their filing blocks.

Veridian: VS-41-7739.

Renn's company: VS-41-2098.

Same prefix.

She turns both pages under her desk lamp. Each carries a blind embossment near the bottom margin, almost invisible unless the light hits from the side. Four narrow lines form a stepped column around the initials VSL.

Vale Soren Legal.

Physical copies. Certified source. Same originating firm.

She opens the Aegis acquisition folio and finds the same embossment on the restructuring schedule. Then she places Torvik's requisition beside it, followed by the printout of Lund's exception record.

The evidence chain is damaged. The original slide image is gone. The anonymous note can guide but cannot prove. Torvik's signature may be genuine, borrowed, or placed on a form he never saw.

But the fractured Batch 743 coupon still exists. Its marks connect Lund's act to the Kessler method. The requisition connects the jig to Aegis. The acquisition record connects Aegis to Veridian. The certified folios connect Veridian's legal origin to the same firm that built Renn's corporate network.

No single page answers the question.

Together, they refuse coincidence.

A knock sounds once.

Before Iris can answer, Nadia opens the door.

Her gaze crosses the desk with quiet efficiency. "You went to Ironwood."

"You monitor prison visitors now?"

"Renn's name triggers an internal notification when a Directorate officer requests access."

"Convenient."

"It's a safeguard."

"Safeguards usually protect evidence."

Nadia's eyes sharpen. "Have you reported the missing slide?"

"To the same system that logged Alan Torvik entering the locker at two in the morning?"

"You have a signature. You don't have proof that Torvik made it."

"Finally, something we agree on."

Nadia steps closer but does not touch the papers. "What did Renn give you?"

"A filing reference."

"And you trusted him?"

"I trusted the registry."

Iris rotates the folios so Nadia can see the matching embossments.

Nadia studies them. For one brief instant, her calm slips. Not enough to name what lies beneath it, but enough for Iris to mark the fracture.

"Vale Soren," Nadia says.

"You know them."

"They handled complex corporate structures during the Kessler period. Half the major infrastructure firms used them."

"Veridian used them too."

"That doesn't prove common control."

"I didn't say it did."

Nadia's shoulders ease by a degree. She had prepared to fight a conclusion Iris had not made.

Interesting.

Iris slides the Batch 743 image beside the folios. "It proves Lund's evidence chain reaches the same legal origin as Renn's network. That is where the material stops today."

"And tomorrow?"

The office phone rings.

Both women look at it.

The external line indicator is lit, followed by the secure executive code.

Iris answers. "Kane."

A measured male voice identifies himself as Stellan Marsh's chief secretary.

"Mr. Marsh requests a private meeting tomorrow at nine. His office. No additional attendees."

Nadia goes very still.

Iris looks at the matching folio marks, the false procurement category, and the empty evidence sleeve beside her keyboard.

"What's the subject?"

"Your Meridian Spine findings."

"Which findings?"

A pause.

"Mr. Marsh said you would know."

The line disconnects.

Iris lowers the receiver, and the dial tone hums into the room. On her desk are Renn's law firm, Veridian Trust, Aegis, Lund, and the missing slide. She does not have the full picture yet, but she has the shape of the chain.

And Stellan Marsh is calling her in.
