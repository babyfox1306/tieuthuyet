Iris Kane lowers the blade toward the seal backing and says, “If these perforations align, somebody opened the bay after certification.”

The room behind them was a cube of white light, but Iris kept her focus on the thin strip of tamper-evident tape pinned under the magnifier. Nadia Okafor stood at the edge of the table, arms crossed, watching.

Iris worked the tip of the scalpel under the backing of the seal she had collected from the bay door the previous night. Beside it lay a fresh strip of the same tape, straight from the sealed roll in the security logbook.

“The perforation pattern on a tamper-evident label is a sequential cut,” Iris said. “The die wears with every label produced. The first label from a roll has a heavier cut than the last. If the variation pattern on the bay seal matches a label from the middle of the roll, and the roll was finished two weeks ago, I can prove the seal was applied recently.”

She lined up the two pieces under the magnifier. The backing of the bay seal showed a slight drag on the left edge of the perforation. The control strip showed the same drag, same edge.

“Same batch,” she said. “But look at the adhesive.”

She pressed the bay seal flat. “Two distinct sets of particles. The first is standard ambient dust, evenly distributed, which means the seal was exposed to air for a period before being applied. The second set is concentrated along the edges. This is ferrous aluminosilicate. It’s used in the Meridian fabrication line’s grinding slurry.”

Nadia said nothing.

“The fabrication line uses aluminosilicate for their finishing passes. The materials lab uses a garnet-based slurry. The IT floor uses compressed air and plastic media. This grain is unique to one source. Someone from the fabrication line touched this seal before it was applied to the bay.”

“Or their equipment did,” Nadia said quietly.

“The grain was transferred after the seal was pulled out of its packaging. Look at the layering. The original dust is covered by the grain. The seal was exposed to the fabrication floor after the packaging was opened, but before it was applied to the bay door. The bay was sealed with a contaminated label. Post-certification interference. The chain of custody on the replacement coupon was broken before the door even closed.”

Iris straightened. “I need the physical access ledger.”

Nadia’s jaw tightened. “The digital log should be enough. We can cross-reference badge swipes.”

“The biometric system was down in the bay corridor for maintenance the day before the replacement test. I checked the IT maintenance log this morning. No biometric verification was recorded for bay four-B for a seventy-two-hour window. The only record of who entered that bay is the paper ledger.”

“The ledger is controlled.”

“I have a standing forensic request. You have two hours to honor it, or I contact Marsh’s office directly and explain that the Directorate’s internal security has a gap large enough to drive a fraud investigation through.”

Nadia studied her for a long moment. “You’re not going to survive this department, Iris.”

“I don’t need to survive the department. I need to survive the evidence.”

Nadia pulled a key from an inner pocket of her jacket. “The physical ledgers are in the shift supervisor’s safe. I logged it out this morning.”

“You knew I would ask for it.”

“I knew you were smart enough to realize the digital copy was useless for your purposes. I didn’t know you would find what you just found.”

“What did you think I would find?”

Nadia didn’t answer. She turned and walked out of the room.

Iris waited.

The air in the bay tasted of recycled metal and the faint petroleum of machine coolant that bled through the ventilation from the fabrication floor. She looked at the seal under the magnifier again. The grain was a betrayal. The fabrication floor was supposed to be a sealed environment. If the contaminant had come from there, the fraud was deeper than a single overridden coupon. Someone had walked the material through the clean room, or their tools had, and they had touched the seal after it was removed from the controlled supply.

Nadia returned holding a heavy bound book with a steel-reinforced spine. She placed it on the table. “Bay four-B. June seventeenth through June twenty-second.”

Iris opened it.

The pages were thick, off-white, covered in printed grid lines. The original entries were handwritten in blue ink, with a date stamp at the top of each day’s log.

She found June twentieth. The day after the original certification. The day she had been at the Ironwood facility.

Three entries. A shift supervisor’s opening check at 06:00. A materials handler at 11:30. And a late entry at 14:17.

Iris read the handwriting. The shift supervisor’s was neat. The handler’s was cramped. The 14:17 entry was a precise mechanical print that looked almost typewritten.

She looked at the credential number column.

Her own number stared back. ND-IFA-10743.

“I was at Ironwood at fourteen-seventeen,” she said. Her voice was flat, empty. “I signed the visitor register at the front desk. I was logged into the secure test chamber under video supervision. I was not in this building.”

Nadia took a slow breath. “But the ledger says you were.”

Iris ran her thumbnail across the paper. “The original machine they used to print the grid lines has a pressure ridge on every character. A tiny ridge of black ribbon wax, pushed up by the dot matrix pin. Every original entry, every page, has this ridge. Look.”

She tilted the page under the lamp. The light caught the surface at a sharp angle, revealing a subtle textured line along each printed character.

“Now look at this row.”

She traced the 14:17 entry, the credential number, the bay code. “The characters have no ridge. They were printed by a different machine. Someone fed this page into a clean ribbon printer and overstamped a single entry.”

Nadia leaned forward. “Overstamped?”

“The underlying entry was erased first. The original ribbon wax was scraped off the paper. See the disturbed fibers here, and here.” Iris pointed to faint discolorations around the ink. “The paper is rougher in those spots. The original characters were manually removed before the new ones were applied.”

She pulled the LED lamp closer. The light caught the indentations at a sharper angle, revealing ghost impressions beneath the new print.

“The original typeface was narrower. The machine that printed the original was an older model, different font spacing. The new printer has a wider strike. The overstamp is misaligned by almost half a millimeter. Whoever did this wasn’t trying to copy the original. They were trying to make it look like a genuine late addition.”

“What was the original entry?”

“I don’t know. Not yet. The ghost impressions are shallow. I need an oblique light source and a higher magnification to read the residual indentations. But the format is visible. Seven characters. Probably a contractor ID or a visitor code.”

She looked up. “Someone replaced a real entry with mine.”

Nadia said nothing.

“The access time is 14:17,” Iris said. “When I was behind the gates at Ironwood. But the credential number stamped beside it is mine.”

The words hung in the sterile air.

“This isn’t a mistake,” Iris said. “It’s a planted piece of chain-of-custody evidence. Whoever created this entry picked the exact time I was physically distant. They used my credential number. They had access to the physical ledger. They knew the biometric system would be down. They knew everything.”

“They are inside the building,” Nadia said. “Inside the investigation.”

“Yes.”

Iris closed the ledger. The click of the cover echoed through the empty corridor.

“The next time someone opens this book, they will see my name attached to the tampered bay. If I publish my findings, the chain of custody is destroyed. If I stay silent, the fraud becomes the official record.”

“You’re trapped.”

Iris shook her head slowly. “I’m not trapped. Contained, maybe — someone wants this inquiry inside a fence. But I have the physical evidence. I have the seal and the grain. I have the ribbon wax. And now I have a precise timestamp for the forgery.”

She picked up the scalpel again.

“Let’s see who owned the original entry.”
