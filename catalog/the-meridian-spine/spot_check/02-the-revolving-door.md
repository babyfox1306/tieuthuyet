"The first material I ever certified is still standing," Petra Lund says, knocking her knuckles against the spine section in the showroom. "That means something where I come from." The echo of the sound was hollow, almost too deep, as if the steel itself was trying to swallow the noise. Iris Kane watched Lund's hand, noting the slight tremor. Lund’s confidence, Iris had already observed, was a carefully constructed facade.

The showroom was a testament to the Directorate's self-proclaimed triumph: a pristine, polished segment of the Meridian Spine, bathed in soft, flattering light. It stood like a monument to integrity, a stark contrast to the rough-hewn reality of construction sites Iris usually frequented. Nadia Okafor stood slightly behind them, her expression unreadable, a quiet observer.

"And how many sections have you certified since then, Ms. Lund?" Iris asked, her voice calm, professional, yet carrying an edge of precise inquiry. She ran a thumbnail along the smooth edge of the display section, feeling for any imperfection, any hairline crack in the narrative.

Lund chuckled, a sound that didn't quite reach her eyes. "Hundreds, Ms. Kane. Thousands. My career has been built on ensuring the structural integrity of this nation's backbone. The Meridian Spine is simply the latest, largest project." She gestured vaguely at the expansive facility surrounding them, a labyrinth of workshops and testing labs humming with muted activity. "Every component that goes into it passes through my department."

Iris nodded slowly, her gaze sweeping over the various display panels detailing the rigorous certification process. "Every component," she repeated, her eyes fixing on a timeline graphic. "Including the load coupons for the base materials. Certified last week, you said."

"That's right. Independent certification; Meridian Spine verified clean," Lund affirmed, puffing out her chest slightly. "My team, personally."

"And the tooling used for those cuts," Iris pressed, her voice unwavering. "Is it standard issue for Directorate labs, or was it a custom piece, perhaps from a contractor?" The question hung in the air, a microscopic thread in the vast, woven fabric of Lund's assurance.

Lund hesitated, her gaze flickering to Nadia for a fraction of a second, almost imperceptibly. "Standard protocol dictates approved equipment, of course," she said, her voice a shade less confident. "But some specialized projects require specialized tools. Nothing unusual." She adjusted her glasses, a tic that Iris filed away. "You understand, Ms. Kane, the scale of the Spine demands flexibility."

Flexibility, Iris thought. Or an opening for manipulation. She turned her attention to the wall display, a digital timeline of the Meridian Spine's material procurement and certification process. Batch numbers, dates, approval stamps. Her eyes moved with forensic speed, tracking the flow of information. She focused on the initial batches, the ones that formed the very foundation of the visible showroom section.

"These early batches," Iris pointed to a series of green checkmarks on the screen. "Material delivery dates are listed here, followed by the testing completion dates. And then, the Directorate's official approval timestamp." Her thumbnail paused at a specific entry. "For this particular structural steel batch, Batch 743. Delivered on the 10th of last month. Testing completed on the 15th." She paused, letting the numbers sink in. "But the Directorate's approval stamp for release to construction is dated the 12th."

A silence descended, heavy and thick. Lund’s confident posture visibly stiffened. Nadia remained still, a statue carved from professional neutrality.

"That's... a minor administrative discrepancy," Lund stammered, her gaze darting between the screen and Iris's unblinking eyes. "Sometimes, preliminary approvals are granted based on initial strength tests, with full certification following shortly after. It's a standard practice for accelerated schedules on a project of this magnitude." Her hands, which had been gesticulating confidently moments before, now clasped together tightly.

Iris’s internal monologue was already dissecting Lund’s explanation. "Preliminary approvals for a project of this magnitude, before all tests are complete? That's not flexibility; that's reckless. Or worse, it’s a cover." Her mind, ever scanning for structural weaknesses, saw not a discrepancy but a deliberate anomaly: a conflict in approval timestamps that suggested backdated certification.

"Standard practice is to ensure "all" tests are completed and verified before any approval stamp is granted, Ms. Lund," Iris countered, her voice quiet but firm. "Especially for load-bearing materials in a national infrastructure project. A preliminary approval isn't a final certification. And the Directorate's seal explicitly states 'Meridian Spine verified clean' — not 'preliminarily accepted pending further review'."

Lund's face colored slightly. "I assure you, Ms. Kane, no material is ever used without full confidence in its integrity. These are merely... internal process adjustments to meet deadlines." She avoided Iris's gaze, turning instead to Nadia. "Perhaps Ms. Kane would benefit from seeing the actual testing facility, rather than just the displays." The attempt to steer the conversation was palpable.

Nadia stepped forward then, her voice smooth and even. "A good suggestion, Petra. Ms. Kane, the labs are impressive. We can show you the tensile strength rigs." She cast a quick, almost imperceptible glance at Iris, a silent urging to move on.

Iris noted the practical offer — a lab tour could confirm whether the dates on screen matched anything physical. "All right. Show me the rigs." Her focus stayed on the conflicting dates a moment longer, then she followed.

"Before we do," Iris said, "I'd like to inspect the equipment used for the cutting of those load coupons. Specifically, the jig that produced the shear line on Batch 743, the one with the... unique pattern." She pulled the load coupon from her pocket, holding it up, the microscopic wave pattern barely visible under the showroom lights. "I'm interested in its calibration records, and its serial number."

Lund's jaw tightened. "The cutting equipment is standard industrial machinery. We have dozens of identical units. It's impossible to pinpoint one specific jig for one specific coupon." Her voice was now laced with a defensive edge. "The sheer volume of material we process..."

"Then we'll inspect all of them," Iris cut in, her tone leaving no room for argument. "Starting with the records for all cutting jigs active last month. I'd like to cross-reference them with manufacturer databases. For quality control, of course."

Lund's evasion was now unmistakable. Her eyes darted around, searching for an escape. "That would be a considerable undertaking, Ms. Kane. My team is already stretched thin with current certifications."

"Then I'll handle it," Iris stated simply. "I'll require full access to your equipment logs and procurement records, Ms. Lund. By the end of the day." She met Lund's gaze, her own unwavering. The lead certifier, for the first time, looked genuinely uncomfortable, her initial confidence completely evaporated.

Later that afternoon, back in the clinical sterility of her Directorate office, Iris pored over the digital records provided by Lund's reluctant team. The task was indeed tedious, a maze of serial numbers, maintenance logs, and calibration reports. Her screen displayed a spreadsheet listing every cutting jig and load test rig used in the Directorate's labs over the past year.

She zoomed in on the data for Batch 743, the material from which her suspect load coupon originated. According to Lund’s records, the cutting was performed by a "Model 3000 Precision Shear." There were six such units in the lab. Iris cross-referenced their usage logs, tracing the movements and operations of each machine. One particular unit, designated MS-Cutter-04, showed a significant spike in activity during the relevant period.

Iris's fingers flew across the keyboard, pulling up the maintenance file for MS-Cutter-04. The reports detailed regular servicing, blade replacements, and calibration checks. All appeared meticulously documented, almost too perfect. She scrolled down, looking for the manufacturer's serial number for the custom tooling Lund had alluded to, the jig responsible for the unique wave pattern.

The field was there, clearly labeled "Tooling Serial Number." But the entry was blank. Not "N/A," not "unrecorded." It was an empty cell, a void in a database otherwise filled with meticulous detail. A system designed for absolute transparency had a hole in its most critical data point.

Iris frowned, a deep line appearing between her brows. She cross-referenced the equipment model and the blank serial number with the manufacturer's public database. No match. No record of specialized tooling for that model with a missing serial number. She then searched for any internal Directorate registry of custom-made equipment. Again, nothing. The silence of the blank field screamed louder than any alarm.

"Deliberately erased," she thought, her internal voice cold and precise. "Not faded, not misprinted. Removed. Erased with surgical precision." This wasn't an oversight. The serial number for the load-test rig's tooling was not registered with the manufacturer — a blank where a chain of custody should have been.

She leaned back in her chair, her eyes fixed on the empty cell. The tool-mark on the coupon, the conflicting timestamps, Lund's evasiveness, and now this. Each piece was a thread, weaving a familiar pattern. A pattern she had seen before, at Kessler.

A soft knock on her door. Nadia.

"Everything alright, Iris?" Nadia's voice was calm, concerned. She stepped into the office, her gaze immediately falling on the screen, then on Iris's face.

"Just... unraveling a few threads," Iris replied, closing the spreadsheet to a neutral desktop background. She didn't want Nadia to see the blank serial number just yet. "This 'independent certification' process is more complex than it appears on the surface."

Nadia nodded. "It often is. Especially with projects of this scale. The financial infrastructure alone is a behemoth. Halveston's philanthropic fund, for example, is pouring billions into the Spine. It requires constant oversight, beyond just material integrity."

Iris noted the casual mention of Halveston, the subtle shift towards the financial angle. "There it is again," she thought. "The redirection. Why is she so keen on Halveston?" It wasn't that financial oversight wasn't important, but it felt like a deliberate attempt to pull her away from the physical evidence she was uncovering.

"The physical evidence speaks loudest," Iris said, meeting Nadia's gaze. "A material discrepancy can bring down an entire structure, no matter how solid the financing."

Nadia's smile was slight. "Of course. But sometimes, where the money flows, the real weakness lies. Halveston's fund has a lot of influence. A lot of approvals go through them." She paused, letting the implication hang. "Just something to keep in mind, as you dig."

Iris simply nodded, a noncommittal gesture. She had her own digging to do. The missing serial number, the backdated approvals, the familiar tool-mark – it all pointed to one thing. The method. The same method that had brought down Kessler. The same method that Renn had used.

Her gaze drifted to the small, worn notepad from her old Kessler files, open on her desk. The single, handwritten line: "Find the other names." Celia Ward's question from a long-forgotten interview looped in Iris's head then, a phantom echo. "Unresolved co-owners." She opened the redacted page from the Kessler case again, the blacked-out lines mocking her. She scrolled to Renn's trial testimony. 'I was paid by a company. A developer. Ask the developers.'
