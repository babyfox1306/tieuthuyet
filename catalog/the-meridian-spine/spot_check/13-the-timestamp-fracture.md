"You've chosen a dangerous path," Stellan Marsh says, his voice flat as he slides the leaked article across his desk. "Let's see if your evidence can walk it."

Iris left the article where it stopped. Touching it would let him pretend they were discussing the leak instead of the certification his office had released thirteen minutes after the article appeared.

She placed two transparent evidence sleeves beside it.

"The Batch 743 test closed at 16:42 on the fifteenth," she said. "The Meridian approval record says the certification was authorized at 15:10."

Marsh looked at the sleeves, not at her. He adjusted his left cufflink by less than a quarter turn.

An inconvenient number had entered the room.

His office had been designed to make inconvenient things look temporary. Pale walls. Low furniture. A ceramic model of the Meridian Spine with every support rendered in white, as though infrastructure could be purified by removing color.

"Those times come from separate systems," he said.

"They come from separate physical print queues."

Iris pushed the first sleeve forward. It held the original thermal strip from the load-testing station, signed across the feed edge by Petra Lund. The second contained a carbon copy from the approval desk, its timestamp struck hard enough to emboss the duplicate sheet beneath it.

Marsh did not reach for either.

"Explain the distinction you believe matters."

He already understood it. Asking her to explain moved the burden back into her mouth, where he could call it interpretation.

She kept her thumbnail against the sealed edge of the second sleeve, feeling for a flaw that was not there.

"The load station printed its close record at 16:42. The approval desk stamped final certification at 15:10. The approval therefore predates the result by ninety-two minutes."

"Or a clock was misaligned."

"The machines were synchronized at 08:00 that morning."

She added a third sleeve. Inside lay the synchronization ticket, narrow and gray, with paired punch marks from the test floor and approval office clocks.

Marsh studied the punch marks. "Synchronization can drift."

"Not backward by ninety-two minutes in under nine hours."

"No system is infallible."

"That is why there are physical controls. The station clock cut a triangular notch into each hourly strip. The approval clock cut a semicircle. Both notches advanced correctly through every record before and after Batch 743."

She set down four comparison strips. The progression was visible without a magnifier.

Marsh finally lifted his gaze. His expression held no anger, only a disciplined disappointment that had probably ended careers more efficiently than shouting.

"You came prepared to establish a discrepancy."

"I came prepared to remove the excuses around it."

"That sentence will play well in another article."

"I did not come for applause."

"No. You came to force an institutional response before the institution could complete its review."

Iris glanced at the leaked article. The headline reported a compromised evidence bay and an access entry made under her stolen credentials. It did not mention the timestamp fracture. That piece had remained inside her case file.

For the moment.

"The institution completed enough of its review to broadcast a clean certification," she said.

"Meridian cannot be suspended every time an analyst finds an irregular field."

"A result authorized before it exists is not an irregular field."

Marsh rose, but not abruptly. He crossed to the side table and poured water into one glass. He did not offer her any.

Even hospitality was evidence here. One glass meant no attempt at warmth, no performance of concern. He wanted the exchange clean enough to survive being quoted.

"Petra Lund has been removed from certification duty," he said. "The compromised bay is sealed. The access system is under examination. Corrective steps are underway."

"None of those steps changes 15:10."

"No. They provide context."

"Context does not reverse time."

His fingers settled around the glass. "What conclusion are you asking me to sign?"

She almost smiled. He knew exactly where her boundary lived and had chosen to stand on it.

"No conclusion yet. An acknowledgment."

"Of what?"

"That the certification time was backdated."

Marsh took one measured drink. The office stayed silent long enough for the ventilation system to click into a lower cycle.

Iris watched his cuff, his hand, the angle of his shoulders. He would not deny a fact that could be carried into public on three pieces of paper. He would narrow it, rename it, and surround it with responsible language.

"The approval record reflects an administrative backdating," he said at last. "That does not, by itself, invalidate the underlying materials result."

There it was.

Not surrender. Marsh did not surrender. He conceded one square inch of ground and built a podium on it.

Iris slid a prepared memorandum across the desk. "Sign that wording."

His eyes dropped to the page.

"You anticipated my language."

"I anticipated the smallest fact you could acknowledge without lying."

The corner of his mouth moved, not quite amusement. "You mistake restraint for evasion."

"Only when they leave the same tool marks."

He read the memorandum. It stated that the recorded approval time preceded completion of the relevant test and had been entered retrospectively. It assigned no motive and named no culprit beyond the already removed certifier.

Marsh uncapped his pen.

"Your public challenge will be limited to the timing issue," he said.

"My release will say what the physical comparison supports."

"You are an officer of this Directorate."

"I am its Lead Forensic Authority."

"Those roles are not in conflict."

"They became inconveniently adjacent yesterday."

His eyes met hers. Marsh never blinked too quickly or looked away too soon. He held eye contact like a hearing room belonged to him even when no cameras were present.

"The country is watching Meridian," he said. "A premature allegation could damage contracts, regional access, and public confidence."

"The country will survive a reopened certification."

"That confidence took years to rebuild."

"Then it should not rest on a timestamp that travels backward."

He signed.

The pen pressure remained even from the first letter to the last. No tremor, no angry gouge through the paper. Stellan Marsh had just acknowledged a backdated certification, and even his signature refused to look cornered.

He capped the pen and pushed the memorandum toward her.

"You have your acknowledgment."

"I have one fact."

"And what will you do with it?"

"Test whether it has neighbors."

For the first time, something sharpened behind his expression.

It vanished before it could become proof.

"Be careful which associations you imply," he said. "The similarity between methods does not establish a common actor."

"I have not named an actor."

"Others will."

"Others can answer for their standards."

Marsh returned to his chair. "Your authority is not unlimited."

"No authority worth trusting is."

His cufflink shifted beneath his thumb again. "You may issue a technical notice reopening Batch 743 certification. It will contain no personal attribution without further evidence."

That was the line he wanted: a controlled technical correction, small enough to absorb.

It was also more than he had intended to grant when she entered.

Iris collected the signed memorandum and every original strip. She counted the sleeves twice before sealing them into the rigid case at her feet.

At the door, Marsh spoke again.

"The Directorate selected you because your conclusions survive pressure."

She turned.

"Then stop applying pressure and see what survives."

His face remained composed. "Pressure is how structures are tested."

"No. Load is how they are tested. Hidden pressure is how results are falsified."

She left before he could reframe the distinction.

At 11:26, Iris issued the technical notice from a terminal in the document-control room. She attached two side-by-side micrographs: the Batch 743 fracture and the archived Kessler override fracture, cropped to show the matching compression geometry and guide-plate damage.

The notice named no person.

It stated only that the Meridian Spine coupon displayed a tooling-method match to the Kessler pattern and that certification must be reopened pending independent examination. Marsh's signed acknowledgment of retrospective approval timing appeared as Appendix Three.

She read every line twice before authorizing distribution.

The notice was partial by design. It did not claim a shared operator, supplier, or chain of command. Physical similarity could establish method. It could not, on its own, select the hand that used it.

At 11:31, the distribution printer produced a custody copy.

At 11:32, someone slid a cream envelope beneath the document-control room door.

Iris did not touch it immediately.

The corridor outside was empty when she checked. No retreating footsteps. No helpful witness arriving at the perfect moment. Anonymous people always imagined anonymity was a vanishing act. Usually, it was only a collection of residues waiting to be read.

She photographed the envelope in place, then lifted it with nylon forceps.

There was no handwriting. Her name had been impressed through a label maker, each letter slightly depressed on the right side. The envelope flap carried a narrow band of brittle adhesive and one trapped blue fiber.

Inside was a folded photocopy and a strip of yellow routing paper.

The photocopy reproduced a policy directive from the Directorate's executive office. Its subject line concerned continuity measures for nationally significant certifications. The body authorized approval entries to be aligned with scheduled release windows when late-stage testing created a publication delay.

Aligned. A polite word for moving a result to where the calendar required it.

A paragraph had been boxed in black pencil. Beside it, the sender had written only three numbers: 15:10.

No personal name appeared anywhere on the copy. The signature block had been cropped away.

Iris turned to the routing strip.

Unlike the photocopy, it was original. The yellow paper had pressure marks from a stapler and a crescent-shaped tear where it had been removed from a packet. Executive Office appeared in purple transfer ink. Beneath it, Materials Assurance. Then Public Release.

The routing date was the fourteenth.

One day before Batch 743 testing finished.

The backdating had not been invented at the approval desk after a delayed result. A policy route had existed before the final test.

That still did not prove who authored it. It proved the timing decision had moved through an executive channel.

Iris slid both items into separate sleeves.

Her terminal chimed with the first response to the technical notice. Meridian's certification status had changed from verified to pending review.

A second alert followed. Her Directorate access had been restricted to laboratory functions.

Efficient. Not careless enough to revoke her credentials while evidence remained under her authority. Just enough to narrow where she could walk.

She looked at the replacement coupon locked in the clear transport case on the examination table.

The coupon had been extracted from the resealed bay after Petra's certification. Its major fracture features matched the override method, but the edge held a faint disturbance she had not mapped. Under ordinary light, it resembled a pale scratch near the machined shoulder.

Iris switched on the bench magnifier.

At twenty times enlargement, the scratch became a crack. Too short for propagation from the primary load fracture. Its edges had been polished unevenly, leaving metal smears that ran across rather than away from the break.

Post-fracture machining.

Someone had cut or ground the coupon after it failed.

She increased the magnification and adjusted the light until it struck at a low angle. Three fine lines appeared around a central pit, evenly separated. Not random stress branching. Not handling damage.

Each branch sat almost exactly one hundred and twenty degrees from the next.

Iris's thumbnail pressed against the case seam.

She had seen that geometry before, but not in the Kessler core sample and not in the seven compression points already released. The pattern came from a different component, one manufactured under a non-standard cooling rate at an unauthorized machine shop.

A component made after the collapse.

A component whose purpose had never been structural.

Under the magnifier, the micro-crack on the replacement coupon splits into three branches — identical to the mark Iris Kane documented on a component fabricated after the Kessler collapse to destroy evidence.
