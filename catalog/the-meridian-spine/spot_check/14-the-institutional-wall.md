"The policy office has no record of any directive regarding certification timelines," the legal officer says, spreading her hands. "Your internal inquiry is closed."

Iris looked at the denial between them. Six paragraphs, twelve citations, no answer.

The legal officer had chosen a conference room with transparent walls, the Directorate's favorite architecture. Nothing suggested concealment like making every obstruction visible.

"Then reopen it," Iris said.

Celeste Venn, senior counsel for institutional affairs, did not blink. "There is no procedural basis."

"I submitted a routing sheet stamped Executive Office, Materials Assurance, and Public Release. Its routing date precedes completion of Batch 743 testing."

"You submitted a scan."

"The original is secured in evidence storage."

"And the document does not identify the author of a directive."

"It identifies movement through the policy office."

"It indicates routing."

Iris set her thumbnail against the page edge. The paper was dense legal stock, designed to survive handling and discourage it. No warped fibers, no replaced pages, no disturbed staple. A structurally excellent refusal.

"Access to the policy archive would establish what was routed," she said.

Venn folded her hands. "Policy records contain privileged deliberative material."

"So your position is that no directive exists, and the records that might prove one exists are privileged."

"My position is stated in the letter."

"Your letter has better posture than your argument."

A pulse moved once in Venn's jaw. "The Directorate gave you authority over laboratory findings. That authority does not extend to unrestricted executive records."

"It extended far enough when Marsh offered me absolute autonomy."

"Director Marsh cannot waive statutory privilege in conversation."

He probably could. He simply had no reason to do it now.

Iris turned the denial ninety degrees. At the bottom, beside Venn's signature, a blue rubber stamp marked the request CLOSED. The upper-left corner of its border was missing, leaving a blunt white notch in the ink.

She had seen that damaged stamp before.

Alan Torvik's procurement approval for the Aegis jig carried the same broken corner.

Not proof of authorship. Not proof of collusion. A stamp could travel between desks, and bureaucracies loved communal objects almost as much as communal blame. But the denial and the procurement approval had passed through the same administrative chain, one that did not appear on either routing record.

Iris slid the letter into a clear sleeve.

Venn watched the movement. "You do not need to preserve that as evidence."

"I preserve anything that tries too hard to be ordinary."

"This meeting is concluded."

"Who physically processed my request?"

"The legal department."

"A department has no hands."

"The intake details are internal."

Iris rose. "Then your denial denies itself."

Venn remained seated. "Ms. Kane, continued attempts to access restricted systems may result in suspension of your remaining credentials."

There it was. Not a threat. A forecast delivered by someone holding the calendar.

"My credentials are already restricted to laboratory functions."

"And those functions remain important."

"Important enough to keep me visible. Not important enough to let me follow the paper."

Venn's expression settled into professional neutrality. "The Meridian review is proceeding through authorized channels."

"Meridian's status is pending review because physical evidence forced that change."

"The Directorate has acknowledged the review."

Acknowledgment was the cheapest currency in the building. It purchased silence without promising action.

Iris opened the door. "Send me the privilege schedule."

"No privilege schedule has been prepared."

"Then prepare one."

The corridor outside held three security officers in gray Directorate uniforms. None addressed her. All three tracked her progress toward the lift.

Her laboratory credential still opened the lift, but the executive floor had vanished from its panel. Efficient again. Marsh did not smash doors. He changed where they led.

Iris pressed Archives, received a red denial, and selected Procurement instead.

Alan Torvik's office was empty.

His desk had been cleared of personal objects, though a pale rectangle on the wood showed where something had stood until recently. His terminal was gone. Two filing drawers hung open, both bare.

A woman at the adjacent workstation kept her attention fixed on a purchasing matrix.

"Where is Torvik?" Iris asked.

"Off-site."

"Where?"

"I don't know."

"When did he leave?"

The woman's fingers stopped above the keyboard. "You should ask his supervisor."

"His supervisor's office says he is in this building."

"I can't help you."

The answer arrived too quickly, polished by rehearsal or fear. Iris studied the desk again. A ceramic cup sat in the waste bin, split cleanly through its printed Directorate seal. Coffee had dried along the inner wall, dark at the bottom and pale near the rim.

Torvik had not packed carefully. Someone had made him leave quickly, or he had believed speed mattered.

Her restricted terminal access could no longer open personnel movement records. The receptionist downstairs refused to provide visitor logs. Facilities claimed no knowledge of office relocations.

The institution did not need a single locked door. It had a thousand people trained to become one.

Iris left the building through the east service entrance and called the number Torvik had used on two archived procurement forms. It rang four times.

"Yes?"

His voice was thin and wary.

"Alan Torvik."

Silence sharpened at the other end.

"This is Iris Kane."

"I know."

"Your office says you're off-site."

"I am."

"Legal says my inquiry is closed."

"Then it's closed."

He sounded as if he were reading the words from a surface in front of him.

"I need ten minutes," Iris said. "Public place. You choose it."

"No."

"You signed the Aegis requisition."

"I signed hundreds of requisitions."

"This one was classified as a standard consumable even though it purchased a custom jig."

"I don't remember it."

His denial lacked the defensive irritation of a man falsely accused. It carried calculation. Each word placed carefully, not to persuade her but to avoid touching something.

"Your approval bears a rubber stamp with a damaged corner," Iris said. "The same stamp appears on a legal denial issued today."

A breath, quickly suppressed.

"Alan?"

"Stop calling this number."

The line went dead.

Iris stared at the screen. He had refused cooperation, but the breath had been physical evidence of its own weak kind: involuntary, real, useless in a report.

She called again. The number no longer connected.

An hour later, she found him without using a system Marsh controlled.

The procurement forms listed a courier annex where original bids were transferred for storage. Torvik's initials appeared on the weekly receipt schedule every Thursday. It was Thursday.

The annex occupied the ground floor of a concrete records building three blocks from the Directorate. Iris waited across the lobby from the service counter until Torvik emerged through a staff door carrying a flat archive carton.

He was smaller than his employee photograph suggested. His shirt collar sat crooked, and a fresh visitor badge had been fixed over the place where his Directorate credential would normally hang.

He saw her and stopped.

The carton tilted. His fingers tightened beneath it.

"We can speak here," Iris said.

"No, we can't."

"Then put down the box and walk outside."

"I have nothing to say."

His gaze moved past her to the lobby entrance. A man in a dark institutional suit stood near the security gates, reading a wall directory without turning a page.

Iris did not look twice. She did not need to. Torvik already had.

"Did you authorize the jig specifications?" she asked.

"I processed the purchase."

"Who supplied the classification code?"

"I don't recall."

"You remembered enough to block my number."

"Please." The word came out low. "Leave this alone."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting."

His right thumb rubbed repeatedly over the carton seam, scraping a paper fiber loose. Fear advertised itself in small maintenance tasks.

"Did you handle the routing stamp?" Iris asked.

Torvik's eyes snapped to hers.

The man by the directory finally turned a page.

Torvik shifted the carton against his chest. "You need to go."

"Are you being threatened?"

"No."

Too fast.

"Has anyone instructed you not to cooperate with me?"

"No."

Slower, but not steadier.

Iris stepped half a pace nearer, leaving enough distance that he could not claim intimidation. "Your signature is physical. The classification is physical. The stamp impression is physical. Refusing to speak does not erase any of them."

His face tightened. "You think evidence protects people because it protected you once."

The words landed harder than an accusation.

"From what?" she asked.

He glanced toward the entrance again. "I didn't say that."

"You did."

"I processed a form. That is all."

"Who brought it to you?"

Torvik thrust the archive carton onto the service counter. The clerk startled.

"I can't help you," he said. "Don't come to my home. Don't contact anyone connected to me."

"I haven't mentioned your home."

Color drained from his face, as if he had stepped somewhere he could not step back from.

He turned away. The man at the directory moved with him, maintaining a distance too consistent to be casual.

Iris stayed where she was.

Following Torvik would give security a procedural excuse to remove her. Confronting the other man would produce a name, likely false, and a complaint, certainly real. Marsh's strength was not that every exit disappeared. It was that each remaining exit led into a room he had prepared.

Her phone vibrated.

A Directorate media advisory filled the screen.

National Integrity Directorate -- independent certification; Meridian Spine verified clean.

Iris read it twice.

Meridian was not verified clean. Its certification status had been changed to pending review after the partial technical release. The advisory did not state that the status had been restored. It placed two phrases beside each other and trusted the public to build the bridge.

Below the heading, the announcement named Stellan Marsh as speaker at an immediate press conference on institutional integrity.

The room was full by the time Iris arrived.

Journalists packed the fixed rows, camera operators lined the walls, and Directorate staff occupied every seat nearest the podium. Iris's badge admitted her to the building but not the reserved section. She took a standing place at the back.

Onstage, Marsh wore a charcoal suit and the calm expression of a man welcoming scrutiny he had already formatted.

"The National Integrity Directorate was created because public trust cannot depend on assurances alone," he said. "It must depend on standards, independent review, and transparent correction when questions arise."

Transparent correction. Iris almost admired the phrase. It turned obstruction into caution and forced evidence into a demonstration of institutional health.

Marsh continued without notes.

"The Meridian Spine remains subject to our established certification safeguards. A technical question concerning a replacement test coupon has been referred for examination. That examination is ongoing. No responsible authority should convert an unresolved laboratory issue into a conclusion about an entire national project."

He had not denied the tamper mark. Denial created a surface that evidence could strike. Instead, he widened the scale until one damaged coupon looked absurdly small beside the country.

A journalist in the second row raised her hand. "Director Marsh, the released comparison reopens the certification and shows a match between the Meridian coupon and the Kessler override method. Now there are reports that the replacement coupon itself was altered after fracture. Did your office know about that tampering before Meridian was declared clean?"

The room tightened.

Marsh adjusted one cufflink.

Only once.

"The Directorate receives preliminary observations every day," he said. "We do not govern by rumor, and we do not authenticate selective claims through speculation."

"Is the tampering report false?"

"I said it is being examined."

"Was Ms. Kane's access restricted after she submitted it?"

Several heads turned toward the back.

Iris kept her face still. The access restriction existed in the credential system. The question did not reveal the tool-mark or publish proof. It exposed the wall around her, and Marsh would make the wall sound like responsible governance.

"Access permissions are routinely calibrated to the scope of active assignments," he said. "No employee is above procedure, and no employee is punished for verified findings."

Perfectly balanced. He had left room to define every inconvenient finding as unverified.

Another journalist called out, "Will you release the policy office records tied to the certification timeline?"

"Protected deliberations remain protected for good reason. Relevant records will be reviewed through appropriate channels."

"By whom?"

"By the Directorate's independent legal and technical officers."

The same legal department. The same damaged stamp. The same chain, hidden in plain sight because every link carried a respectable title.

Marsh's gaze moved across the room, controlled and unhurried. "This institution will not compromise its standards to satisfy a news cycle. Nor will it retreat from legitimate scrutiny."

A final question rose over the others.

"Director Marsh, can the public still trust the Directorate?"

From the back of the packed press room, Iris Kane watched Stellan Marsh field the question. He smiled.

"The Directorate is built on a foundation of integrity. Any allegation to the contrary will be thoroughly investigated."

He met Iris Kane's eyes for a fraction of a second -- a warning.
