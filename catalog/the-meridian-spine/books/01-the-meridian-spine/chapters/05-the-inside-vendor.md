"You need to stop digging into our own procurement," Nadia Okafor said, her voice low. "The Directorate is the institution that needs to survive, not your private investigation."

Iris Kane did not look up from the note. She read it a third time, memorizing the curve of each typed character.

"You're looking at the wrong supplier. Veridian Trust acquired Aegis after the fact. The real tooling vendor is still inside the Directorate. — A friend."

She slid the paper into a transparency sleeve and sets it inside her evidence box.

"Iris." Nadia's fingers tapped against the doorframe.

"I heard you." Iris closed the box lid. "I'm not investigating procurement for its own sake. I'm tracing how a custom jig entered the certification flow. That path goes through the people who authorize purchase orders inside this building."

Nadia stepped inside and shuts the door. Her voice drops. "If you chase an internal supply clerk, you waste time the real architects will use to vanish. Halveston is the vector. He financed the Spine. He owns Veridian Trust. He has the money and the motive."

"Halveston bought Aegis after the jig was already in the chain. The acquisition was clean. It didn't create the tool. It only bought the company that held the paper. The decision happened before the purchase."

"Then the decision was Petra Lund's. She used the method."

"Petra used the method. She didn't approve her own requisition." Iris pulled up the file on her laptop. "Someone in procurement authorized a sole-source jig purchase with no competitive bid, no inventory serial number, no oversight. That's a procurement officer with override level."

Nadia's expression shifted almost imperceptibly. "Who?"

"Alan Torvik. He approved the requisition for Aegis Fabrication." Iris turned the screen. The scanned form shows Torvik's signature on the approval line. Dated three weeks before the first override.

Nadia read it in silence. Her thumb runs along the edge of the desk before she speaks. "When did you pull this?"

"Twenty minutes ago."

"Did you talk to him?"

"I went to his office. He wasn't there. His assistant said he was in a meeting. I left a message." Iris closed the laptop. "I plan to go back."

Nadia straightened. "Then you have a name on a page. That's not a confession."

"Of course not. But it's a name that doesn't appear in any of the Spine's internal audit reports. Torvik is not listed in the procurement log for special tooling. The requisition was coded as a standard consumable. That's deliberate concealment eight inches below the radar."

"Concealment that could have been done by someone using his access," Nadia said. "His badge, his password. Or a forged digital signature. You don't know it was him."

"I know I have to ask him."

Nadia took a breath. "Do you understand what happens if you pull that thread inside the building? The Director has given you extraordinary access. You've used it to confront a senior materials authority. Now you want to interrogate a procurement officer. The optics are a purge. The media will call it a witch hunt."

"The media will call it an investigation if it produces a conviction."

"Convictions are built on evidence that survives scrutiny. If you accuse Torvik and he's clean, you lose your standing. The Directorate will close ranks. You'll never get near the real source."

Iris stood. Their eyes meet. "You keep pointing me at Halveston. You showed up at Petra's office. Now you want me to drop the internal trace. I will verify Torvik myself — and I will still check the fund."

Nadia's face remained still. "I'm protecting the institution that gives you the authority to investigate at all. The Directorate is not your enemy."

"It's not my ally either." Iris picked up her evidence case. "Neither are you, until I know whose direction you're following."

She walked past Nadia and into the corridor. The door swung shut behind her.

The procurement floor was on the fourth level. Iris took the stairs. The building hummed with the vibration of the testing floors below.

Alan Torvik's cubicle was in a row of identical gray partitions. His desk was clean. A single framed certificate on the wall—completed training in contract compliance. The computer was off. The chair was pushed in.

Iris stood in the opening. An assistant approached from the next cubicle.

"He's still in the all-hands. Should be out by four."

"I'll wait."

The assistant hesitated. "I think he's booked solid after. Maybe you can send him an email."

"I'll wait." Iris sat in the visitor chair beside the desk. She pulled out her phone and reviews the micrograph image—the seven compression points, the drag mark, the asymmetric bite. She memorized the pattern again. If the slide is lost, she still has the digital file. But digital files can be questioned. The slide was the primary.

She looked up at the ceiling. Two hours until four o'clock.

The minutes passed. People glanced at her as they walk past. No one stopped.

At 3:47, a man in a gray suit rounds the corner. He was tall, balding, carrying a laptop case. He saw Iris and slows.

"Ms. Kane?"

"Alan Torvik?"

He nodded, eyes wary. "I got your message. This is about one of my requisitions?"

"Yes." Iris stood. "Aegis Fabrication. A specialized calibration jig requisitioned three months ago."

Torvik's face tightened. "I handled dozens of tooling orders for the Spine. I'd need to see the specific file."

"I have it here." Iris pulled out the printed requisition. "Your signature. AF-J9-4417."

He took the paper, reads it. "Aegis. Yes, I remember. A standard calibration fixture for the new test frame. The supplier was the only one certified for that specific model."

"The requisition doesn't mention a test frame. It says 'custom calibration jig for specialty use.'"

Torvik looked up. "That's the same thing. The frame needed a custom jig to calibrate the sensor alignment."

"I reviewed the purchase records. The jig was never logged into the inventory system. No equipment serial number was assigned. The testing machine had its own serial number, but the jig field on the certification record was left blank."

He handed the paper back. "That's not my department. I processed the purchase order. After that, it's logistics and equipment management. I don't track what happens to the tool after it arrives."

"The purchase order was coded as a consumable under the general supply budget. Not as a capital tool. That classification bypassed the serialization requirement."

Torvik's jaw hardened. He glanced at the assistant, then back at Iris. "The coding was provided by the requester. Petra Lund's office. They sent the category code."

"Petra sent you the code?"

"That's what I said." He crossed his arms. "If there's an issue with the classification, you should take it up with her. I approved what was submitted."

Iris held his gaze. He did not blink. Either he was telling the truth, or he had prepared for this question.

"Thank you. I may need to follow up."

"Fine." Torvik stepped past her into his cubicle and sets his bag down. He did not ask what the investigation is about. He did not ask why she was interested in one jig out of hundreds.

Iris walked away, her mind cataloging the gaps. Torvik's answer was plausible. It was also convenient. The category code came from Petra—and Petra is already exposed. He was deflecting responsibility onto a target she had already burned.

But if he was right, the procurement records alone will not prove him culpable. They would prove the paper path, not the intent.

She reached the stairwell and stops.

Behind her, the corridor hummed. The building was full of people, each one a possible source or a possible wall.

She took the stairs down to the evidence locker.

The basement level was colder. The air smelled of metal and concrete dust. She swiped her badge at the locker room door.

The lock clicked open.

Her evidence box was on the third shelf, label facing out. She pulled it down. The tamper seal was intact. She cut it and lifts the lid.

Inside, the paper evidence was there. The printed procurement records, the tool-mark images, the note.

But the slide tray was missing.

The cardboard sleeve that held it sits at the bottom of the box. She lifted it out. Empty.

She tilted it under the light. A fine white powder catches the glare—fingerprint powder. Someone wore gloves, removed the slide, and didn't wipe the interior.

She photographed the sleeve with her phone. She photographed the empty box from three angles. Then she closed it and returned it to the shelf.

The logbook by the door records every entry. She signed out the log sheet for the last seventy-two hours.

Three names: her own, from earlier today. A security patrol at midnight. And, at 2:07 AM, a signature she does not recognize.

Initials: AT.

Alan Torvik.

She stared at the line. The ink was consistent with the other midnight entries. The time stamp matched the shift schedule.

If Torvik entered the locker at 2:07 AM, he had access to her evidence. And he knew the tray was there because she had logged it in that same morning.

She took a picture of the log sheet.

Then she stood in the middle of the locker room, the empty sleeve in her hand, and the weight of what she had lost settled into her chest.

The micrograph was her only physical proof that the Batch 743 coupon had been manipulated with the same method that collapsed the Kessler tunnel. Without it, her case against Petra Lund rested on her word, the digital image, and the fractured coupon—which could still be challenged as contaminated evidence.

Someone inside the Directorate took the slide.

Someone who knew exactly which box to open, which sleeve to pull, and which surface to wipe.

And the initials in the log point to the same man she just interviewed.

Iris slid the photo of the log into her phone and walked out. The corridor was empty. The lights flickered.

She had Alan Torvik as a suspect for evidence theft. She had no slide. She had no confession.

And every question she asks from this point forward will be answered by people who already knew she was blind in one hand.
