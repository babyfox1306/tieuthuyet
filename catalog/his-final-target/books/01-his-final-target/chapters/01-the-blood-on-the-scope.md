The cold concrete pressed against Sloane Mercer’s cheek, a metallic tang of blood in the air, and then a scream tore from her throat as her eyes focused on the still form of Mara Mercer, lying in a widening pool of crimson.

The sound scraped her vocal cords raw, a jagged noise that didn't belong to the silence of a rooftop at dawn. She scrambled backward, her palms scraping against the gravel, breath hitching in uneven, desperate gasps. The world spun—a violent, nauseating tilt of grey sky and dark tar—and she pressed a hand to her temple, trying to anchor herself to reality.

"Mara?"

Her voice cracked, brittle as dry wood. No answer. Just the wind whistling through the HVAC vents nearby, a low, mournful moan that mocked her panic.

Sloane forced herself to look again. Red hair plastered against pale skin. A jacket Sloane had bought her last Christmas, now ruined. The angle of the body. Wrong. Everything was wrong. She was a firearms investigator; she knew death. She knew the mechanics of it, the physics of velocity and tissue damage. But this didn't compute. She was just here. She was just... where?

Where had she been five minutes ago?

The thought hit her with the force of a recoil. Three months. A gaping, silent void where her life should have been. The last solid memory was a rainy Tuesday in a coffee shop, discussing ballistics striations with a lab tech. Then nothing. Just this. Just blood and a rooftop and the corpse of her little sister.

Panic, cold and sharp, spiked in her chest. She curled her left hand inward, her thumb pressing instinctively against the inside of her wrist. She traced a small, frantic circle against the skin. Once. Twice. Again. It was a compulsive motion, a grounding technique she didn't remember learning but her body knew by heart. It calmed the tremor in her fingers, slowed the frantic rabbit-beat of her heart.

"Focus, Sloane," she whispered harshly. "Facts. Analyze the scene."

She scrambled to her knees, her investigator's brain trying to claw its way past the sister's grief. Exit wound. No exit wound visible from this angle. That meant the round was still inside, or the bullet was a frangible design. Distance from target to shooter? She looked around the roof. Empty. Except for the shadow near the access door.

A man stood there. Still as a gargoyle. He wore a tactical suit, black matte fabric that drank the morning light, and a mask that obscured everything below his nose. A rifle hung at his side, casually, as if he were merely carrying a briefcase and not an instrument of death.

Sloane’s breath caught in her throat. She didn't reach for a weapon—she had none. She didn't run—there was nowhere to go. She straightened her spine, locking her knees, and stared him down.

"Who are you?" she demanded, her voice steadier than she felt. "Did you do this?"

The man didn't speak immediately. He took a step forward, his boots making no sound on the gravel. Then another. He moved with a fluid, predatory grace that screamed professional. He stopped ten feet away, close enough for her to see the cold calculation in his visible eyes, dark and unreadable.

"You know who I am," he said. His voice was a low rumble, devoid of inflection, yet it sent a shiver racing down her spine that had nothing to do with the chill wind.

"I don't," she spat, gesturing wildly toward Mara’s body. "I don't know anything. I don't know how I got here. I don't know..." She trailed off, the horror of the memory loss crashing over her again. "Who are you?"

He tilted his head, studying her with an intensity that felt like a physical touch. He raised a gloved hand to the scope of his rifle, his thumb brushing the focus ring. He turned it exactly one quarter rotation away from clarity, a subtle, almost nervous tic that seemed out of place on such a statue of a man.

"Check your pocket," he said.

Sloane stared at him. "What?"

"Your left jacket pocket. Check it."

She hesitated. Every instinct screamed that this was a trap, that reaching into her clothes would be the last thing she ever did. But the curiosity, the desperate need for a fact to hold onto, won out. Her hand shook as she slipped it into the interior pocket of her leather jacket.

Her fingers brushed paper. Thick, heavy stock. She pulled it out.

It was a single sheet, folded in thirds. She unfolded it with hands that felt numb, her eyes skimming over the typed text. The header was stark: PRIVATE CONTRACT / TERMINATION ORDER.

Target: Mara Mercer.
Method: Executed.
Bearer: Sloane Mercer.

The paper slipped from her fingers, fluttering to the blood-spattered ground. She looked up at the masked man, denial burning hot in her throat. "No. No, that's fake. I would never... I'm a federal investigator. I don't hire contracts. I don't..."

"You do," he said. He sounded almost tired. "You hired me three months ago, Sloane. You paid half upfront. The remainder is due upon completion."

"That's impossible." She backed away, her heel catching on a piece of loose concrete. She stumbled but caught herself, her eyes darting between him and Mara. "She's my sister. I would rather die than hurt her."

"Is that so?" He took another step. Now he was too close. She could smell him—gun oil, cold air, and beneath it, something faintly spicy, like sandalwood and rain. It was a scent that cracked something open in her mind, a door jammed shut, but she couldn't reach the handle.

"Look at the signature," he commanded softly.

Sloane looked down at the paper again. There, at the bottom, was her signature. Sharp, jagged, unmistakably hers. Next to it, printed block letters: L. VEY.

"Lucian Vey," she whispered. The name tasted like ash and honey. Why did that name sound like a warning and a prayer all at once?

"Me," he said. He reached up, his movements slow and deliberate, and unbuckled the strap of his mask. Sloane’s breath hitched. She wanted to look away, to run to her sister, to wake up from this nightmare, but she was frozen. Paralyzed by the gaze of those dark eyes.

He pulled the mask away.

The face beneath was striking, even in the harsh light of dawn. Sharp cheekbones, a jawline that looked cut from granite, a mouth that seemed set in a permanent line of grim determination. There was a small, jagged scar above his left eyebrow, a flaw in the perfection that made him terrifyingly real.

She didn't know him. She was sure she had never seen this man before in her life. But as she looked into his eyes, something inside her wept. It was a visceral reaction, a phantom ache in her chest that had nothing to do with the body cooling on the concrete ten feet away. Her heart skipped a beat, then accelerated, pounding a rhythm she recognized.

"You," she breathed, the word torn from her. "I know you."

"You don't," he said. He looked at her with a strange, haunting sadness, his eyes tracing the lines of her face as if he were trying to memorize her one last time. "You used to. But you made sure of that."

He reached into his own pocket and withdrew a small, silver drive. He held it out to her, but when she reached for it, he pulled it back slightly.

"The contract was specific," he said, his voice dropping an octave, lower, intimate. "You hired me to take her. You hired me to make it look real. And you hired someone else to scrub your mind. You wanted a clean slate, Sloane. You wanted to forget what you were becoming."

"I'm a good person," she insisted, her voice rising in near-hysteria. "I solve crimes. I put people like you away."

"We are exactly the same," he said, stepping closer. He was invading her personal space now, a deliberate provocation. She should have been terrified. She was terrified. But there was something else. A magnetic pull. A heat radiating from him that made her skin prickle. He didn't touch her, but she could feel the phantom pressure of his hand on her waist, the ghost of a touch from a life she couldn't recall.

"Prove it," she challenged, her chin lifting. She tapped her temple, her forensic brain scrambling for leverage. "If I hired you, where's the digital trail? Bank transfers? Dark web comms? It's all traceable. I would know how to hide it, but I also know how to find it."

"You encrypted the trail yourself with a polymorphic key. It's gone. The money is gone. All that's left is the physical agreement and the outcome." He gestured toward Mara with a tilt of his head. "The outcome."

Sloane looked at her sister again. The grief was a physical weight, crushing her lungs. She fell to her knees beside the body, reaching out a trembling hand to check for a pulse. She knew it was futile. She knew the physics of that much blood loss. But she had to try.

Her fingers brushed the cooling skin of Mara's neck. Nothing.

"Why?" The sob broke free, uncontrollable. "Why would I do this?"

"Because you had to," Lucian said from above her. His tone had changed. The professional detachment was cracking, revealing a fissure of raw emotion. "Because the network was closing in. Because you made a choice to save the few by sacrificing the one. You were always ruthless, Sloane. Even when you pretended not to be."

"Shut up!" she screamed, surging to her feet. She launched herself at him, her hands curling into fists, striking his chest. It was like hitting a solid wall of muscle. He didn't flinch, didn't step back. He just stood there and took it, absorbing her rage.

"Hit me," he said quietly, catching her wrists as she swung again. "Go ahead. It won't change the facts. It won't bring her back."

He held her wrists easily, his grip firm but not bruising. His thumbs rested over her pulse points. He stood there, holding her suspended, forcing her to look at him. The contact sent a jolt of electricity through her system, confusing and terrifying. Her body was reacting to him, leaning into his strength, betraying her mind's hatred.

"Let go of me," she snarled, struggling.

"Not until you calm down." He leaned in closer, his face inches from hers. His gaze dropped to her lips, then snapped back to her eyes. The air between them crackled, charged with a tension that was definitely not hatred. It was recognition. It was ownership.

"You erased your memories to protect yourself," he murmured, his breath mingling with hers. "But you forgot one thing, Sloane. You forgot that the body remembers what the mind tries to bury."

"Stop talking in riddles!" She twisted her wrists, trying to break his hold. In her struggle, her left hand twisted in his grip, forcing her palm outward. Her thumb spasmed, tracing that frantic circle against her wrist again, right over his gloved hand.

Lucian went rigid. He stared at her wrist, his eyes narrowing. Then, almost imperceptibly, his grip tightened, his thumb pressing down on that exact spot on her wrist where she had traced the circle.

"What are you doing?" she whispered, suddenly still.

He released her abruptly, taking a step back as if burned. He flexed his hand, looking at his glove, then back at her with an expression she couldn't decipher—fear, anger, and a profound, deep longing.

"Nothing," he said, his voice rough. He turned away from her, walking toward the edge of the roof.

Sloane rubbed her wrists, feeling the lingering warmth of his touch. She watched him, her mind racing. He was lying. He was hiding something. The way he looked at her wrist... it wasn't just a tic. It was a trigger.

She looked down at the ground near her feet. Something glinted in the morning light, catching a stray beam of sun. A spent brass casing. It must have ejected when he fired.

She crouched down, ignoring the ache in her knees, and picked it up. It was still warm. Warmth from the firing pin, warmth from the chamber. She brought it to her nose, expecting the acrid, bitter stench of burnt propellant.

Instead, she smelled something else. Something sweet. Like burnt sugar and almonds.

The scent slammed into her brain, bypassing the logic centers and striking the primitive hindbrain. A phantom ache throbbed in her wrist, sharp and sudden, distinct from the panic. Her fingers twitched, mimicking the motion of reloading, of settling the weapon into a shoulder groove.

She didn't recognize the scent. She didn't recognize the motion. But her body did. And it knew exactly who had taught it to her.

Lucian turned back to her. The wind ruffled his dark hair, exposing the sharp line of his jaw. He looked like a man standing on a precipice, torn between jumping and staying.

"The contract included a contingency," he said, his voice carrying over the wind. He didn't look at Mara. He looked only at Sloane.

Sloane stood up, the casing clutched tight in her fist, the heat searing her palm. "What contingency?"

"You ordered a kill switch," he said. He took a slow breath, his chest expanding. He reached up and touched the scar above his eyebrow, a nervous gesture that seemed entirely too human for a man in his profession.

"You were afraid, Sloane. Afraid that one day, the barrier you built would break. That you would remember the things you did. The things you made me do."

He walked toward her again, but this time there was no threat in his posture. Only a terrible, final resignation. He stopped in front of her, close enough that she could see the gold flecks in his dark eyes.

"If you ever remembered," he said softly, "the contract is void. And the secondary order activates."

Sloane’s heart hammered against her ribs. "What secondary order?"

Lucian reached out, his hand hovering near her face. He didn't touch her, but she could feel the heat radiating from his palm. He looked at her with an intensity that burned, a mixture of love and horror that stole the breath from her lungs.

"You hired me," he said, the words dropping like stones into a deep well. "And you told me to kill you if you ever remembered."
