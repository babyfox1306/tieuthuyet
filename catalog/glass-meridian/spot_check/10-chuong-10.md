"Put that away." The command was a hiss, barely audible over the hiss of the espresso machine, but it carried the weight of a death sentence. I didn't flinch, my finger pressing the faded photograph harder against the scarred table of the sterile, nondescript café, forcing Professor Chen to look at it. The air between us smelled of stale roasted beans and desperation, a fitting perfume for a conspiracy that had already cost too much.

Professor Chen’s gaze darted around the room, checking for eavesdroppers in the empty corners before his eyes landed on the image I had slid across the laminate surface. His face, usually a mask of academic detachment, drained of color so quickly I worried he might slide right off his chair. Then, with a trembling hand, he reached for his glasses, pulling them off and polishing the lenses frantically with a handkerchief, even though they were perfectly clean.

"Tell me what it means," I demanded, my voice low and sharp, cutting through his nervous tic. "You knew my mother. You worked in the same circles. This symbol was in her private journals, hidden behind a panel in her desk. Don't tell me you don't recognize it."

Chen replaced his glasses, his magnified eyes swimming with anxiety. He looked like a man who had just realized he was standing on a landmine. I watched him closely, my mind dissecting his micro-expressions—the bead of sweat forming at his temple, the rhythmic tap of his foot against the linoleum floor. He was terrified. And fear, I had learned, was the most efficient lever for extracting truth.

"It is not a simple matter of recognition, Lin Wei," he whispered, leaning in, his voice trembling. "Some things are buried for a reason. Digging them up... it disturbs things that should remain sleeping. Your mother knew that. Why do you think she never spoke of this?"

"My mother is dying," I countered, the steel in my tone making him flinch. I tapped the photograph again. The image was grainy, a black and white shot of a laboratory from decades ago, but the symbol scrawled on the whiteboard in the background was distinct—a jagged, geometric abstraction that looked like a bird breaking free from a cage, or perhaps, a shackle. "She is forgetting her own life, Professor. If there is a chance this explains why... if there is a chance it leads to a treatment... I don't care about the danger. I need to know."

Chen swallowed hard, his throat bobbing. He reached for his water glass, took a shaky sip, and set it down with a clatter. He looked at the symbol again, his eyes lingering on the sharp angles of the design. I could see the war raging behind his eyes—the loyalty to an old friend versus the primal instinct for self-preservation.

"This..." He pointed a shaking finger at the symbol, refusing to touch the paper. "This is the mark of the Initiative. We didn't call it that in public. In the halls, behind closed doors, it was referred to as Project Phoenix."

"Project Phoenix," I repeated, the name tasting of ash and rebirth. It sounded grand, mythical, but Chen's reaction was anything but heroic. He looked at the symbol as if it were a venomous snake coiled to strike. "What was it? A research group? A clinical trial?"

"It was a mistake," Chen hissed, his voice dropping an octave, the fear spiking again. "It was supposed to be about pushing the boundaries of regenerative medicine. Genetic manipulation. Cellular reconstruction. The goal was... admirable. To eradicate hereditary disease. To perfect the human vessel. But the methods..." He trailed off, wiping his glasses again, the cloth squeaking against the glass in the quiet café. "The methods were... ethically compromised, to put it mildly."

I leaned back, crossing my arms, my mind racing. Ethically compromised. In the world of high-stakes medical research, that phrase could mean anything from falsified consent forms to illegal human experimentation. Given the cryptic nature of my mother’s notes and the formidable shadow of the Aurelian Group hovering over her medical history, I was betting on the latter.

"My mother was a researcher," I said, testing the waters. "Was she part of it?"

Chen hesitated. The silence stretched, thick and suffocating. He looked away, staring at a stain on the ceiling, avoiding my gaze. It was a confirmation, though a silent one.

"She was brilliant," Chen said finally, his voice laced with a sorrow that felt genuine. "The best of us. She believed in the potential for good. But Marcus Thorne... and the others... they saw the data as something else. They saw a product. They saw a legacy." He paused, his eyes snapping back to mine, intense and warning. "Listen to me, Lin Wei. Project Phoenix was shut down over a decade ago. The files were sealed, the participants scattered. Finding this symbol..." He nodded at the photo. "It means you are touching a live wire. The people who funded it, the people who truly benefited from it... they are still out there. They do not like loose ends."

The mention of Marcus Thorne sent a jolt through my system. Adrian’s father. The estranged architect of the Aurelian empire. The connection I had suspected was there, solidifying into a tangible link. My mother’s illness, Adrian’s cold distance, the sudden marriage, the shadowy threats—they were all converging on this single, extinct project.

"Who funded it?" I pressed, ignoring his warning. my journalistic instincts overriding the knot of fear in my stomach. "Was it Aurelian? Was it Thorne Biomedical?"

Chen paled even further, if that were possible. He began to gather his things, his movements frantic and clumsy. "I have said too much. I should never have agreed to meet you. If anyone knows I spoke to you..."

"Professor," I reached across the table, gripping his wrist. His skin was clammy, his pulse racing beneath my fingers. "My mother is fading. Every day she loses a piece of herself. If you know anything else—names, locations, a specific lab—anything that could help me find the truth, you have to tell me."

He looked at my hand on his wrist, then up at my face. For a moment, I saw the man he must have been before the fear set in—a man who cared for my mother, a man of science who valued truth. But the moment shattered as quickly as it had formed. He pulled his wrist away sharply.

"There are no records left," he said, standing up and nearly knocking his chair over. "The fire destroyed everything. That is the official story. Believe that story, Lin Wei. For your own sake." He threw a few crumpled bills on the table, not even waiting for his coffee. "Forget the name. Forget the symbol. Go back to your husband and your safe life. The past is a graveyard, not a playground."

With that, he turned and hurried out of the café, his coat flapping behind him like the wings of a frightened bird. I watched him go, the name 'Project Phoenix' echoing in my mind, a riddle wrapped in an enigma. I hadn't gotten names or locations, but I had gotten a confirmation. The conspiracy was real. It was deep, and it was dangerous. And it centered on a lineage, a "perfected" human vessel.
