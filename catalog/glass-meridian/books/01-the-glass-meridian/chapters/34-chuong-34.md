The sterile glow of the server room cast harsh shadows across Adrian’s face, washing him in pale blue and green. Lin Wei watched from behind, tracking the data streams across three monitors. The hum of cooling fans vibrated through the floor, a mechanical undercurrent to her racing pulse. They had rented this space under a false identity, paid in cryptocurrency, and still she felt the walls closing in.

Adrian’s fingers moved across the keyboard with surgical precision. A dozen windows opened and closed. He was chasing something buried deep in the encrypted partitions they had extracted from the Glass Meridian’s network.

“I found it,” he said, voice flat.

Lin Wei leaned closer. “Show me.”

He highlighted a block of code. “A sub-lease agreement for a research facility in Jakarta. Legitimate on the surface, but the digital signature layer uses a private key that’s been dormant for six years. It matches the shell company pattern we saw earlier, but there’s an extra stamp.” He zoomed in on a watermark sequence. “This only exists in Aurelian’s board-level communications. The key was generated on a server that was decommissioned three years ago. Except it wasn’t decommissioned. It was moved.”

Her pulse quickened. “Marcus Thorne.”

Adrian’s hand paused over the mouse. Then, slowly, he traced his finger across the screen, following the curve of the signature. Almost imperceptibly, his hand trembled.

Lin Wei saw it. The subtle shake that betrayed the composure he fought to maintain. She had seen him face down armed men, walk into explosions, but this—this was different. This was legacy.

“He designed this himself,” Adrian said. “The encryption layer, the key generation. I taught him, once, how to build a secure chain. Showed him the vulnerabilities in standard protocols. He used that knowledge to hide his operations.”

She heard the bitterness in his words. “He used your work.”

“He used everything.” Adrian pulled his hand back and tapped a command. The screen shifted to display a recovered communication log. “This is the real find. A direct message thread between two users. One is tagged as ‘MT’—his personal handle. The other is ‘MD’.”

Lin Wei scanned the lines. The most recent message:

MD: Phase two requires green light. Subject transfers for Ascension prep. Confirm approval.

MT: Approved. Use Site B. Discretion. No trace to Aurelian.

She let out a slow breath. “He’s actively directing the Ascension Program. This isn’t something he set in motion years ago. He’s giving orders now.”

Adrian nodded. “He is the one approving subjects. He is the one ensuring no trace leads back to his company. He is the operational leader of this entire network.”

Lin Wei studied the timestamps. “Six hours ago. Which means there’s a response window open. They’re expecting confirmation.”

Adrian pulled up a network topology map. “We have two options. I can spoof a delayed acknowledgment—buy us a few more days. Or I can trace this relay back to its source. The second option guarantees they’ll detect the intrusion, but we’ll get a physical location.”

She moved around the table to stand beside him. Her hand found his shoulder. The tension in his muscles was rigid, coiled tight as an earthquake waiting to break.

“Which one gets us closer to stopping him?”

Adrian looked at her hand, then into her eyes. The raw vulnerability there made her chest tighten. This was the man who had charged into Hanfield without hesitation, who had pulled her from explosions. Now he was silent, waiting.

She squeezed gently. “We’re in this together. Whatever you decide.”

He held her gaze for a long moment. Then he covered her hand with his—a fleeting pressure, a silent acknowledgment.

“Thank you,” he said.

When he turned back to the keyboard, his hands were steady.

“I’ll initiate the trace,” he said. “I’ll send a ping forcing a handshake. We’ll get the physical IP, but it will trigger a security flag. We’ll have maybe two minutes before they trace back to this server.”

Lin Wei already had a flash drive connected to the secondary port. “That’s enough to copy and destroy logs.”

“Assume a human response. They’ll know someone probed their system within seconds.”

“Then we move faster.” She positioned herself to pull cables from the wall. “Execute.”

Adrian pressed Enter.

The monitor displayed: PING SENT. Then a progress bar: WAITING FOR HANDSHAKE.

Seconds stretched. Lin Wei watched Adrian’s profile, the way his eyes tracked each update. She imagined the weight he carried. The father who had built Aurelian Group into an empire, only to reveal it was a mask for something darker. The father who had groomed Adrian since childhood to either inherit the kingdom or become a pawn. Maybe both.

The screen flashed. HANDSHAKE RECEIVED.

A string of IP addresses unfolded, bouncing through nodes in Dubai, Luxembourg, and settling on a static IP in Geneva.

“Private residence,” Lin Wei said, pulling up a mapping application on her tablet. “Exclusive district. Property registered under a charitable trust.”

“Inner council,” Adrian said. “We have their command center.”

Before she could respond, a new window appeared on the intercepted channel.

MT: Warning. Intrusion detected. Suspect compromised. Prepare emergency shutdown.

Adrian swore under his breath. “He knows. Sixty seconds.”

Lin Wei yanked the flash drive, wrapping it in her fist. Adrian typed furiously, overwriting local logs, activating the remote kill switch.

“Everything on that drive?” he asked.

“The contracts, communications, trace results. All of it.”

Adrian killed the monitors. “We’re gone.”

They slipped into the back corridor. Red exit sign at the far end. No alarms yet. Her heart hammered as they reached the stairwell and descended, footsteps echoing like gunshots.

Then they were out, into the humid Macau night.

Adrian led her through a maze of backstreets until they reached a small noodle shop. They slid into a booth near the back. A woman brought them tea without looking at their faces.

Lin Wei set the flash drive on the table between them. The plastic casing was warm from her grip.

“We have the evidence,” she said. “We can release it now. Interpol, SEC, the press. Marcus Thorne will be exposed.”

Adrian stared at the drive. “And the Glass Meridian? They’ll replace him. We still don’t know the full scope of the inner council. We don’t know their endgame.”

“We know they’re accelerating. The warning said ’emergency shutdown’. They’ll move their timeline up.” She leaned forward. “I caught a reference in the trace logs. They’re planning to activate the next phase within seventy-two hours.”

Adrian’s eyes narrowed. “You’re certain?”

“The message chain mentioned a synchronization window. It aligns with three days from now.”

“Then we don’t have time to infiltrate the inner council from scratch.”

“We don’t.” She held his gaze. “So the choice is now. Expose Thorne publicly, let the authorities handle him, hope the Meridian’s plans are disrupted enough. Or stay in the shadows, keep hunting, but risk that by the time we find the network’s heart, the Ascension Program will have already moved on to its next victims.”

Adrian’s hand hovered over the drive. “If we expose him, we lose the ability to track the network. They’ll go dark, change signatures, disappear into the financial fog. We might never find them again.”

“And if we don’t expose him, we keep him in control while we try to dismantle the system piece by piece. But we might not stop the next phase in time.”

He looked at her. The weight of the decision pressed between them.

Lin Wei reached out and touched his hand. “Whatever we choose, we choose together.”

Adrian turned his hand over, palm up. His fingers brushed hers—a ghost of a caress, barely there, yet loaded with everything unsaid.

“Then let’s make sure it’s right,” he said.

But neither of them moved to pick up the drive.

The tea cooled between them. The noodle shop hummed with quiet conversation. And somewhere in Geneva, Marcus Thorne was already preparing his next move.

The choice would have to be made before dawn.

Dawn was six hours away.
