The café smelled of burnt espresso and sterile air, its fluorescent lights humming a low, steady drone that matched the thrumming in Lin Wei's veins. She had chosen the booth in the far corner, away from the window, away from the only door. Old habit. Journalists learned to sit where no one could approach unseen.

Professor Chen arrived three minutes early, which meant he was nervous. He always arrived exactly on time when calm. Early meant he was already thinking about escape.

He slid into the seat across from her, his overcoat still buttoned, his hands wrapped around the coffee he didn't order yet. "Miss Lin. This is... unexpected. Your mother's condition—"

"Is stable for now." Lin Wei kept her voice low, barely above a whisper, the sound swallowed by the hiss of the espresso machine. "That's not why I asked you here."

She pulled the photograph from her bag. It was old, the edges soft from handling, the image faded to sepia tones. Her mother stood in a white lab coat beside a man whose face had been scratched out with deliberate precision. Behind them, mounted on the wall, was a symbol—a jagged, angular crest, geometric and cold, like something carved by machinery rather than drawn by hand.

Professor Chen's eyes locked onto it. His hand moved automatically to his glasses, lifting them off his face, wiping the lenses with the edge of his tie, even though they were clean. He did it once. Twice. A third time.

"You recognize it," Lin Wei said. Not a question.

"I don't—" He stopped, cleared his throat. "Where did you get this?"

"My mother's journal. The one she kept locked in her study for twenty years." Lin Wei pushed the photograph closer. "She never showed it to anyone. Not even me. I found it after the last round of treatment, hidden behind a loose panel in her bookcase."

The professor's hand trembled as he reached for the photograph. He held it up, his thumb grazing the edge of the symbol, then set it down as if it had burned him. "Miss Lin, I strongly advise you to—"

"To forget I ever saw it? To burn the journal and move on?" Lin Wei's voice remained low, but steel crept into its edges. "My mother is dying, Professor. The doctors can't explain why her cells are degrading the way they are. They say it's 'idiopathic.' But you and I both know that's medical code for 'we don't want to know.'"

His glasses came off again. Wipe. Wipe. Replace.

"Please," she said, softening. "I'm not asking for classified documents. I'm asking for the truth about a symbol that appears in my mother's most private writings, linked to her before I was born. If there's something in her past that can help me understand her present—help me save her—I need to know."

The professor glanced around the café. The barista was absorbed in a mobile game. The only other customer, a middle-aged man in a raincoat, was reading a newspaper at the counter, his face hidden behind the pages.

Professor Chen leaned forward, his voice dropping to a whisper that barely navigated the space between them. "Project Phoenix."

The words hit Lin Wei like a cold wave. She had suspected, had built theories, but hearing them spoken aloud—confirmed by someone who had been there—made it real.

"What is it?" she asked.

"A mistake." He wiped his glasses again. "A massive, catastrophic mistake disguised as scientific advancement. Late nineties, early two-thousands. A joint initiative between several institutions and a private funding partner." He paused, swallowing. "Aurelian Group, among others."

Lin Wei kept her face neutral, but her pulse quickened. Adrian Vale's company. His father's legacy.

"The premise was ambitious," Professor Chen continued, his voice thick with old regret. "Genetic optimization. Cellular reconstruction. In theory, they aimed to correct hereditary defects, enhance biological resilience against disease and aging. In practice..."

He stopped, staring at the photograph as if it might come to life.

"In practice," Lin Wei prompted.

"They cut corners. Used undocumented compounds. Human test subjects who either couldn't consent or were pressured into signing waivers they didn't understand." His hand moved to his glasses again, but this time he didn't wipe them. He simply held them, his knuckles white. "I was young. I was a research assistant. I didn't know the full scope until it was too late. By the time the project was officially terminated, records were sealed, participants were scattered, and the whole thing was buried under legal protections and non-disclosure agreements."

"Terminated?" Lin Wei tilted her head. "Are you certain?"

Professor Chen's eyes met hers, and she saw fear there—genuine, bone-deep fear. "Nothing about Project Phoenix is certain. That's the point."

Lin Wei tapped the photograph. "This symbol. It was their logo?"

"They called it the Meridian Crest. Meant to represent the bridge between human limitation and human potential." He let out a bitter laugh. "More like a brand on cattle."

The Meridian. Lin Wei filed the word away, matching it to the fragment she had decrypted: ...the Glass... safeguards... Project Phoenix stability... Could "Glass Meridian" be the same? She didn't push. Not yet.

"The project was shut down after a scandal," Professor Chen said. "Irregularities in data reporting. Deaths that were classified as 'adverse events' but never properly investigated. The lead researcher—your mother's supervisor—disappeared. The funding dried up. Everyone scattered."

"Everyone except my mother."

He nodded slowly. "Lin Mei was the exception. She refused to sign the non-disclosure. She wanted to go public. I don't know what happened next—I left the institution shortly after. But I heard rumors. She kept copies. She kept the evidence."

Lin Wei's mind raced. That's why her mother was targeted. That's why she was silenced. Not because she was a victim of the experiments, but because she was a witness.

"Why are you telling me this now?" she asked quietly. "You could have kept your silence."

Professor Chen's fingers twitched toward his glasses, but he stopped himself. "Because I saw the look in your eyes when you mentioned your mother's condition. I see the same look in the mirror every morning." He leaned back, his face gaunt under the harsh lighting. "But understand this, Miss Lin: Project Phoenix is not a rabbit hole. It's a trap. The people behind it—they don't just hide their secrets. They eliminate anyone who tries to uncover them."

"I've already been attacked," Lin Wei said flatly. "In an alley near an abandoned research facility. Someone wanted me dead."

His face paled. "Then you know. You're already in the crosshairs."

"I need more than warnings, Professor. I need direction."

He hesitated. His hand went to his pocket, retrieving a pen. He grabbed a napkin from the dispenser and scribbled something quickly, his handwriting small and cramped.

He slid the napkin across the table. On it were four characters: C004.

"What is this?" Lin Wei asked.

"A fragment from the original data logs. One of the few pieces that survived the purge. I kept it—call it a souvenir of my guilt." He lowered his head. "I don't know what it means. But it's the only physical trace I have that links to the deepest layer of Project Phoenix. The part they burned most thoroughly."

Lin Wei stared at the letters and numbers. A designation. A code. Something that might unlock the next door.

"Was my mother's name in the logs?" she asked.

Professor Chen shook his head. "Not that I recall. But your mother was careful. She used aliases. Multiple. If she was ever recorded in Project Phoenix records, it would be under a different identifier." He paused, something flickering in his eyes. "But there was a test subject, a child, who was registered under a similar code. C00 series. They were the special cases. The successes."

"A child subject?"

"Born into the program. Designed, not born, in the conventional sense. Genetic optimization from conception." His voice cracked. "They called them the 'New Horizon' cohort. If any of them survived—if they grew up—they would be walking, breathing proof of what Project Phoenix achieved. And also proof of its crimes."

Lin Wei's hand closed around the napkin. C004. A child. A designed human. If such a person existed, they might hold the key to everything.

Professor Chen stood, leaving his untouched coffee. "I've said too much. If anyone asks, this conversation never happened." He buttoned his overcoat, his hands still trembling. "For your own sake, Miss Lin—and for your mother's—let this die. Some secrets don't deserve to be unearthed."

He walked away without looking back.

Lin Wei remained seated, the napkin crumpled in her palm. The name Project Phoenix echoed through her skull, each repetition carving deeper grooves of certainty and dread. Her mother had been there. She had seen the horror. And someone had made sure she paid for it with her health, her sanity, her life.

She slipped the napkin into her inner pocket and stood, leaving a bill on the table. The café door chimed as she stepped out into the damp evening air. Streetlights flickered to life, casting long shadows across the pavement.

She turned toward the parking lot, her mind still churning through the professor's words. C004. A child. Born into the program.

A flicker of movement caught her eye.

She stopped.

Down the narrow alley that ran alongside the café, a figure stood half-obscured by shadow. Tall. Still. Watching.

Adrian Vale.

Their eyes met across the distance. He didn't move, didn't approach, didn't call out. He simply stood there, a reminder, a sentinel, a warning. His jaw tightened almost imperceptibly, a tell she had begun to recognize.

He had been there the entire time.

Lin Wei's pulse hammered. Her investigation was not a private crusade—it was a monitored, observed, cataloged event. And Adrian Vale was its silent curator.

She turned away, forcing her legs to carry her toward the car. But her hand, still in her pocket, closed around the napkin.

C004.

A new thread. A new name. A new piece of a puzzle that was growing larger, darker, and far more personal than she had ever imagined.

The game was not just being played.

She was being played.
