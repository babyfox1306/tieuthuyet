The muted clink of crystal against crystal punctuated conversations conducted in murmurs so practiced they sounded rehearsed. Polite smiles revealed nothing; calculating eyes revealed everything. Lin Wei adjusted the thin strap of her borrowed evening gown—charcoal silk, off-the-shoulder, elegant enough to pass inspection, simple enough to disappear into the crowd—and reminded herself that she was not here to be seen. She was here to listen.

The invitation had arrived like a gift from the universe: a press credential for the Apex Capital Forum, a private salon tucked into the mezzanine of the Marina Bay Sands, where Asia's wealthiest financiers, biotech moguls, and a suspiciously diverse selection of academics mingled beneath chandeliers that cost more than her annual salary. The official program promised discussions on "innovative investment frameworks" and "next-generation healthcare equities." Lin Wei had read between those lines with the fluency of someone who had spent a decade decoding corporate doublespeak.

She accepted a flute of sparkling water from a passing server—not champagne; she needed a clear head—and positioned herself near a cluster of men in bespoke suits who had clustered around an oval table near the floor-to-ceiling windows. The view of the bay was spectacular. None of them were looking at it.

"The quarterly rebalancing is already baked in," one of them was saying, his accent a careful blend of London and Singapore. "What's interesting is the upstream positioning. If the bio-equity curve normalizes by Q3, we're looking at a seventeen-point spread on the back-end instruments."

Lin Wei's pen, hidden in the folds of her clutch, moved in tiny, invisible strokes against a folded receipt.

Bio-equity. Curve normalization. Back-end instruments.

The language was clinical, detached—textbook investment jargon on the surface. But she had heard Adrian Vale use that same tone when discussing Project Phoenix. Clean words concealing contaminated contents.

A woman with a severe chignon and a sapphire pendant that caught the light leaned into the group. "The safeguards are holding," she said, her voice pitched low but piercing. "But the Phoenix side of the ledger remains... volatile. The board wants stability before the next calibration cycle."

Lin Wei's heart stuttered. She kept her expression perfectly neutral—a skill honed through years of interviewing fraudsters—and lifted her water glass to her lips.

Phoenix side of the ledger. Safeguards. Calibration cycle.

The words landed like ice water down her spine. These people were talking about Project Phoenix the way commodity traders talked about oil futures. As if it were a market instrument. As if lives were a tradeable asset on a spreadsheet.

She excused herself from earshot and drifted toward the dessert station, where a smaller, more intimate circle had formed around a man whose silver hair and expensive spectacles reminded her uncomfortably of Professor Chen. He was speaking to a younger woman in Mandarin, and Lin Wei slowed her pace just enough to catch fragments.

"...the cellular restructuring protocols are ahead of schedule," the silver-haired man was saying. "But the funding mechanism requires a fresh injection of capital through the standard vehicle. The Meridian's preferred route is—"

He caught himself, glancing around with the practiced caution of someone who had learned, through bitter experience, that walls had ears. His gaze swept over Lin Wei without interest. Just another woman in an evening gown, wandering toward the macarons.

Lin Wei smiled politely, selected a raspberry macaron she had no intention of eating, and moved on.

The Meridian's preferred route.

There it was again. The Glass Meridian, threaded through conversations like a ghost in the machine, referenced with the casual familiarity of people who used it as a tool rather than a conspiracy. They weren't afraid of it. They used it.

She found a quiet alcove near a marble pillar and let herself breathe for three seconds. Her mind was already cataloguing, cross-referencing, building a picture from fragments that shouldn't fit together but somehow did. Financial instruments. Bio-equity. Calibration cycles. The Meridian. And underneath it all, her mother's name—conspicuously absent from every document she had reviewed, yet haunting every conversation she overheard.

A movement at the far end of the mezzanine drew her eye.

Adrian Vale stood beneath a archway draped in white orchids, a tumbler of amber liquid in one hand, his posture relaxed in a way that was almost too convincing. He wore a black suit with subtle charcoal pinstripes, and he was watching the room the way a chess master watches a board—not moving a piece, just absorbing the positions of every player.

He was not supposed to be here. She had not told him about the forum. She had made certain of it, clearing her browser history and routing her invitation confirmation through a burner email account. Yet here he stood, thirty feet away, looking like he owned the building and the city and everyone in it.

Their eyes met. For exactly one heartbeat, something electric arced across the distance—recognition, challenge, and beneath it, a thread of something she refused to name. Then his gaze slid away, as if she were no one of consequence, and he turned to speak to a woman in a crimson dress whose diamond necklace caught the light in a way that seemed almost deliberate.

Lin Wei felt the familiar heat of irritation spark in her chest. Of course he would show up. Of course he would pretend he hadn't noticed me. Controlling the narrative, as always.

But beneath the irritation, a smaller, more dangerous thought surfaced: He came here. He didn't have to. He could have let me walk into this alone.

She crushed that thought the way she crushed a fact into a headline—ruthlessly, and without sentiment.

A burst of laughter from the main cluster drew her attention back. A heavyset man in a silver tie was holding forth, his voice carrying just enough to reach her alcove.

"...the beauty of the bio-equity model is its self-correcting nature," he was saying, his English tinged with a Hong Kong inflection. "You don't need regulatory oversight when the market itself enforces compliance. The Glass framework handles the equilibrium. All we need is patience and capital."

"The recalibration is the delicate part," another man countered, his tone serious. "If the Phoenix portfolio destabilizes before the next cycle, the spillover could contaminate the entire Meridian structure. Three years of positioning, gone."

Lin Wei's grip tightened on her water glass. The condensation felt icy against her fingers. She scribbled furiously on her receipt: bio-equity model / self-correcting market / Glass framework handles equilibrium / Phoenix portfolio / recalibration cycle / Meridian structure.

C005. She didn't know yet what it meant—not fully—but the shape of it was emerging. The Glass Meridian wasn't just a research initiative or a shadowy organization. It was a financial architecture. A system designed to treat human biology as a tradeable commodity, regulated not by laws but by market forces, with Project Phoenix as its most volatile holding.

And these people—these smiling, champagne-sipping predators—were its shareholders.

She was so focused on the notes in her clutch that she almost missed the woman in the crimson dress moving toward her. The woman with the diamond necklace. Up close, she was striking in an almost aggressive way—high cheekbones, dark eyes, a smile that didn't reach them.

"Lovely gown," the woman said in a voice as smooth as polished steel. "I don't believe we've met."

Lin Wei's journalist instincts screamed a warning, but her cover was solid. "Liu Yan," she said, offering the name from her press credential. "Financial Times Asia desk."

"Alexandra Voss," the woman replied, extending a hand decorated with a ring set with a stone that was almost certainly a blood diamond. "How are you finding the forum?"

"Educational," Lin Wei said carefully. "The panel on healthcare equities was particularly... illuminating."

Alexandra's smile sharpened by a single, calculated degree. "Healthcare equities are the future, Miss Liu. The intersection of biology and investment is where fortunes are made. Surely your paper understands that?"

They know, Lin Wei thought, a cold finger tracing her spine. They know I'm not who I say I am, or they're testing me, or—

"I should let you circulate," Alexandra said, already stepping back, her heels clicking against the marble with the precision of a retreating blade. "Do enjoy the evening. And do be careful with the macarons. The raspberry ones are to die for."

The last three words hung in the air like a veiled threat, or perhaps a confirmation, or perhaps nothing at all—just the casual cruelty of the idle rich. Lin Wei watched Alexandra return to Adrian Vale's orbit, and something in her chest tightened with an emotion she refused to call fear.

She needed air. She needed to process. She needed to get out before the walls closed in.

She slipped through a side door onto a service corridor, the noise of the salon fading to a muffled hum behind her. Her heels echoed against concrete as she navigated the narrow space, her mind still racing through the fragments she'd collected. Bio-equity. Phoenix portfolio. Meridian structure. The Glass framework.

A dead end. She checked her phone—no signal—and turned back toward the main corridor.

A security guard stood at the junction, arms crossed, watching her with an expression that was not quite suspicious but not quite neutral either.

"Service elevator is that way," he said, pointing. "No public access beyond this point."

Lin Wei nodded, smiled, and followed his direction, her heart hammering a steady, controlled rhythm against her ribs. Calm. Collected. You've been in worse spots. You've interviewed people who wanted you dead.

She told herself that as she stepped out of the service elevator into the basement parking garage, the cool air hitting her exposed shoulders like a splash of cold water. The garage was nearly empty—a few luxury sedans, a gleaming black Mercedes, a silver Aston Martin.

And parked in the far corner, in a spot that afforded a clear view of the garage exit, sat a matte-black Rolls-Royce Cullinan.

Adrian Vale's car.

Lin Wei stopped dead. Her first instinct was to run—back into the elevator, up the stairs, anywhere but here. But running was an admission of guilt, and she had done nothing wrong. Not tonight. Not yet.

She walked toward the exit with measured steps, her chin high, her pulse a drumbeat of controlled fury. The night air of Singapore hit her face as she stepped onto the street, warm and thick with humidity and the salt edge of the bay. The Marina was alive with light—skyscrapers ablaze, the Supertree Grove glowing like something from a fever dream—but the street itself was quiet.

And there, parked beneath a banyan tree with its engine off and its windows tinted to opacity, was the Rolls-Royce.

Lin Wei stared at it. From this angle, she couldn't see inside. Couldn't tell if he was watching her, or if he had already left, or if the car was simply a coincidence—a luxury vehicle parked on a public street in a city full of them.

But she knew. She knew.

The phrase from the salon echoed in her mind, a mantra of commercialized horror: the bio-equity model is its self-correcting nature. The market itself enforces compliance. The Glass framework handles the equilibrium.

She looked at the car. The car said nothing.

A guardian or a captor. The line between them thinner than the silk of her borrowed gown, thinner than the margin of safety she had mistaken for trust.

Somewhere behind her, inside that gleaming fortress of polite smiles and poisoned compliments, the architects of her mother's destruction were toasting to a future she was only beginning to understand.

And somewhere in that black Rolls-Royce, Adrian Vale was watching.

Lin Wei walked toward the light, and the shadow, and the choice she wasn't ready to make.
