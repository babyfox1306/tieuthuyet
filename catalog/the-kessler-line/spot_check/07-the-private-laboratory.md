The private laboratory was housed in a former textile mill, the entrance marked by a single brass plaque. Iris Kane carried the core sample inside like a fuse.

The reception area smelled of solvent and old brick. A woman with gray-streaked hair sat behind the desk, her hands resting on a worn brass paperweight shaped like a tension-test jaw. The bite marks on the metal had been worn smooth from decades of handling.

Iris set her bag on the counter. While waiting, her thumb implicitly found the sharp edge of the wooden counter, running her nail along the seam to feel for any split or shift in the joinery. It was a habit she couldn't shake—structural integrity was measured in micro-fractions, and every surface had a breaking point.

"I called earlier," she said, pulling her hand back. "I need to log a sample for analysis."

The woman slid a clipboard across the desk. "Chain-of-custody form. Fill out the sample number, source location, and your credentials."

Iris filled it in capital letters, leaving no room for ambiguity. She listed the coordinates as station depth Kessler eastbound, the stratum boundary, the direction of the fracture line. When she reached the required signatures, she read each line before signing. The form obligated the lab to store the sample under lock and to notify her of any access by third parties.

The woman watched without hurry. Then she looked up from the form and said, "Someone from your commission called earlier. They asked when you'd arrive and what equipment you'd need."

Iris's pen stopped. She lifted her eyes.

"A man," the technician added. "He said he was from Mr. Vance's office."

Iris considered the possibility that Theo Vance had checked on her as a professional courtesy. Routine calls existed in every organization. But this lab was her private choice, not a commission-approved vendor. She had mentioned nothing to Vance beyond the standard updates on the Marek paperwork.

"Did you tell him?" Iris asked.

The technician shrugged. "I said what you'd requested. Matrix gas analyzer and thin-section mount. Standard."

Iris kept her face still. Her right hand rested on the clipboard, her thumbnail instinctively pressing against the plastic edge, tracing the molded seam to look for structural flaws as she processed the breach. "Please note that all communication about this sample goes through me directly. No exceptions."

"I'll put it in the file."

The technician stamped the form and handed her a copy. She took the core sample from Iris's bag, handling it with the same caution Iris had used, and carried it into a back room. Iris followed as far as the door. Inside, shelving units ran floor to ceiling, each labeled with grid coordinates. A metal vault stood against the far wall.

The technician spun the lock cylinder, placed the sample inside on a padded shelf, and closed the door. The bolts slid into place with a solid clunk.

"You'll get a confirmation code when the preliminary scan starts," the technician said. "About five business days."

"That's acceptable."

Iris took the chain-of-custody copy and folded it into an inner pocket of her jacket. She did not ask about the caller again, but the question stayed in her throat.

She stepped out of the mill into the late afternoon light. The air smelled of river water and diesel. A black sedan was parked across the street, two silhouettes visible through the windshield. The engine was running.

She did not look at it directly. She walked to her own car, keys already in her hand, and got in without hesitation. Through the rearview mirror, she caught the sedan's license plate and typed it into her phone as she pulled away.

She drove east for seven blocks, then made a hard left, then another left at the next intersection. The sedan stayed three car lengths behind, matching every turn. She took a longer loop through an industrial zone, watching the distance between them shrink and stretch like a measured interval.

At a red light, she opened the license plate in a public registry query. The search returned a government pool vehicle registered to the secretariat.

The secretariat. The same office that vetted every finding she submitted. The same office whose stamp appeared on the delivery contract she had found in the forensic archive.

She drove for ten more minutes until the sedan peeled off at a roundabout, likely satisfied that she was heading toward her office. But she did not go to the office. She turned into a parking garage near the river, took a spot on the third level, and sat with the engine off.

She unlocked her phone and opened a PDF that she had saved from the forensic archive weeks ago: the procurement summary for the Vantor Group's liner supply contract. It had survived the redaction team's scissors. She zoomed in on the approval chain.

A procurement officer's signature. A date stamp. Then the secretariat's rubber stamp—a circular impression with no individual name, no position, no department code. An authorization code that did not correspond to any existing review process.

The chain was meant to stop there.

Iris enlarged the image and studied the stamp. The ink was slightly uneven, as though the pad had been dry at the center. She traced the code with her finger on the screen. Fourteen alphanumeric characters. A format that matched nothing in the published secretariat taxonomy.

Whoever had authorized the fast-track had used the secretariat as a blind. The commission's own gatekeeping body was the gap in the record. She could not see the name behind it, but she knew the path now. The secretariat was a wall between her and the person who had signed off on the contract—and that wall had been built deliberately.

She thought about the technician's words. A man from Mr. Vance's office. Vance worked inside the commission. He had access to the secretariat. He also knew about the Marek investigation before anyone else. But he had been helpful, supportive even. He had fed her the signature discrepancy that broke Marek open.

That was the problem. A helpful person inside a broken system was either a genuine ally or the one who knew exactly how to steer her.

She copied the license plate from the sedan into a notes file. Then she dialed the lab number.

The technician answered.

"Iris Kane," she said. "Earlier, you mentioned the caller who asked about my appointment. Did he give a name?"

A pause. The technician's voice came back flat. "They said they were from Mr. Vance's office."

"Is that all?"

"That's all. No name, no extension. Just the office."

Iris thanked her and ended the call.

She sat in the parked car, the dark screen of her phone reflecting her own face. Her left hand gripped the steering wheel, her thumbnail digging into the leather stitching, tracing the groove mechanically as if diagnosing a crack in a concrete pillar. The call had not been a mistake. It had been a marker. Someone inside the commission wanted to know where she was, what she was doing, which evidence she had touched.

She had just handed them a very clear answer.

She looked at the folded chain-of-custody form in her jacket pocket. The sample was locked in a vault with her name on it. The preliminary scan would take five days. That gave her five days to find the person behind the secretariat before they found her.

She started the engine and pulled out of the garage. The sedan was gone, but the plate was saved. She would run it through the internal registry when she got home, trace the sign-out log, find out who had authorized the vehicle that morning.

The commissioner's name was on every document she had touched, but her fingers had never brushed the surface of the real chain of command. The secretariat was the knot. And knots had a breaking point.

She drove toward the river, toward the part of the city where the mill sat quiet, and wondered whether the technician was already on the phone to Mr. Vance's office, reporting that the sample had arrived and that the investigator had asked questions she should not have asked.
