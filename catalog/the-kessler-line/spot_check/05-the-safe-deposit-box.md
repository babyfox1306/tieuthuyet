The warrant was signed at 3:17 p.m. By 4:00, Iris Kane was standing at the bank counter, watching the manager's face change.

Theo Vance was the only other person on the third floor of the commission building when she left the judge's chambers. He stood by his desk, slipping a folder into his bag. His phone buzzed. He glanced at the screen and his expression flickered. It was the micro-flicker of someone who had just received a message they did not want to read in company.

"Everything okay?" she asked.

"Just Operations. Server maintenance tomorrow."

The screen was black before he pocketed it. She had seen him respond to emails from the secretariat. He usually typed a reply. This time, he just looked and hid.

"I got the warrant," she said.

"For the bank?"

"Marek's safe deposit box. I'll let you know what I find."

He nodded. He did not ask to come. He did not offer to accompany her. She filed it.

The hallway leading to Commissioner Renn's office was empty. The door was open. He was inside, coat on, briefcase on the desk, ready to leave for the weekend. He saw her standing in the threshold and gestured her in.

"Iris. You're still here."

"I'm going to the bank. The Marek warrant came through."

Renn's hand paused over his briefcase latch. "I saw the docket. I'd advise against executing it today."

"The finding was published. The case isn't closed."

"It is for the public. Marek's fraud killed sixty-three people. Seismic microfracture compounded by dispatch error. The narrative is clean. Don't muddy it by chasing a ghost through his personal accounts."

"If he was paid to forge the stamp, the payment is evidence of a broader conspiracy."

"It is evidence of a dead man's greed. The families want answers, not a treasure hunt. The commission has given them an answer. Let it stand."

"You're telling me to stop investigating."

"I'm telling you to be strategic. The moment a magistrate's name goes on a warrant, the media starts asking why the inquiry is digging into a dead man's finances. It looks like an obsession. It looks like you don't trust your own finding."

"I don't."

Renn set both hands flat on the desk. The wood was polished. The surface caught the grey light from the window. "Then find something concrete. The core sample is in your drawer. The telemetry logs are in the archive. Use them. Don't let a burner phone call pull you off the trail."

"The box was emptied today. Someone in this building made a call."

"You don't know that."

"I know the log shows an authorised visit at 11:45 this morning. I know the person who signed in is blacked out. I know the ink is still wet."

Renn straightened. His expression did not change, but something shifted behind his eyes. A recalculation. "Then someone in the secretariat is protecting their own interests. That is not the same as the commission protecting a conspiracy. You need to separate the two."

"The compartment emptied the box. Emptying the box destroyed evidence. That is obstruction."

"It is tampering. It is not a confession. Find the person who signed the authorisation, and you will find your rogue actor. But do not assume the entire commission is compromised because one person moved faster than you."

He picked up his briefcase. The meeting was over.

Adrienne Sol was waiting in the lobby. The press conference had ended fifteen minutes ago. The cameras were gone, the cables coiled, the microphones packed away. She stood by the grand staircase in a charcoal suit and heels that added four inches to her height. Her posture was immaculate. Her smile was polished.

"Ms. Kane. I was hoping to catch you."

"Ms. Sol."

"We were disappointed the inquiry didn't loop us into the Marek finding before it went to the families. The wording made Vantor look as though we harboured a criminal intentionally."

"The wording was forensic. Your company hired a man who forged a safety certification. That is a fact, not a perception."

Sol's smile was a blade wrapped in silk. "The board has asked me to extend our full cooperation for Phase Two. Whatever you need. Names, documents, access to the tunnel site. We are an open book."

"I need the inspection rosters for the two years preceding the collapse. Every site visit, every sign-off, every signature."

"Human Resources will have a comprehensive list by end of business Wednesday."

"I need it by Monday morning."

Sol's smile tightened at the edges. "We have processes, Ms. Kane. Records must be collated. Discrepancies checked. We cannot throw files at you and hope they are accurate."

"Your processes missed a forged certification stamp. I am not interested in your process. I am interested in your data."

Sol stepped closer. The lobby was empty. The receptionist had disappeared into the back office.

"You are making enemies, Ms. Kane. Inside the commission and outside it. That is a dangerous position for a technical investigator whose contract is renewed quarterly."

"Is that a threat?"

"It is friendly advice from one professional to another. Dov Marek was a dead man before you ever heard his name. The company has accepted his guilt. The commission has published it. You have won. Take your victory and move on."

"Marek's safe deposit box was emptied three hours before I executed my warrant."

Sol's expression did not flicker. "Then someone in the commission is trying to protect something. You should find out who."

"I intend to."

Sol walked away. Her heels clicked on the marble floor, steady as a metronome.

The bank was three blocks away. The air outside was cold. The sun was low, casting long shadows across the pavement. The foyer clock was two minutes slow. Iris noted it because the log timestamp for the earlier visit was exactly 10:30 — and the clock would have shown 10:28.

Manager Ellis met her at the counter. He wore a brass nameplate and a tie that was knotted too tight. He looked at the warrant and blinked.

"This is for Mr. Marek's box."

"Yes."

"He's deceased."

"The warrant is still valid."

Ellis hesitated. He picked up the phone, then put it down. He led her through the security door and down the stairs into the vault. The air was cool and still. The boxes lined the wall in polished brass, silent and indifferent.

Box 4121. He inserted the master key. The lock turned. The box slid out.

It weighed nothing.

Iris took it to the small viewing room. She lifted the lid.

Red velvet. A single strand of hair. The faint scent of disturbed metal.

Empty.

She looked at Ellis. His face was the colour of the paper in her hand.

"This box was accessed today."

"I don't — "

"Show me the log."

The log was on the counter in the lobby. The entry for 11:45 a.m. showed the box number, the time, and a signature line. The visitor's name had been blacked out with a thick marker. Iris touched the ink. It was still tacky.

"Who signed for this?"

"I can't disclose it. The visit was authorised by the commission's confidentiality protocol."

"That protocol does not override a court warrant."

"The visitor presented a signed authorisation from the commission secretariat. I followed procedure."

"You followed a procedure that emptied the box before I could execute my warrant."

"I followed the protocol."

Iris pressed her thumbnail against the edge of the logbook. The surface was smooth. The surface always lied.

"I need a copy of this log."

"You can have the entry. The rest of the log is bank property."

"The warrant gives me access to all records pertaining to the box."

"The warrant gives you access to the box. The log is a bank record."

"It is a record of access to the box. It is covered."

Ellis looked pale. "I'll need to call our legal counsel."

Iris Kane pressed the manager on the redacted name. "I can't disclose it," he said. "The visit was authorised by the commission's confidentiality protocol." She looked at the log. The ink was still wet.
