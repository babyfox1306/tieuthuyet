At 6:01 PM, Iris Kane dialed the number. A voice answered on the first ring. “Alan Watters. You have twenty minutes.”

She ended the call and left her office without logging out of her terminal. No jacket, no bag—just her phone and the small notepad she kept in her coat pocket. The elevator doors closed behind her at 6:03.

The park was three blocks east of the commission building. She had chosen it because the benches faced the fountain, giving her a clear sightline to every approach. A man in a gray coat sat on the second bench from the left, reading a folded newspaper. She sat down next to him, leaving a foot of space.

He did not look at her. “You are not supposed to have my number.”

“I found it through the procurement archive index,” she said. “Your name came up on three vendor appeals between 2019 and 2021. Then it disappeared.”

“I disappeared on purpose.” He turned a page of the newspaper. Under his thumb she could see the corner of a manila envelope pressed between the pages. “You have been asking about the Kessler certification chain in a way that makes people nervous. Specifically procurement-adjacent people.”

“I have not asked anyone in procurement. I have barely asked anyone at all.”

“You pulled a file from Records that should have stayed buried. You talked to a former site inspector who died the same week.” His voice was flat, recited. “And you brought a core sample to an independent lab. That one got attention.”

Iris kept her face still. “Then you know why I need what you brought.”

He slid the envelope free and held it between them, still concealed by the newspaper’s edge. “This is a copy of the fast‑track approval for Vantor’s Kessler contract. Original is sealed in the procurement archive under a restriction level that requires a ministerial signature to open. This copy was created before the restriction was applied.”

She took the envelope. It was thin, unmarked. “How did you get it?”

“I processed the original. You will see my initials on the routing slip. I kept a photocopy because I had a bad feeling about the handwriting.” He folded the newspaper and stood. “I have never seen you. You never saw me. If anyone asks, you found that document in a trash bin behind the commission building.”

He walked toward the east gate without looking back. She waited until he was out of sight, then slipped the envelope into her coat pocket and left the park.

The interior of her car was cold and dark. She locked the doors, placed the envelope on the passenger seat, and studied it by the dome light before opening it. The creases were worn, the paper slightly yellowed—genuine age, not staged.

Inside was a single page: an expedited procurement authorization for Vantor Group, dated eleven months before the Kessler collapse. The header read COMMISSION ON INFRASTRUCTURE AND TRANSPORT — PROCUREMENT DIVISION — FAST‑TRACK AUTHORIZATION — RESTRICTED.

The body was brief. It waived the standard competitive tender, cited an urgent safety remediation deadline, and directed the signing officer to proceed directly with Vantor. The approval signature was a rubber stamp—someone else’s name, standard delegation.

But the handwritten annotations in the margin drew her eye. A set of initials next to the routing step: “AR / Procurement” with a date. A circled note: "“Priority. Deliver to legal by COB.”" And at the bottom, a single line in the same hand: "“Terms renegotiated per meeting of 14/02. Apply new schedule.”"

The handwriting was compressed, right‑slanting, with tight loops on the lowercase "e"s and an abrupt downward stroke on the "t" crossbar. It looked like someone had written quickly but deliberately, pressing hard enough to leave a groove in the page.

She pulled the certification report from her bag. The forged signature pages were in a separate folder—she had requested a full photocopy from Records after the lab confirmed the stamp mismatch. She removed the original report from its sleeve and laid the fast‑track document beside it.

The same hand.

The initials on the forgery’s routing slip were different—a different clerk—but the marginal annotation in the lower right corner of the certification report was unmistakable. A short phrase: "“Check liner spec against delivery date.”" The "e"s matched. The "t" crossbar matched. The same person had handled both documents.

Iris traced the annotation on the certification report with her thumbnail, feeling the slight indentation. The same pressure. The same rhythm.

She turned to the fast‑track document’s processing header. At the top right, a line read: "“Input terminal: TERM-47B / SEC-PROC / CIT-NET / 10:14:22.”"

Terminal 47B. That was the procurement secretariat’s internal designation for a specific network point in the commission building. The same terminal that had processed the certification routing logs she had reviewed in the Initial Forensic Archive—the terminal that had entered the forged acceptance stamp into the system.

She checked. The archive logs showed the stamp entry had come from Terminal 47B at 09:47 on the same day as the certification. The fast‑track document had been processed through the same terminal at 10:14.

Fifty-seven minutes apart.

Someone had sat at that terminal, entered the forged certification stamp, then processed the fast‑track authorization for the same contractor. Same hands. Same terminal. Same morning.

Iris placed both documents side by side and photographed them with her phone. She captured the routing slips, the annotations, the terminal IDs, and the page watermarks. She saved the images to a folder labeled "“Kessler — Personal”" and emailed them to a backup account that required two‑factor authentication.

She sat back in the driver’s seat, the photographs secured. The interior of the car felt colder now.

The evidence was not complete. The terminal ID pointed to the procurement secretariat, but the department had multiple users. She needed the access logs for Terminal 47B on that date—every login, every user authentication, every session. The logs would tell her who had been assigned to that terminal, and whether the same user had processed both transactions.

The inquiry’s systems were accessible from her commission credentials, but the logs would be restricted. She would need a different route—possibly through the network event logs that the IT desk archived weekly, or through the secretariat’s shift records. Neither was easy to obtain without raising an alert.

She looked at the handwriting again. The compressed script had a specific rhythm, a specific slant. She had seen it elsewhere, not on documents but on a signature line in her own file. The note Theo Vance had written on her appointment letter: "“Welcome aboard. —TV.”"

The loops on the "e"s were not identical. The slant was slightly different. But the pressure, the abrupt "t" crossbar—there was a family resemblance. Not proof. Not yet.

But enough to know where to look first.

She placed the fast‑track document back in its envelope and the certification report back in its folder. The engine turned over with a low hum. She did not drive anywhere immediately. She sat in the silence of the car, the two documents resting on the passenger seat like objects that had become heavier in the minutes since she had touched them.

The handwriting annotations were the same. The terminal ID was the same. Someone in the secretariat had worked both sides of the Kessler contract—approving the fast‑track procurement and entering the forged certification into the system. That person was still employed, still walking the commission’s corridors.

Iris restarted the engine and pulled out of the parking space. She would not request the terminal logs through official channels. She would find another way. There were records in the IT department that no one thought to lock down—shift schedules, terminal assignment sheets, system access archives. If she could get to those before someone noticed she had the fast‑track document, she could trace Terminal 47B to a name.

The handwriting already pointed in a direction she did not want to follow. But she had followed worse leads to worse conclusions. She would follow this one to the terminal logs first, and then she would know.

The park bench where they had met had a brass dedication plaque that listed the donor’s name as "In Memory of Stephen — 63". The same number as the Kessler dead. The same Stephen her research said was a Vantor engineer who died in a car crash the year before. She had noted it when she chose the bench. Now the number felt like another message she was only beginning to decode.

She drove toward the commission building. The terminal logs would tell her whose hands had worked both the forgery and the fast‑track. And whether those hands belonged to the same person she had been trusting for weeks.
