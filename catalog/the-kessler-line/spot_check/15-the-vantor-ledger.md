The Vantor Group tower gleamed in the twilight, a monument to corporate power. Iris Kane studied the blueprints Theo Vance had sketched, then slipped through the service entrance, a ghost in the shadows.

The first inconsistency waited six steps inside.

Theo had marked the service corridor as unsecured between the loading bay and the freight elevators. Instead, a smoked-glass partition sealed the passage, and a biometric scanner glowed beside it.

Unlisted. Recently installed.

Iris ran her thumbnail along the edge of the blueprint. The paper had softened where Theo had pressed too hard with his pencil, leaving a groove beneath the words finance access.

He had warned her Sol would begin cleaning the paper trail.

He had not warned her the building would grow new locks overnight.

Footsteps crossed the loading bay behind her.

Iris folded the blueprint into quarters and slipped it into her jacket. A security guard entered through the service door, carrying a paper cup and staring at his phone. His dark uniform bore no corporate insignia.

Personal security, then. Sol had stopped trusting the building’s ordinary staff.

The guard looked up.

Iris lifted the plastic crate she had taken from the recycling dock. It held toner cartridges and two sealed packets of maintenance wipes, enough dull objects to make the lie look institutional.

“You’re late,” he said.

“So is the third-floor printer fault.”

“There’s no maintenance scheduled.”

“There usually isn’t after someone pours sweetener into a paper tray.”

The guard’s expression did not change. “Identification.”

Iris set the crate down slowly. “Left pocket.”

“Take it out.”

She reached into her jacket, keeping her movements bored rather than careful. Careful people invited scrutiny. Irritated contractors invited paperwork.

The pass Theo had provided was six months old and belonged to an external facilities vendor whose contract had since been absorbed by Vantor. The photograph was poor enough to be useful.

The guard took it without touching her hand.

“This credential is inactive.”

“Then whoever called me dragged me across the city for nothing.” Iris glanced at the crate. “Which would make tonight consistent with every other Vantor assignment.”

His eyes narrowed.

A second guard appeared in the corridor beyond the partition. He was broader, alert, and wearing an earpiece.

“Problem?” he asked through the glass.

“Inactive vendor credential.”

The second guard pressed a control. The partition unlocked with a soft click.

“Name of the employee who called you,” he said.

Iris gave him the surname printed on the maintenance request she had found in the loading dock’s disposal bin. It was not enough to pass inspection. It only had to survive the first ten seconds.

The guard checked a tablet.

“There’s no open ticket.”

“Because internal support closed it.”

“Then why are you here?”

“To fix what they closed.”

Silence stretched.

Iris kept her face flat. Her pulse was trying to break into a sprint without the rest of her. Very ambitious of it.

The first guard’s radio hissed.

“West lobby,” a voice said. “Delivery dispute.”

He looked toward the loading bay. The second guard considered Iris for another moment, then handed back the pass.

“Freight elevator. Third floor only.”

“Generous.”

“If you leave the third floor, the pass flags.”

“Then perhaps tell your printers to fail in approved locations.”

She picked up the crate and walked through the partition.

The scanner beside the glass caught her attention as she passed. A faint waxy residue marked the lower edge of its black plate. It carried a clean floral scent with a bitter citrus note beneath it.

Iris had smelled that scent twice before, both times when Adrienne Sol had extended a hand across a conference table. Expensive hand cream, applied sparingly. Distinctive enough to linger after the hand was gone.

Sol had used this entrance.

Not the executive lobby. Not the secured underground garage. The service corridor.

Iris kept walking.

The freight elevator opened without complaint. Inside, Theo’s blueprint showed three monitored floors between her and finance, but the control panel told a more unpleasant story. The buttons for six, nine, twelve, and sixteen had been replaced with blank steel plates. A small camera tracked from the upper corner.

She pressed three.

The doors closed.

Theo had told her the internal ledger was mirrored through finance because Vantor’s operational payments moved under harmless procurement labels. The ledger would not say suppressed load test. Corporations preferred language that made guilt sound like inventory management.

Pre-production anomaly. Technical reconciliation. Retention adjustment.

Words washed clean of consequences.

The elevator stopped on the second floor.

Iris shifted the crate against her hip. The doors opened onto an empty corridor, then remained open.

No one entered.

A red sensor blinked above the frame.

Weight check, Iris thought.

The crate suddenly mattered. So did the vendor pass, her height, and every gram Vantor’s security system had decided belonged to a legitimate maintenance contractor.

She stepped out before the elevator could trap her.

The doors closed immediately.

“Subtle,” she murmured.

The second floor was dark except for emergency strips near the floor. Theo’s blueprint identified a stairwell at the east end, but the corridor had been divided by another smoked-glass barrier. The scanner beside this one had no card slot.

Palm and pulse recognition.

Unlisted again.

Two guards’ voices approached from an intersecting hall.

“She came through service?”

“Vendor credential.”

“Sol’s instruction was no contractors after nineteen hundred.”

Iris moved toward the nearest door. Locked.

The next one opened into a records room lined with narrow shelving. She slipped inside and eased the door almost shut.

The guards stopped outside.

“Loading bay confirmed her face?”

“Not yet.”

“Then she doesn’t move beyond three.”

“She said printer fault.”

“No open faults.”

Iris rested the crate silently on the floor. Through the gap, one guard’s shadow cut across the emergency lighting.

“Check the third floor,” the other said. “I’ll pull the service feed.”

Their footsteps separated.

Iris waited until one shadow disappeared. Then she opened the door, stepped behind the remaining guard, and walked in the same direction he was moving.

He heard her after four strides.

“Stop.”

She did not run. Running admitted the wrong thing too early.

The guard turned. “How did you get here?”

“Your elevator opened here.”

“You were cleared for three.”

“Then repair the elevator after I repair the printer.”

His gaze dropped to her empty hands.

The crate remained in the records room.

A basic error. Irritating.

“Where’s your equipment?”

“Behind you.”

He glanced back by reflex.

Iris shoved the records-room door open against his shoulder, catching him off balance. She drove the heel of her hand into the scanner’s emergency release. Nothing happened.

Naturally. Vantor had paid for competent hardware.

The guard recovered and caught her sleeve.

Iris twisted free, leaving a strip of fabric in his fist, and slammed the records-room door between them. His radio crackled as she sprinted toward the stairwell.

“Female intruder, second floor east corridor.”

The stairwell door accepted the inactive pass.

Theo had been right about one lock, which was almost insulting.

Iris took the stairs two at a time. Finance occupied the ninth floor, but the door there lacked the crash bar shown on the blueprint. A brushed steel panel held another biometric reader.

The same hand cream residue gleamed along its edge.

Sol had not merely used the service entrance. She had traveled this route herself, touching scanners that did not exist on the official security plan.

Iris pulled a sealed maintenance wipe from her pocket. Earlier, at the service entrance, she had pressed the adhesive side of a transparent equipment label against the oily residue left on the scanner. A partial print was a poor key.

Poor did not mean useless.

She laid the label over the ninth-floor reader and pressed her palm against it to provide warmth.

The scanner flashed amber.

“Come on.”

Amber again.

Below, the stairwell door opened.

“East stairwell clear to seven,” a guard said.

Iris shifted the label half a millimeter and pressed harder.

The scanner turned green.

The finance door released.

She entered and pulled it closed before the guards reached the next landing.

Rows of low desks filled the floor. The monitors were dark, but thin white lights glowed beneath the cabinets. No personal photographs, no abandoned mugs, no evidence that people did anything here except convert decisions into numbers.

Theo’s blueprint marked Sol’s finance terminal behind a frosted partition at the far end.

Iris crossed the floor quickly.

A glass door protected the executive accounting suite. This scanner required a card and a fingerprint. Theo’s copied credential supplied the card. Sol’s lifted print supplied the rest.

Green.

Too easy.

Iris paused with her hand on the door.

Theo had taught her that reaction. When a system became easier after resisting her twice, it was not cooperation. It was observation.

She checked the frame. A narrow copper contact had been added near the hinge, connected to a silent alarm circuit.

Iris wedged a folded strip from the blueprint beneath it before opening the door.

Inside, Sol’s terminal waited on a bare desk. The only decoration was a ceramic dish containing three identical black binder clips.

Iris sat and connected the authorization token Theo had drawn from memory before his arrest. Six digits. A pause. Two more. Then the physical key sequence hidden beneath Vantor’s standard login screen.

The monitor opened onto a procurement dashboard.

She selected archived adjustments.

Nothing.

She entered Theo’s phrase: pre-production anomaly.

The screen populated with ledger entries.

Codes replaced names. Dates replaced explanations. Payments moved through inspection, materials review, technical reconciliation, and retention adjustment. Some entries predated Marek’s fraudulent certification.

There it was. Physical sequence made financial.

Marek had executed the certification. Vance had manipulated access and carried instructions. But these entries showed the activity grouped, funded, and scheduled through Sol’s executive controls.

Not rumor. Not a frightened man buying cooperation after arrest.

Coordination.

Iris inserted a blank storage wafer and began copying the ledger.

Eight percent.

The files stalled.

A warning appeared: local archive incomplete. External channel reconciliation required.

“Of course it is.”

She traced the ledger references manually. Every pre-production anomaly code linked to a second entry, but the destination field contained only a small angular symbol: three intersecting strokes inside an open square.

No department name. No vendor account. No ordinary encryption marker.

She selected it.

A hidden communications panel unfolded across the screen.

Encrypted packets appeared in narrow columns, each carrying the same symbol. The outgoing side bore Sol’s executive authentication. The receiving side had no listed identity.

Iris tried to copy the channel index.

Access denied.

She tried to print it.

The terminal emitted a low tone.

Outside the glass partition, an elevator chimed.

Iris froze.

A man’s voice carried across the empty floor. “Finance seal shows an active session.”

“Sol remote-authorized it?”

“No.”

The copy reached nineteen percent.

Iris opened the ledger’s temporary cache and redirected it to the local printer. Paper began feeding somewhere beyond the partition, each page making a sharp mechanical snap.

The guards heard it.

“Executive suite.”

Footsteps accelerated.

Iris canceled the full transfer and selected only the entries carrying pre-production anomaly codes. Twenty-three percent. Thirty-one.

The glass door handle moved.

The copper contact shifted against the folded blueprint.

Iris grabbed the ceramic dish and jammed it beneath the handle.

The first guard hit the door from outside. The dish cracked.

Forty-six percent.

“Kane,” a voice called through the glass.

Her name changed the calculation.

They had not discovered an intruder. They had anticipated one.

The monitor flickered. A remote cursor crossed the screen and began closing archive windows one by one.

Adrienne Sol was not present, but someone was watching the terminal.

Iris yanked the storage wafer free at fifty-two percent. The partial ledger vanished from the screen as the remote session purged it.

The ceramic dish split under another impact. One binder clip shot across the desk.

Iris snatched the printed pages from the output tray. Seven sheets, the last one cut off halfway through an anomaly entry. The unfamiliar symbol appeared beside six transactions.

She folded the pages under her jacket.

The glass door burst inward.

Iris shoved the desk chair into the first guard’s knees and ran for the secondary exit. Theo’s blueprint showed a connecting hall to the western stairwell.

The wall held no door.

She dragged her thumbnail along the panel seam until it caught on a narrow break in the surface. Concealed latch.

She pressed it.

A section of wall opened.

Behind it stretched a corridor absent from every plan Theo had drawn.

Red lights ignited overhead.

The security alarm blared, a piercing shriek that echoed through the empty corridors. Iris Kane bolted, the partial log of the Vantor ledger clutched in her hand. On a deserted floor, she glanced back. A red light pulsed above a door she hadn't seen before, bearing a symbol she couldn't place. She knew, then, that Adrienne Sol had deeper connections than Theo Vance had realized.
