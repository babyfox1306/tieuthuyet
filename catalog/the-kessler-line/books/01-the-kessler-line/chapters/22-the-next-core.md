The hum of the core drill was a familiar comfort, a rhythmic pulse against the new concrete. Months had passed since the Kessler Line inquiry, and Iris Kane was back where she belonged: in the field, reading the bones of a structure.

The site was a medium-span overpass outside Portstown, the kind of unglamorous municipal project that kept the transport network alive. Iris adjusted the drill's angle, feeling the bite of the bit as it sank into the foundation column. The vibration traveled up her wrists, into her shoulders, a language she understood better than any spoken testimony. She watched the core sample lengthen, a perfect cylinder of cured concrete. No aggregate separation. No microfractures. This one was clean.

The site foreman, a wiry man named Decker, kept his distance. He'd read the news. Everyone had. Iris didn't mind. The less conversation, the more work got done. She repositioned the bit for a second extraction near the side of the footing. The core barrel screamed against the aggregate, a high-pitched protest before settling into the low, steady grind that meant the teeth had found their rhythm.

"How long for the full readout?" Decker asked from the edge of the pit.

"Three business days for the strength tests," Iris said, not looking up. "The core's clean. No voids, no alkali reaction. Your forms were well-tamped."

Decker nodded, relief plain on his face. "We kept a tight crew. Heard what happened at the Kessler site. Don't want that kind of attention."

Iris pulled the bit free and examined the fresh core. "Attention isn't the problem. Bad concrete is."

Back at the field trailer, she dropped the core into a padded case and pulled out her tablet. A notification from the Commission's dissolution archive had arrived overnight. The Kessler Line corridor had been fully dismantled. The replacement line would use a different alignment, different contractors, a completely new oversight board. The inquiry recommendations were being codified into a new transportation infrastructure law, currently in committee.

Renn was in federal custody, his appeal dismissed. Adrienne Sol had pleaded down to a reduced sentence in exchange for a full accounting of Vantor's post-collapse document destruction. Marek was dead, which made him the only one who wouldn't answer for his role in the hearing rooms.

And Vance—Theo Vance—was still talking.

Iris paused on a brief news wire. Former Kessler Line Safety Director Theo Vance provided additional testimony yesterday in the reopened examination of the Ridgeline Bridge collapse, citing systemic pressure to fast-track certifications that predated the Kessler project. His cooperation had been instrumental in expanding the scope of the inquiry into past practices. Nine years prior, a single span had given way during peak traffic. No one had been charged. Vance was a junior safety inspector then. His records from that period were now part of the evidence pool.

Iris set the tablet aside and picked up the core sample, running her finger along its flawless surface. Good concrete. She'd file her report, sign the inspection, and move on to the next site. That was the rhythm.

But the archive delivery included a physical file as well. A binder, thick with documents, sent from the dissolution secretariat. The covering note was brief. Enclosed please find the full appendix to the Vantor Group contract ledger, as originally filed with the Ministerial Oversight Committee. This document was recovered from the Commission's evidence vault and is provided for historical completeness.

Iris opened the binder. The binding was fresh, but the pages inside were old. Some bore the faint yellowing of long storage. The contract itself was familiar. She had memorized its terms during the inquiry. The scope of work, the delivery milestones, the certification schedule. All of it pointed to Renn's hidden stake.

But the appendix was different.

It listed every signatory to the contract's financial clauses. Dov Marek. A Vantor Group board representative. A witness from the Ministry of Transport. And then, a full page of redacted names.

The black marker was heavy, but the paper beneath it held a watermark. Iris held the page up to the trailer's fluorescent light. The watermark was a series of interlocking, stylized initials. No company name accompanied them. No public entity. The ink underneath was a different generation than the rest of the ledger—older, pressed into the paper instead of printed. Typewriter ribbon, not laser toner.

She ran her thumbnail over the edge of the blacked-out area. The paper was slightly raised, as if the original ink had been sealed under a layer of correction fluid before the black marker was applied. Someone had gone to significant trouble to remove a set of names from this contract.

She flipped to the page's reverse side. Nothing. The redaction was only on the front. She checked the ledger's binding. The page was an insert, not part of the original print run. It had been added later, then redacted.

She read the paper the way she read concrete—for texture, for pressure, for the subtle language of materials. The heavy bond was a grade above the rest of the ledger, a private press watermark that didn't match any standard entity registration. She tilted it again, catching the light at an angle that made the initials stand out. Three characters. Interlocked.

She noted the paper's weight, the press of the original typewriter keys beneath the correction fluid. The ink was an industry standard, carbon-based, used in official documents in the years before the Kessler contract was signed. But the watermark was not standard. It belonged to a private press, the kind that printed their own letterhead as a mark of quality. But these initials were not in any registry she could recall.

The Kessler Line inquiry had consumed her for over a year. She had traced the load-test failure to Renn, the fast-track to his private stake, the cover-up to a four-tier architecture of deceit. Every piece had been laid out, tested, and published. The record was complete. And yet—here, buried in the appendix of a contract signed before the tunnel was even excavated—was a loose end. A set of names that had been deliberately erased.

It could be nothing. A subsidiary dispute. A confidentiality agreement that had expired. A legitimate legal carve-out.

But the watermark didn't match any known entity. And the paper felt wrong.

She pulled out her phone and dialed the number for the dissolution secretariat's archive desk.

"This is Iris Kane," she said. "I need a copy of the full co-owner ledger for the Kessler Line's original Vantor contract. Unredacted."

The voice on the other end hesitated. "That file is sealed under the terms of the inquiry's dissolution..."

"I have a copy in my hand with a redacted page," Iris said. "I'm not asking for classified material. I'm asking for the document that's supposed to be public."

Another pause. "I'll have to check the retention schedule."

"Check it tonight. I'll call back tomorrow."

She hung up and set the phone on the folding table. The binder sat open on her lap, the redacted page staring back at her. She closed it slowly, feeling the weight of the paper, the press of the binding.

The core samples from the Portstown site were packed in the bed of her truck, labeled and sealed. She would drive them to the lab in the morning, pick up her next set of work orders, and start again. That was the job.

Iris Kane ran her thumbnail over the redacted names in the old ledger, a familiar unease stirring. The Kessler Line was closed, the architect arrested, but the shadow of another structure, unseen and unexamined, stretched far beyond the tunnel. She closed the file, the faint scent of old paper clinging to her fingers. The work, she realized, was far from over.
