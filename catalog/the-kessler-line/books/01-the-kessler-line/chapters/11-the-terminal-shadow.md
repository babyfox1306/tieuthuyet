Iris Kane sent the meeting request to Theo Vance's secure email. Subject: "Urgent: Procedural Discrepancy." She knew he would come.

She arranged the evidence across the conference table in chronological order. The fast-track document. The forged certification. The terminal access logs. The re-certification memo on top—C003a in her private filing system. The sequence needed to tell the story before a single word was spoken.

Theo arrived thirteen minutes early, which told her everything she needed to know about his anxiety. A confident man arrived exactly on time. A guilty man arrived early to gauge the room. She watched him through the glass wall, noting the way he ran his hand through his hair before pushing the door open. The gesture looked familiar—she had cataloged it over the past weeks—but today the fingers trembled. His usually neat hair fell into disarray.

"Iris." He settled into the chair across from her, placing a leather portfolio flat on the table. The portfolio was a prop. A shield. "Your message was cryptic."

"I wanted to keep it that way." She didn't offer coffee, water, or greeting. The pleasantries were finished.

She slid the fast-track approval across the polished surface. His name wasn't on the signature line—he was too careful for that—but the terminal stamp was unmistakable. Time-stamped 14:23 on the date in question. User-coded to his authorization queue.

Theo's eyes scanned the page. His expression didn't change, but his hand drifted to his collar, loosening a tie that wasn't tight.

"Recognize it?" Iris asked.

"It's a fast-track approval." His voice was steady, but the steadiness sounded rehearsed. "These are standard procedure for emergency procurement. You know that."

"I know the procedure is standard. I also know this particular fast-track was executed three weeks before the Kessler Line's load test. Someone pushed a six-month certification process through in twelve days. That's not standard. That's surgical."

She placed the second sheet beside the first. The forged liner certification, stamped with the commission's approval seal. Same terminal origin. Same user code. Same fourteen-minute processing window.

Theo's jaw tightened. A muscle in his temple pulsed. "Where did you get these?"

"The commission's archives." Iris let the statement hang. "The access logs are very specific about who looked at what, and when. You were careful, Theo. You didn't leave fingerprints on the paper. But you left a digital shadow behind every key you pressed. And shadows have shapes."

She placed the terminal logs in front of him. Fifty-six pages of system timestamps, user activity records, and authorization IDs. Pages thirty-two through forty-one told the story: his terminal, his credentials, his action. The override of the original load-test data. The deletion of the first failure report. The insertion of the recertification memo into the archive.

Theo flipped through the pages mechanically. His eyes moved too fast to be reading. He was looking for a way out.

"I don't know what you think you've found," he said, "but you're making a mistake. These records can be spoofed. Stolen. Faked."

"They can." Iris nodded. "But they weren't. I verified the chain of custody myself. The logs haven't been tampered with. The only person who touched them was the person who created them."

She pulled the final document from her folder. The re-certification memo. C003a. Drafted on commission letterhead, dated two days before the collapse, never circulated.

"This memo," Iris said, "was written in your office. It proves you knew the liner materials were uncertified. It proves you authorized them anyway. And it proves you drafted a cover story to retroactively justify the decision."

Theo stared at the memo. The color drained from his face, leaving him pale. He looked at the documents again, as if hoping the evidence would rearrange itself into innocence.

"Iris..." He stopped. Swallowed. His hands were flat on the table, palms down.

"Don't tell me it's not what it looks like," she said. "I've spent the last decade reading evidence. This is not a discrepancy. This is a conspiracy, and you are at the center of it."

The silence stretched. The air conditioning hummed overhead. A muffled phone rang somewhere down the hall.

"I didn't build it," Theo said, his voice low.

"Then who did?"

His composure fractured. His shoulders dropped. His hands came up to his face, pressing against his eyes. "Someone above the site. Above me. I never got a name that held."

The admission landed hard. Iris kept her expression neutral, but her mind was racing.

"You work for the commission. You have access. You know who calls."

"I know who called "me"." Theo's voice cracked. "A Vantor operations line. A private number. The voice changed twice in six months. Same pressure. Same threat shape. The load test failed on the first run. The liner material fractured under standard pressure—twenty-three percent below spec. I watched the video footage. The hydraulic press applied standard operating pressure. The liner panel bowed at forty-seven percent of tolerance. By the fourth cycle, the fracture lines spiderwebbed across the material in a pattern that any junior engineer would recognize as catastrophic failure."

He paused, his hands shaking on the table.

"The lead engineer stopped the test and marked it as failed in the system. Within the hour the failure report was gone from the master queue. That same afternoon I got the call. Override the test result. Classify the sequence. Walk the certification through the expedited lane before the second round of vendor audits was triggered."

"And you did it." Not a question. A confirmation.

"I asked why anyone wanted an uncertified liner in a live tunnel." Theo's voice was barely audible. "I was told it wasn't my job to ask questions. I was told the contract had been cleared at a level above the commission. I was told the liner was going into the tunnel regardless of what the test said, and my choice was whether I wanted to be part of the solution or part of the problem."

Iris's thumbnail pressed into a hairline crack in the table. "Why risk lives for a contractor?"

"Because the contractor was Vantor. They were behind on delivery. The liner was the bottleneck in the supply chain. If the certification failed, the entire contract would be exposed to review. Whoever called me didn't care about the material. They cared about the contract. They cared about the timeline. They cared about people they answer to."

"Who do they answer to?"

Theo shook his head. "I don't know. I never knew. Everything was compartmentalized. I was a foot soldier. I got instructions, I executed them. I forged the certification. I buried the load-test report. I built the wall around the fast-track approval."

"And the re-certification memo?"

"I drafted it under instruction. It was supposed to be a failsafe. If anyone questioned the certification after the tunnel was operational, the memo would surface and provide retroactive cover. But the collapse happened first."

Iris filed away every date, every scrap of procedure. The architecture of the conspiracy was coming into focus—but only as far as the evidence reached. Marek was the offered head. Vance was the operative. Above him, a still-unnamed hand in operations. She would not invent a name to fill the gap.

She leaned forward. "You're going to testify."

Theo laughed, the sound hollow and broken. "Testify to what? That I'm a criminal? I know how this ends, Iris. I go down with the ship."

"Or you help me sink the ship first."

He looked at her. The silence returned, heavier this time. The air conditioning hummed. A phone rang somewhere down the hall.

"They will destroy you," he said. "Whoever they are. They have people in the security office. They have people in the archives. They know everything that happens in this building."

"Then they already know you're here." Iris met his eyes. "The only advantage you have left is saying it first—with paper, not speeches."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Then his shoulders sagged.

"I need a laptop."

"You don't get a laptop. You get a pen and paper. I want dates, locations, terminals, call times. Every document you handled. Every email you sent and deleted. No invented names. If you don't know, write that you don't know."

He picked up the pen. The first stroke was unsteady, then the writing began to flow. Dates. Times. Conference room numbers. Phone calls routed through burner lines.

Iris watched him write, her mind already moving ahead. The next hand was still blank. Blank meant she had to find it in concrete, not in his mouth.

Theo paused, the pen hovering above the paper. "The load test... it failed, Iris. Not just the material. The whole system. The dispatch error was real, but it wouldn't have mattered if the tunnel had held. The tunnel didn't hold because the liner was wrong. And the liner was wrong because the certification was signed off without a physical audit—on my terminal, under my code."

He looked up at her, his eyes hollow. "I didn't know people would die. I thought it was just a piece of paper. I thought it was politics."

"It was politics," Iris said. "And politics killed sixty-three people."

Theo finished writing. He slumped in his chair, the carefully constructed facade finally shattering.

"I gave the orders that reached the liner," he whispered, his voice hoarse. "I overrode. I buried the failure report. I cannot give you the name above me. I never held it."

Iris took the pages. Folded them. Placed them in her jacket pocket.

"You're going to help me follow the physical trail," she said. "Not invent a villain to make yourself smaller."

His agreement was a whisper. "Yes."

She looked at the hairline crack in the table. The second tier had fallen. The next name would have to be earned.
