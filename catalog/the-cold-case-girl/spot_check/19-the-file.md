The photograph arrived at 2:14 AM. A page from the Julian Croft boating-accident file, annotated in blue and initialed V.R. Victor Rhodes had been building a case on me for months, and I hadn't seen it coming.

I studied the image on my phone until the case number blurred. The report was official, stamped with digits I'd memorized years ago. But the annotations were new. Blue ink, slanted script, each conclusion linked to a source page.

"Fuel-line coupling deformation — documented as fire damage, not tested for deliberate tampering."

"Subject arrived at marina 45 minutes before departure — no witnesses."

"Cell phone ping placed subject within 200 meters of slip C-12 at 22:47."

Subject. He'd written "subject" like I was a case study. Like I was a specimen under glass.

I checked the timestamp on the message. 2:14 AM. Rhodes could assume I'd be awake and alone, running through every mistake I'd ever made. The timing felt like a surgeon placing a blade.

Another photograph arrived. It showed a close-up of the brass fitting recovered from the engine compartment.

"Tool mark consistent with deliberate loosening. Crescent on coupling matches pre-fire marina photograph."

I set the phone face-down on the table. The safe house was cold, the kind of cold that seeps through concrete and settles in your bones. I'd chosen this place for its anonymity, for the lack of windows facing the street, for the deadbolt that could hold against a battering ram. But none of that mattered now. Rhodes had found the one wound that could bleed me dry, and he was pressing on it with surgical precision.

I picked up the phone and called Wren.

She answered on the first ring. "You're up late."

"Tell me you're at a terminal."

"I'm always at a terminal. What's wrong?"

"I need you to trace a file. Julian Croft boating accident report, case number ending in 447. Someone accessed it recently — I need to know where and when."

Keys clicked in the background. "Give me a minute."

I waited, my eyes fixed on the photograph. The blue ink seemed to glow on the screen, each annotation a small knife. Rhodes had been thorough. He'd cross-referenced the marina's security logs, the cell tower data, the witness statements that had been conveniently lost in the original investigation. He'd found the gaps I'd left, the seams I'd thought were invisible.

"Got it," Wren said. "The state records portal logged a licensed-investigator credential three days ago. The account bills to a firm in the Rhodes Building. That places the request with his people, not on a machine I can see."

"Can you confirm physical possession?"

"Not from here. The portal shows one high-resolution download. The annotations in his message prove someone worked from that copy, but I cannot see where he stored it."

I closed my eyes. "Can you delete it?"

"Not from the state archive, and I will not alter a government record. Even if I could erase the download receipt, it would not touch his copy or the physical evidence."

"Can you see where the download went?"

More keys clicked. "Only an endpoint identifier registered to the firm. After the download, the trail ends. Assume copies. Do not pretend we know how many."

The limit was worse than false certainty. I'd known from the first time I heard his voice on the podcast that he was careful, calm, and dangerous. Knowing it and feeling it were different things. His competence made the room feel airless.

"Thank you, Wren. Keep monitoring the portal credential. If his investigators request the file again, I want to know."

"Already set up an alert. Be careful, Nadia. He's baiting you."

"I know."

I ended the call, studied the photograph once more, then dialed Sasha.

She answered with the sharp clarity of someone who'd been awake for hours. "Tell me it's not a crisis."

"It's a crisis."

"I'll make coffee. What do you have?"

I described the photographs, the annotations, and the server access. I told her about the fuel line, the cell-phone ping, and the tool mark on the fitting. I laid out every piece of evidence Rhodes had collected, and when I finished, the silence stretched long enough to worry me.

"Sasha?"

"I'm here. I'm just thinking." Papers shuffled in the background. "The file alone isn't enough to charge you. It's circumstantial—a boating accident with suspicious annotations doesn't prove murder. But the recovered fitting and parking record give investigators places to start."

"What photograph?"

"The parking receipt tied to the marina. Four digits of your card number, timestamped before the fire."

My stomach turned. "I didn't leave that."

"You must have. Or Rhodes is lying about when he found it. Either way, he has pieces that create a narrative. The receipt places your card near the marina, and his photograph places you at the later trap. A prosecutor could build outward from both."

"But not convict."

"Not without a confession or a weapon. And you're smart enough to give neither." She paused. "But here's the problem, Nadia. Rhodes isn't a prosecutor. He doesn't need a conviction. He needs leverage. And with this file, he has it."

"What do I do?"

"Find the file. Learn its custody. But understand that if he has backups—"

"Wren found the download event. We don't know where the copy or the physical evidence is."

Sasha exhaled slowly. "Then you do not touch anything until you know the architecture. One destroyed copy could trigger the rest."

"How long do I have?"

"I don't know. He sent you the photograph at 2:14 AM. That's a message — he wants you to know he has it. He wants you to panic. He wants you to make a mistake."

"I won't."

"You already have. You left evidence at Croft's death scene. You trusted a network that might have a leak. You underestimated how thoroughly Rhodes would investigate you." Her voice softened, just slightly. "I'm not saying this to hurt you. I'm saying it because you need to see clearly. The hunter has been hunted before, but never like this."

I looked at the photograph again. The blue ink seemed brighter now, almost accusing. "I need to see the original file. My copy."

"Where is it?"

"Storage. I kept everything from the Croft case — the reports, the photographs, the notes. I've had it for about three years."

"Then go look at it. Remind yourself what you're fighting for. And call me when you're ready to move."

"I will."

I ended the call and stood up, my legs heavy with exhaustion and adrenaline. The storage box was in the corner of the safe house, tucked behind a false panel I'd installed myself. I pulled it out and set it on the table, my fingers tracing the edges of the lid.

The box was plain, unmarked, the kind you'd buy at any office supply store. But inside was the origin wound — every piece of paper, every photograph, every scrap of evidence from the night I killed Julian Croft.

I opened the lid.

The file was thinner than I remembered. About three years of carrying it, and I'd never added to it. Never needed to. The story was complete: Julian Croft had murdered my younger sister, and I had killed him for it. Precise, controlled, supposedly unsolvable.

But Rhodes had solved it.

I pulled out the photograph of the fuel line—the loosened coupling and the crescent where my wrench had slipped. The glossy surface held the ghost of the metal I'd touched that night.

The boat had been moored at slip C-12, the same slip where Julian Croft kept his weekend cruiser. I'd arrived at 10:02 PM, forty-five minutes before he was scheduled to leave. No witnesses. No security cameras. Just me and the fuel line and the knowledge that what I was about to do would change everything.

I'd made one mistake that had nothing to do with the engine. I paid for marina parking with a card I stopped using the next morning. The receipt preserved only four digits, but Rhodes had matched them to an old podcast-expense record.

I set the photograph down and picked up the recovery report. Julian Croft's body had been found three days after the yacht burned. The official report called it an accidental boating fire. But the annotations about the loosened fuel-line coupling suggested deliberate sabotage.

I closed my eyes and saw the cramped engine compartment again. The wrench closing on the fuel line. The walk back across the dock. The explosion after I was already in the parking lot.

That was the night I'd crossed the line from victim to executioner. Now Rhodes was using the evidence I left around that line to destroy me.

I looked at my phone. The photograph was still there, the blue ink still glowing. And beneath it, a new message.

"I have the fitting, the receipt, and the report. You cannot erase all three."

The phone slipped from my fingers and hit the table with a sharp crack.

He was right about the evidence. Destroying one record would not erase the others. I needed to understand who controlled each piece before I moved.

I picked up the phone and typed a single word.

"Watch me."

Then I closed the file, slid the storage box back into its hiding place, and began to plan.
