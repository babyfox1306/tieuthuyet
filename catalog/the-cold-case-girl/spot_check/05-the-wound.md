She had a laugh that filled a room. And then the room went silent.

I held the photograph by its softened edges. Mara at seventeen, her head thrown back, that unrestrained laugh captured on cheap glossy paper. She'd just won the county science fair. Our mother had framed the certificate. I'd stolen the photograph the night after the funeral.

The boating accident report sat on my desk, the file open to the third page. Julian Croft's name jumped out at me, and I was no longer in my apartment. I was back in the courtroom, almost four years ago, watching a man who should have been convicted smile at the jury.

The memory hit like a physical blow.

Mara had called me that night. I'd been working late at the station, editing a segment on police misconduct. She'd left a voicemail, her voice bright and slightly tipsy: "Nadia, you won't believe what I found out about Croft. He's been—" The message cut off. I'd replayed it a thousand times, trying to hear what she'd discovered. The police report said she dropped her phone in the struggle.

I'd found her body before the paramedics arrived. The apartment door was unlocked, which was wrong—Mara always locked it, twice, because she was paranoid about the building's faulty deadbolt. He'd strangled her with her own scarf, the one I'd bought her for Christmas. The medical examiner listed the cause as asphyxiation due to ligature strangulation.

The trial was a circus.

Julian Croft had money. His family owned a chain of car dealerships across three states, and that money bought a defense team that turned Mara into a narrative. She'd been unstable, they argued. She'd been obsessed with Croft after a brief dating history. The bruises on her wrists? Self-inflicted, according to their expert witness. The scratches on Croft's face? He'd been trying to restrain her during a psychotic episode.

I remembered the prosecutor's tie during closing arguments. It was crooked, the knot slightly off-center, the red silk slipping to the left. I couldn't stop staring at it. This man was supposed to deliver justice for my sister, and he couldn't even straighten his tie. The sloppiness of it—the casual incompetence—made me want to scream.

But the detail that haunted me most was the alibi witness.

A woman named Patricia Dornan. She testified that Julian was with her the night Mara died, that they'd been at a charity gala until midnight. She was polished, well-dressed, her story airtight. The jury believed her.

Six months later, Patricia recanted. She'd been paid, she admitted to a reporter. She'd never been at the gala. Julian Croft had paid her fifty thousand dollars to lie.

The recantation could not undo an acquittal. Double jeopardy barred a second murder trial, and prosecutors declined a separate witness-tampering case because Patricia's account was uncorroborated. Julian Croft stayed free, and Patricia Dornan disappeared back into whatever life she'd sold.

My thumb tapped against my left palm. Once. Twice. The photograph of Mara trembled in my grip.

I only kill the provably guilty. That's the rule. Julian Croft was the first to meet that standard, and I'd spent months gathering evidence, watching him, learning his patterns. The boating fire that killed him was clean—the loosened fuel-line coupling, the leak after the engine pressurized, the vapor, the ignition. The official report listed it as an accidental boating fire.

No one questioned it. No one looked twice.

But Victor Rhodes was looking now.

I forced myself back to the present. The apartment was quiet, the only sound the hum of my laptop fan. The photograph of Mara stared up at me, frozen in that moment before everything collapsed.

Sasha's name flashed on my screen.

"Tell me something good," I said, my voice steady.

"The boating report drew no flags," Sasha said. "I've got a contact in the Coast Guard office who pulled the original. No internal memos asking questions. It's buried."

"Too buried?"

A pause. "What do you mean?"

I leaned back in my chair, the leather creaking. "The report on Julian Croft's death was filed three years ago. It's sitting in a database that Victor Rhodes is currently digging through. He found Elise Marchetti's file. He's going to find this one."

"You don't know that."

"He's smart, Sasha. Patient. He's already connected me to the case. He's going to wonder why a podcaster with no personal connection to Elise is suddenly so interested in her murder."

"Then we make sure there's nothing to find."

"There's always something to find. The marina access logs Wren reviewed after I finally told her about Croft—does my name appear anywhere?"

"No name," Sasha said. "The public digital entry records only a time and vehicle class. The surviving parking receipt carries four digits from your old card, nothing more."

I closed my eyes. The network was good. Sasha was careful. Priya was meticulous. Wren was paranoid. But Victor Rhodes wasn't a random detective chasing a cold case. He was the man who had arranged Elise Marchetti's murder and walked away without a scratch. He knew how to hide things. He knew how to find them.

"Keep your contact close," I said. "If anyone starts asking questions about the Croft file, I want to know before they finish the search."

"Already set up. Wren's monitoring the access logs on the county database. Anyone pulls that file, we get a ping."

"Good."

"Nadia." Sasha's voice softened. "You okay?"

I looked at Mara's photograph. The laugh that filled a room. The silence that followed.

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm working."

"That's the same thing."

I almost smiled. "I'll call you tomorrow."

The line went dead. I set the phone down and picked up the photograph again, tracing the outline of Mara's face with my finger. She'd be twenty-seven now. Probably married, maybe with a kid. She'd wanted to be a veterinarian, had been saving up for vet school. She'd been volunteering at an animal shelter on weekends.

And Julian Croft had taken all of that because she'd found something she shouldn't have.

I had never learned what she discovered that night. The voicemail cut off before she could say it. The police never investigated. The prosecutor never asked. The system failed her, and I wouldn't.

That's the rule. That's always been the rule.

I set the photograph down and opened my laptop. The Elise Marchetti file was spread across three windows: the police report, the autopsy photos, the timeline of Victor Rhodes's movements that night. I'd read it so many times I could recite it from memory, but I needed to see it again. I needed to find the crack, the one detail that everyone else had missed.

Because Victor Rhodes was going to find my crack first if I was not careful.

By two in the morning, the case numbers had blurred. Mara's photograph remained beside the keyboard. She would have hated the secrecy, the violence, and my insistence that there was no other way.

I knew that and kept typing.

The half-finished outline for the Elise Marchetti season sharpened beneath my hands.

Victor Rhodes behaved like a man who believed he was untouchable, a man who believed he'd already won.

He didn't know who he was dealing with yet.

But he was about to find out.
