The storage unit smelled of dust and old cardboard. It was the safest place we had.

I watched Sasha Okafor, Priya Anand, and Wren Delgado file through the metal door, each carrying their own version of wariness. The space was fifteen by twenty feet, concrete walls, a single overhead bulb that cast harsh shadows. I'd rented it three weeks ago under a name that didn't exist, paid in cash, soundproofed the walls myself with foam panels from a construction supply store.

Wren set down a laptop bag and a thermos. She unscrewed the lid and poured coffee into four paper cups, handing them out without asking. Black for me. Cream for Sasha. Two sugars for Priya. The gesture was so precise, so practiced, that I almost missed the way her eyes flickered to the exit before settling.

"The walls are thick," I said. "No windows. No cameras within two hundred feet."

Sasha took her coffee and leaned against a stack of plastic bins. Her suit was expensive, navy blue, the kind that said "I belong in courtrooms". "This is the meeting. The real one."

"It is."

Priya set her coffee down without drinking. Her hands were trembling. I'd known Priya for four years, met her through a mutual contact who vouched for her discretion. She'd never been the jumpy type. Tonight, she looked like she hadn't slept in a week.

"There's a problem," she said.

I waited.

She pulled a folded document from her bag and handed it to me. A death certificate. John Mercer, age fifty-seven, cause of death listed as myocardial infarction. Standard. Routine. I scanned the signature at the bottom—Priya Anand, MD.

"Look at the signature," she said.

I looked again. The letters were slightly compressed, the "A" in Anand a little tighter than usual, the "P" in Priya carrying a sharper angle. Subtle. The kind of thing only someone who studied handwriting would notice.

"It's different," I said.

"Because the scan doesn't look like mine." Her voice dropped. "The signature is compressed, and the coding line was amended after I signed it. I've been reviewing my files for the past three days. Three certificates from the last two months have the same scanning artifact."

Sasha stepped closer. "You're sure?"

"I've signed thousands of death certificates. I know my own hand." Priya's jaw tightened. "My office opened RH03 after a coding discrepancy triggered an internal review. I didn't think much of it until an anonymous follow-up attached one of these scans."

Wren had gone still. "A complaint about what?"

"A clerk corrected the manner-of-death code after I signed. That is enough for a routine check. The anonymous follow-up claimed the correction concealed fraud, and it was too detailed for someone outside the system."

The watcher thread I'd been feeling for weeks pulled tighter. Rhodes was the obvious suspicion, not proof. He was methodical, patient, the kind of predator who tested the ground before stepping. If he'd found Priya, he was circling.

"He's probing," I said. "Looking for weaknesses."

"Weaknesses we didn't know we had." Sasha's voice was flat. "If the scans are artifacts, the originals will settle it. If someone altered the underlying records, the review expands. Then they investigate. They trace."

"And if they trace," Priya finished, "they find me. And if they find me, they find you."

The silence stretched. Wren poured herself another cup of coffee, her movements deliberately slow. She was thinking, calculating, her hacker's brain running probabilities.

"I can hash the digital records we already hold and compare them to any certified copies," she said. "But if the underlying originals differ, we have a problem."

"Assume they do," I said. "Assume Rhodes has copies of everything."

Wren nodded. "Then we don't touch the audit. Priya requests certified originals, answers only the documented coding issue, and keeps counsel present. We isolate our archive from anything she produces."

"That protects the review," Priya said. "It doesn't stop Rhodes."

"No. It did not."

I pulled out a manila folder and spread the contents across a plastic table I'd set up in the corner. Victor Rhodes's schedule, compiled from public records, social media, and a dozen smaller sources Wren had scraped. His attorney's contact information. A pattern of meetings at a private club—every Thursday evening, same time, same table.

"He's predictable," Wren said. "Thursday nights, he drives himself. No driver, no security detail. The club has a parking garage with a blind spot in the camera coverage. If you know the right time window, you can access the vehicle without being seen."

"How long is the window?"

"Eleven minutes. Between when he parks and when the attendant does a sweep."

Eleven minutes. Tight. Possible.

Sasha stepped forward. "Legal contingencies. If this goes wrong, we need a narrative that withstands scrutiny. Production cover—you schedule the episode and public posts in advance, but we do not pretend that proves where you were. Burner phone protocol—Wren sets up a relay so operational calls do not touch your regular account. And if someone gets picked up, you say nothing. Absolutely nothing. I get you out, but only if you keep your mouth shut."

"Agreed," I said.

"The execution itself." Sasha's voice dropped. "How?"

"Car accident," I said. "Tampered brake line. A turn on the highway, a curve he takes too fast, and the car pulls wide before he recovers. It looks like a tragedy. A rich man, too confident, too careless."

Priya's face went pale. "I sign death certificates. I don't plan them."

"You don't have to plan this one," I said. "If the body reaches your office and the injuries support a crash, you process what is there."

The words hung in the air. I watched Priya process them, watched her fear war with her loyalty. She'd joined my network because she believed in the work. Because she'd seen too many guilty men walk free, too many victims buried without justice. But this was different. This was active. Intentional.

She nodded. A small, almost imperceptible movement. "If the findings support it. I won't bury trauma or toxicology that contradicts the scene."

Wren pulled up a laptop and projected a map onto the concrete wall. "The club is here. The garage entrance is here. The blind spot is here." She traced the route with her finger. "If you enter through the service door, you have access to the parking level without passing the front desk. The front-left hose fitting is reachable from the driver's side. Score it at the stress point and leave it under tension. Normal pressure may hold. A hard application may split it and dump that circuit."

"How long does the fluid take to drain?"

"It may not fail at all," Wren said. "And one failed circuit does not erase every brake. You are counting on sudden imbalance, surprise, the curve, and that weak railing."

"Four variables," Priya said.

"Four risks," I corrected.

I memorized the timing. Filed it away.

"Thursday night," I said. "That is the first viable window. We do it then."

Sasha made a note. "I'll have the production calendar and counsel packet ready by Wednesday. Wren, you handle the relay. Priya—"

"I know." Priya's voice was steady now. "I stay quiet. I keep my head down."

"And if the audit finds the certificates before then?"

"Then I stop operational work, invoke counsel, and let the review proceed on the records as they exist."

We went through the details for another hour. Timing, routes, contingencies. Wren showed me the exact tools I'd need and the fitting's stress point. Sasha walked through the legal risks, the worst-case scenarios, the escape protocols. Priya sat in silence, her coffee untouched, her eyes fixed on the death certificate in her hands.

When we finished, the others left one by one. Wren first, then Sasha, then Priya, each disappearing into the night like ghosts.

I stayed.

The storage unit felt smaller now. The foam panels absorbed every sound, leaving only the hum of the overhead light and the beating of my own heart. I sat down on a plastic crate and pulled out my phone.

One new voicemail.

I played it.

"Nadia. It's Victor Rhodes." His voice was calm, unhurried, almost friendly. "I listened to your latest episode. You're very thorough. Almost too thorough for a podcaster. I was wondering—how did you know about the boating accident? The one from three years ago. I don't recall it being in any public records."

The message ended.

The boating-accident message remained on the screen. Julian Croft's death. My first kill. The part of my history the network knew only in fragments until the report surfaced.

He knew.

Not everything. Not yet. But enough to ask the question. Enough to make me feel the watcher thread cinch around my throat.

I counted three breaths before I moved.

Rhodes was testing me. Prodding. Seeing how I'd react.

And I'd just committed to killing him.

I looked at the map still projected on the wall, at the route I'd traced to the club, at the blind spot in the garage where I'd score the brake fitting and walk away.

It was the right plan. The controlled plan. The one that would end him and leave no trace.

But somewhere in the dark, someone was watching.
