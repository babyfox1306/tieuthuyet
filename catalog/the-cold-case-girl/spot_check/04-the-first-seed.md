The file on Elise Marchetti was thin. That was the first sign the system had failed her.

I flipped through the pages in the back corner of a twenty-four-hour diner near the river, the fluorescent buzz doing nothing to warm the cold coffee in front of me. Sasha was late. That wasn't like her. But then again, neither was calling a meeting at midnight in a place where the vinyl booths had more tears than the witness statements I was reading.

Elise Marchetti, twenty-eight. Last seen leaving the Portland bookstore where she worked. Body found three days later in Forest Park. Strangulation. Defensive wounds on her forearms. A degraded male profile beneath one fingernail, consistent with Rhodes but not exclusive.

I traced the outline of her face in the crime scene photo. Dark hair, sharp cheekbones, a smile that looked like she'd just heard a good joke. She'd been dead six years. The active investigation had stalled five years earlier.

The bell above the door jingled. Sasha Okafor walked in carrying a messenger bag that sagged around what looked like a small library. She spotted me, nodded once, and slid into the booth across from me without a word.

"You look like hell," I said.

"I've been pulling discovery on a case from 2020." She unzipped the bag and started pulling out files. "The DA's office doesn't digitize anything older than five years unless you threaten to sue. And even then, they 'lose' boxes."

"Convenient."

"Very." She slid a stack of documents across the table. "Elise Marchetti's full case file. Police reports, witness statements, forensics logs, and the coroner's notes. Priya pulled a copy from the medical examiner's archive before they purged it last year."

I picked up the first report. Dated three days after Elise's body was found. The responding officer's notes were sparse: "Deceased female, apparent homicide, no witnesses, case referred to detectives."

"Three paragraphs," I said. "For a murder."

Sasha's jaw tightened. "The detective assigned to it was Danforth. Retired now. I pulled his clearance rate—twenty-three percent. Lowest in the division for three consecutive years."

"And he got the Marchetti case."

"Because her family didn't have money for a private investigator, and she wasn't a white woman from the Hills." Sasha's voice was flat, professional, but I caught the edge underneath. "She had two misdemeanor possession charges from college. Easy to write off."

I set the report down and picked up the next one. Witness statements. Bar patrons who remembered seeing Elise that night. A bartender who said she'd been talking to a man in a dark jacket. Description: tall, well-dressed, late thirties or early forties. No name. No security footage—the bar's cameras had been broken for three weeks.

"Rhodes," I said.

"Probably. But there's nothing tying him to her except proximity and pattern." Sasha pulled out a second folder, thinner than the first, and set it on top of the stack. "There's something else. This came with the file—it was misfiled in the evidence log, so it ended up in the Marchetti box by accident."

I opened it. A boating accident report. About three years old. I knew the place before I read it—Marina del Rey. Julian Croft's body had been recovered three days after the fire.

The page was unremarkable. Standard report. Name, time, location, official ruling: accidental boating fire. No suspicion of foul play. The investigating officer had signed off in under an hour.

I kept my face still. My heart rate didn't change. I'd been trained for this moment—not by anyone specific, but by the years I'd spent learning to separate my emotions from my actions. The death had looked accidental. The report had closed it. There was no reason for this to be in front of me.

Except it was.

I let my eyes drift to the bottom of the page, where the fine print listed the investigating officer's notes. Most of it was boilerplate—weather conditions, water temperature, time of death estimation. But one line caught my attention:

"Fuel-line coupling: localized deformation noted; attributed to heat or mechanical failure. No follow-up."

I read it twice. The first time, I felt nothing. The second time, something cold settled in my chest. The fuel line had been my insurance—a way to make the accident look plausible if anyone looked too hard. I'd used a wrench to break the brass coupling's seal in the engine compartment, just enough for fuel to leak once the system pressurized and vapor to accumulate before ignition. It had worked. The authorities had ruled the fire accidental.

But someone had noticed. Someone had written it down.

And now that note was sitting on a diner table in front of Sasha Okafor, who was watching me with a curious expression.

"Something interesting?" she asked.

I closed the folder. "Misfiled report. Somebody's boating accident from a few years ago. Doesn't have anything to do with Elise."

Sasha's eyes lingered on the folder for a moment, but she didn't push. "Priya said there might be some cross-contamination in the evidence archive. Old cases get misfiled all the time."

"Apparently." I slid the folder to the side and picked up the coroner's report for Elise Marchetti. "Let's focus on what matters."

For the next hour, we went through every document in the file. Sasha had marked key passages with sticky notes—inconsistencies in the timeline, missing evidence logs, a witness who'd recanted her statement three days after giving it. The pattern was subtle but unmistakable: someone had interfered with the investigation. Not through brute force, but through careful, patient manipulation.

The broken cameras and pre-murder threat showed that the pressure around Elise had begun before she died. How much of it belonged to Rhodes was still a question.

"You need to be careful," Sasha said as she packed up the files. "This case is cold for a reason. Whoever buried it has resources."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at me, her face unreadable. "I'm not just talking about the police. I'm talking about the kind of money that buys silence. The kind of connections that make evidence disappear."

"I've dealt with that before."

"Not like this." She paused. "Victor Rhodes isn't just a rich man who got away with murder. He's a strategist. Someone in his orbit is watching you now, and we don't know when it started."

The words landed like a punch to the ribs. "What do you mean?"

Sasha pulled out her phone and showed me a screenshot. It was a calendar entry from a shared document—a schedule for my podcast, complete with recording dates, interview times, and public appearances. The document had been accessed from an IP address registered to Gerald Vance's law firm.

"Someone in his network is tracking you," she said. "This was shared with his attorney's office three weeks ago."

Three weeks ago. Before I'd even announced the Marchetti season.

I looked at the screenshot, then back at Sasha. "Can you trace who accessed it?"

"I'm working on it. But Wren says the trail ends—whoever it is knows how to cover their tracks."

"Then we need to change our approach."

"I agree." She stood up, slinging the messenger bag over her shoulder. "I'll send you the digital copies. And Nadia—be careful with that misfiled report. Priya said it might be connected to something else, but she couldn't figure out what."

I nodded, but I didn't say anything. The boating accident folder sat on the table between us, thin and innocuous, holding a secret that could destroy everything I'd built.

After Sasha left, I sat alone in the booth, staring at the folder. The diner was empty except for a busboy wiping down the counter and a cook reading a newspaper in the back. The fluorescent lights hummed overhead, casting everything in a pale, sickly glow.

I opened the folder again. The report was exactly as I remembered it—official, routine, stamped by the county medical examiner. But the note about the fuel line was there, buried in the fine print, a detail that should have been invisible.

I traced a finger over the words. An abnormally deformed coupling, attributed to heat or mechanical failure and never pursued.

The investigating officer had noticed. He'd written it down. And then he'd let it go.

Why?

I thought about the timeline. Julian Croft had died about three years ago. I'd damaged the fuel line, left the yacht, and watched the explosion from the marina parking lot. His body was recovered three days later. The investigation had been perfunctual—a quick look, a faster conclusion, and a report that had been filed and forgotten.

Or so I'd thought.

I read the note again. The handwriting was cramped, almost illegible, as if the officer had written it in a hurry. It wasn't signed. There was no follow-up, no cross-reference, no indication that anyone had ever looked at it again.

But the report had been misfiled in the Marchetti case. I could not tell whether that was ordinary archival failure or a message.

The thought made my stomach turn.

I closed the folder and slipped it into my bag. A dead man's file should not have felt that heavy, but it carried a secret I'd thought was buried forever.

The page shifted beneath my unsteady hand.

I needed to know who had put that report in the Marchetti file. I needed to know if it was an accident—or a message.

And I needed to know if Victor Rhodes already knew what I'd done.

The diner's clock ticked past one in the morning. Three years since I'd killed Julian Croft, the first man. Three years of treating the report as buried.

Now one overlooked line had put the past on the table between Victor Rhodes and me.

I slipped the folder into my bag without giving the memory a name.

But I knew I would have to.
