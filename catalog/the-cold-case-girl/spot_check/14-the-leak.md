The phone rang at 6:14 AM. Priya's voice was tight, controlled — the voice of someone who could no longer tell review from accusation. "They copied everything, Nadia. The certificates. The signature scans. Eighteen months of logs."

I sat up in bed, the sheet pooling around my waist. My brain shifted from sleep to operation mode in half a second, the way it always did when the network was under threat.

"Slow down. Where are you?"

"In the audit conference room. They brought in a police liaison for the interview. Detective Marcus Holt—the badge number from the first request finally has a name."

Marcus Holt. I had never met him, but Sasha's preliminary search had found twelve years in investigations, including three in homicide and the last eighteen months on a contractor-fraud task force. Methodical. Patient. The kind of detective who built cases brick by brick until the wall was too high to climb.

"What did he ask to see?"

"Death certificates. Toxicology reports. My signature logs from the last eighteen months." Her voice cracked on the last word. "He asked about the Vargas file—the legitimate overdose from March. My signature was on an amended coding sheet."

I closed my eyes. Vargas was not one of ours. The toxicology supported overdose; a clerk had entered the wrong manner-of-death code and corrected it after Priya signed. It was ordinary administrative debris. Fear made it look designed.

It should have been harmless.

Now a detective was using it to ask who had queried Priya's signature records from outside the county system.

"Have you said anything?"

"I told them what happened. That the cause never changed and the coding correction came from the clerk."

"Good. Keep to what you can document. Do not guess, and do not answer questions about another file without counsel."

"Nadia, what if they—"

"I'm going to call Sasha. She'll have someone at your office within the hour. Ask to pause before they move beyond Vargas. Do not let panic turn a true answer into three speculative ones."

"Okay."

"I mean it, Priya. Not another answer without Marcus."

"Okay."

I hung up. The fractures in the ceiling formed a geography of failure I'd been ignoring for too long.

I made myself breathe.

I called Sasha.

She answered on the second ring, her voice already sharp and awake. "Tell me."

"RH03 brought in a police liaison. Marcus Holt. The office produced copies of Priya's signature logs."

A pause. I could hear her breathing, calculating, running through the legal implications the way most people ran through their morning routine.

"How much does he have?"

"At least one case. The Vargas overdose from March, but Priya says the correction was legitimate."

"Can she prove it?"

"With the clerk's worksheet and the toxicology addendum."

Another pause. Longer this time.

"Then Vargas is not the danger. The danger is that Holt follows the outside signature queries until he reaches the private contractor Rhodes used."

"I know."

"I need to be clear with you. If I send someone to represent her, he protects Priya, not the network. He will tell her to answer truthfully about Vargas and refuse questions beyond the review's scope."

"That's what I want."

"If Holt gets a legal path to Marcus Webb or Daniel Ortega, no lawyer can make that path disappear. Do not ask me to manufacture privilege around evidence that already exists."

I stood up and walked to the window. The sky was gray, the street empty, the city still sleeping through the crisis that was already consuming my network.

"Send someone who will keep her from answering fear instead of questions."

"I'll send Marcus Chen. He's solid. He'll protect her interests."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me when this doesn't blow up in our faces."

She hung up.

I called Wren.

"Tell me you're not going to ask me for something illegal at six in the morning," she said, her voice groggy.

"RH03 has a police liaison. Marcus Holt has copies of Priya's signature logs."

The grogginess vanished. "How?"

"I need you to find out why Holt was attached to a coding review."

"Give me an hour."

"You have thirty minutes."

"That's not—"

"Wren. Thirty minutes."

The line went silent. Then: "I'll call you back."

I paced the apartment while I waited. The walls felt too close, the air too thin. Every instinct told me to move, to act, to burn the evidence and disappear into the shadows I'd built for myself.

But that wasn't how this worked. That wasn't how any of this worked.

I was a predator who had been forced onto the defensive. And predators who stayed still too long became prey.

Twenty-three minutes later, a notification chimed.

"Anonymous email," Wren said. "Sent three days ago to the RH03 tip address. The sender claimed a coding correction in the Vargas file concealed medical fraud. It named Priya and attached one of the distorted signature scans."

"Can you trace the sender?"

"Already did. Burner account. Created two weeks ago, used once, then deleted. The IP route goes through three different servers in two different countries."

"Victor Rhodes."

"Possibly," Wren said. "The method matches his pattern. The route does not prove who sent it."

I stopped pacing. "He's not trying to take me down directly. He's trying to destroy my network piece by piece. Make me vulnerable. Isolate me."

"That's what it looks like."

"Can you verify Vargas without touching the county system?"

"From records we already hold, maybe. Why?"

"I need the dispatch log, the toxicology addendum, and the clerk's worksheet compared. If the amendment was legitimate, Sasha can produce the documents through counsel."

"And if it wasn't legitimate?"

"Then no one changes a record. Priya faces the discrepancy as it exists."

"That's a new rule."

I paused. An old version of me had already begun designing the false trail.

"It is an old rule I ignored too often."

"You still want me inside live audit files."

"No. Use the research cache. Give Sasha copies and hashes. Do not write to the county system."

"Understood."

I didn't answer. The line was silent for a long moment.

"I need the Vargas case number," Wren finally said. "Only that. I will search our existing cache."

"I'll send it to you. You have two hours."

"Nadia, I'm not—"

"You're the only one I trust to do this, Wren. You're the only one who can."

Another silence. Then a soft exhale.

"Two hours."

"Thank you."

I hung up and walked to my laptop. The encrypted archive opened with a password I had never written down or spoken aloud. Inside was the research cache Wren administered.

I found what I needed under Vargas: the original toxicology addendum, the clerk's correction request, and a dispatch log with matching times.

It was boring. It was verifiable.

It was not a lie.

I sent copies to Sasha and gave Wren only the file hashes so she could detect alteration without entering the live system.

Then I called Sasha back.

"She's still in the audit conference room," Sasha said. "Marcus is with her. He produced the Vargas documents and advised her not to speculate."

"Good."

"Holt is not pushing Vargas anymore. He wants the identity of the private contractor that queried Priya's signature samples."

"Does he have a name?"

"Not yet. He has an account number and a payment routed through a shell vendor. The same query appeared in his contractor-fraud case."

My blood went cold. Rhodes had paid a contractor to pull Priya's signatures. Holt had reached the contractor independently. Two investigations had crossed without either man controlling the other.

"Does he know it leads to Rhodes?"

"Not yet. Marcus is keeping the review inside its written scope, but he cannot obstruct Holt's separate case."

"Then we preserve the request and do nothing to it."

"Nadia, what are you planning?"

"Nothing that changes evidence."

I hung up before she could argue.

The apartment was quiet. The gray light through the window cast long shadows across the floor, turning the familiar space into something strange and threatening.

I held the phone until my hand steadied.

Victor Rhodes had made one move by buying Priya's signature samples. Holt and the audit had made another. Fear had merged them into an all-powerful enemy because that story was easier than uncertainty.

Rhodes had still paid to examine Priya's signatures. That move was deliberate.

He was trying to break me.

And the only way to stop him was to become more ruthless than he was.

I opened my laptop again. The encrypted folder blinked at me, waiting.

Inside was another file. One I hadn't opened in years. One I'd promised myself I'd never open again.

The file on Julian Croft.

My first kill.

The file name, dates, and buried details belonged to a life I'd almost convinced myself was someone else's.

But they were mine. Every word, every photograph, every piece of evidence I'd collected and then hidden.

Rhodes had researched me. He'd found the connection between the podcast, the boating accident, and the unsolved murder of a man who'd killed my sister.

The evidence proved he knew about Julian. Fear made the next leap for me: if he knew about the first kill, he must know about everything. I did not yet have proof of that.

I couldn't let him destroy my network to get to me. I couldn't let Priya pay for my choices, or Sasha, or Wren.

I closed the laptop and picked up my keys.

The storage unit was across town, in a facility I'd paid for with cash under a name that didn't exist. The file inside was my private record of Julian Croft's death, not the only evidence the fire had left behind.

I'd kept it as a trophy. A reminder of what I'd done and why.

Now it was a liability.

But it was also a weapon.

If Rhodes wanted to play this game, I'd show him the rules.

I walked out the door, the keys cold in my hand, and headed for the one place I'd sworn I'd never go again.
