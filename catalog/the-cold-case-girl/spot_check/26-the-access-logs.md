The hidden recovery token had been used seventeen times.

Sasha read the dates aloud while I built a second timeline on paper. The first access came eleven weeks earlier, before I announced the Elise season. The next six were small: member lists, dead-drop schedules, fragments of case summaries. Enough to prove the network existed. Not enough to expose every person in it.

Then the exports grew.

Three weeks ago, the token opened the Julian Croft operational record.

Two weeks ago, it opened the archive index.

Nine days ago, it copied the private language file—the one containing clean finish and the system's blind spot.

Rhodes had not guessed the phrases. He had received them through a door Wren built.

"She rationed it," Sasha said. "At first."

I nodded. "She gave him enough to keep him from releasing what he already had."

"Then something changed."

The change appeared as a compressed packet sent from the investigations firm to Wren's private relay. We could not read the message, but its name remained in the relay cache:

DELGADO_DISCOVERY_PACKAGE.

Twenty minutes after receiving it, Wren exported the Croft record.

"Blackmail," Sasha said.

"Probably."

"That doesn't absolve her."

"No."

It explained the progression. Wren had not joined Rhodes as a believer. She had tried to control his appetite with limited disclosures, telling herself each small betrayal prevented a larger one. When the pressure increased, she had crossed the line she could not uncross.

I pressed my thumb into my palm once, then made myself stop. Pain was not analysis.

"We need the package," I said.

Sasha pointed at the old archive terminal. "If we search her relay, she'll see us."

"She already knows the maintenance file was bait. Rhodes quoted it forty minutes after she opened it."

"Then why hasn't she run?"

The answer arrived as a system alert.

ADMINISTRATOR PURGE REQUESTED.

Wren was trying to erase the old archive.

Sasha disconnected the terminal from the network. The purge stalled at twelve percent. Most of what it touched was bait. The useful part was the request itself: Wren had signed it with the hidden recovery token, connecting her identity to the credential she had denied existed.

I called her.

She answered after five rings. Wind moved across the microphone.

"Where are you?" I asked.

"Out."

"You tried to purge the archive."

Silence.

"The old pier," I said. "Locker 317. The blind spot. He repeated all three."

Her breath caught, so quietly I might have missed it if I had wanted to.

"Nadia, listen to me."

"I am listening."

"You don't understand what he has."

"Then tell me."

A vehicle passed near her, tires hissing on wet pavement. "Not on this line."

"Come to Sasha's office."

"I can't."

"Won't."

"Can't." The word broke in the middle. "He watches the people I contact. He has a draft federal complaint with my name on it, intrusion logs from the defense contractor, every illegal access I made for you, and enough of the network to put Priya and Sasha beside me. He said if I stopped answering, he would file it."

There it was. Not redemption. Cause.

"How much did you give him?"

"At first? Schedules. Old routes. Things I thought he already knew. I kept the names out."

"Then Julian."

"He sent me the complaint with Sasha's home address and Priya's daughter's school attached." Her voice thinned. "I thought if I gave him one file, he'd leave them out."

"Men like Rhodes don't become satisfied."

"I know that now."

"Where are you?"

She did not answer.

"Wren."

"I left you the package. The real one. You taught me to keep insurance."

The call ended.

I dialed again. The number was dead.

Sasha had heard enough through the speaker. Her face had lost all expression.

"She chose for us," she said.

"Yes."

"She used my home and Priya's child to justify it."

"Yes."

Understanding did not soften the betrayal. It gave it boundaries. Wren had been coerced, but every time she opened the archive she had still made a choice about who else would bear the risk.

An email arrived on the new, isolated address Sasha and I had created that morning. No text. One attachment: a photograph of a bus-station locker key taped beneath the table where Wren had first shown me the archive architecture.

Under the photograph, she had written:

I kept one thing from him.

We did not go immediately. We finished the migration first.

By noon, the old archive contained nothing but false schedules and sterile case summaries. By one, every real credential was dead. By two, Sasha's steel box held the only working copy of the network index.

Only then did we leave for the locker.
