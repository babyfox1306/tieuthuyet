Before dawn, Sasha moved me from the hunting cabin to our condemned one-bedroom safe house in the city. The needle went through my skin with a small, wet sound as gray light reached the window, six hours after Rhodes had photographed me above the curve and driven untouched through my trap. I'd cleaned the wound at the cabin. Now I had enough light to close it properly.

I pulled the thread tight, watching the edges of the cut come together. Shallow, but deep enough to leave a scar. The kind of scar that asked questions if anyone saw it. A branch had opened my forearm while I scrambled away from the ridge. I hadn't felt it until Rhodes's taillights were gone.

I counted the locks again, though I knew each one was set.

The safe house was a one-bedroom in a building that had been condemned twice and ignored three times. No heat. No hot water. No neighbors who asked questions. I'd paid cash for six months upfront under a name that belonged to a woman who died in 2019. She didn't need it anymore. I did.

I finished the stitch, tied it off, and cut the thread with my teeth. The curved needle from my sewing kit sat on the coffee table next to a roll of sterile gauze. Same type of needle I'd used after scraping my hand at Julian Croft's marina about three years ago. Not the same needle, exactly. Same type. Same curve. Same ritual of control.

I wrapped the gauze around my forearm and taped it in place. The bleeding had stopped. The shaking hadn't.

Victor Rhodes had known I was coming.

The meeting message explained why he knew the curve and timing. It did not explain why he knew about the scored fitting or exactly where I would stand to watch. He had stopped beneath the ridge long enough to photograph me, then left without calling the police. The cut on my arm was mine; the failure belonged to someone else. Someone had given him the plan.

Now I had a wound I could stitch and a breach I couldn't see.

I picked up my phone. The screen was cracked — must have happened during the fall — but it still worked. I dialed Sasha Okafor.

She answered on the second ring. "Tell me you're not calling from a hospital."

"Safe house," I said. "I'm closing the cut from the ridge. I'll live."

A pause. I could hear her breathing, calculating.

"Stitches," she repeated. "That bad?"

"Could have been worse. He knew."

"Knew what?"

"Everything. The route. The scored fitting. My observation point." I leaned back against the threadbare couch, staring at the water stain on the ceiling. "He stopped and took my picture, Sasha. Then he let me run."

Another pause. Longer this time.

"Then he doesn't have proof," she said finally. "If he had proof, you'd be in a cell right now. He's playing. Testing."

"He's already tested me. I failed."

"You're alive. That's not failing."

I didn't answer. The needle was still on the coffee table. I picked it up, turned it over in my fingers. The curve caught the light.

"I need to know my legal exposure," I said. "If he goes to the police, what happens?"

"With what evidence? A photograph of you above a public road? He can make it ugly, but he cannot prove you touched his car without exposing the private surveillance he used to follow you." Sasha's voice was sharp, precise. "He wants leverage he can control. A police case would take that control away."

"Unless he has something else."

"Does he?"

I thought about the voicemail he'd left me three days ago. The one with phrasing from our private archive. The photograph from the ridge proved he was no longer merely in my head.

"He knows things he shouldn't," I said.

"What things?"

"Things I never said out loud."

"Then you have a leak."

The word sat between us like a third person in the room. A leak. Someone in my network. Someone who knew my patterns, my timing, my notes.

"Don't say that," I said.

"I have to say it. You know I have to say it." Sasha's voice softened, just a fraction. "You need to pause. Take a breath. Let the dust settle."

"I can't pause."

"You can, or you'll make a mistake. You already made one tonight."

The truth burned, but it was still the truth. I'd been too confident. Too sure that Victor Rhodes was just another target. I'd forgotten that he hunted too.

"I'll think about it," I said.

"Don't think. Do."

We hung up. I waited a long moment, then dialed Wren Delgado.

She answered without greeting. "I've been tracking your phone pings. You're not where you're supposed to be."

"I'm in the safe house."

"Which one?"

"The one we don't talk about."

A beat. "Right. That one."

"The GPS spoofing script. Did it work?"

"It self-deleted at 11:47 PM. Complete wipe. No trace." A pause. "But there's something you need to know."

"Tell me."

"Victor Rhodes's phone pinged a tower near your apartment at 11:43 PM. The same tower you were spoofing from."

I closed my eyes. Mutual vulnerability. He'd been tracking me while I was tracking him. Two hunters circling the same prey, except the prey was each other.

"He knows where I live," I said.

"He knows where you were. And he knows the spoofing script existed, even if he can't prove it was yours." Wren's voice was steady, but I caught the edge underneath. "You're exposed, Nadia. Not fatally. But exposed."

"Can you scrub the tower data?"

"Already tried. It's encrypted on the carrier side. I'd need physical access to the server farm, and I'm not doing that for a ping that lasts four seconds."

"Then we live with it."

"We live with it. But you need to be careful. He's not just watching you. He's watching everyone who touches you."

I thought about Priya. About Sasha. About the network that had held together for nearly three years without a single exposed operation. One leak, and it all came apart.

"One more thing," I said. "I need you to pull something from storage."

"Storage" was a code word for the unit where I'd sealed the Croft folder about three years ago and left it untouched.

"Which box?"

"The one with the Julian Croft file."

Silence. Then: "You sure?"

"I need the photograph. The one of the yacht."

"I'll have it at the drop point in two hours."

"Thank you, Wren."

"Don't thank me. Just stay alive."

She hung up.

I sat in the dark for two hours, watching the crack in my phone screen shift as the battery drained. The wound on my arm throbbed, a dull ache that kept me grounded. Every time I closed my eyes, I saw Victor Rhodes beneath the ridge, phone raised, wearing that calm, almost gentle smile.

He'd been faster. But I'd been smarter. And smart meant planning for failure.

When the doorbell rang, I didn't answer it. I waited. Three rings, then silence. I looked out the window and saw a plain envelope taped to the railing of the fire escape.

I retrieved it, brought it inside, and tore it open.

The photograph slid out. Glossy. 8x10. Julian Croft's yacht docked at Marina del Rey. The one whose fuel-line coupling I'd loosened about three years ago, staging a fire that looked like an accident.

I held it up to the light.

The fuel line was visible in the corner of the frame. A pale crescent marked the brass coupling where my wrench had slipped as I broke its seal. Anyone comparing the photograph to the recovered part would have a physical link to deliberate tampering.

I'd kept this photograph as insurance. A reminder of what I'd done, and what I could do again.

Now it was a contingency.

I called Sasha back.

"I need you to come to the safe house," I said. "There's something I need you to hold."

"Hold where?"

"Somewhere safe. Somewhere that isn't me."

An hour later, Sasha walked through the door. She took one look at my arm and didn't say a word. Just sat down across from me, her eyes on the envelope on the coffee table.

"What's in it?"

"A photograph. Of Julian Croft's yacht."

Her jaw tightened. "Why do I need that?"

"Because if I'm arrested. Or killed. I want you to release it. To the police. To the press. To whoever asks."

"This is your first kill."

"It's my only kill that leaves a trail." I slid the envelope across the table. "If Victor Rhodes comes for me, I need someone to make sure he doesn't come alone."

Sasha took the envelope. Held it like it was weighted with everything I'd done and everything I'd lost.

"You understand what this means," she said. "If I release this, you go down."

"If I'm already dead, it doesn't matter."

"And if you're alive?"

"Then I burn. But I take him with me."

She sealed the envelope, tucked it into her bag, and stood. "I'll keep it safe."

"I know you will."

She walked to the door. Paused. Looked back.

"Be careful, Nadia. He's not like the others."

"I know."

She left.

The door clicked shut. The lock engaged. And I was alone.

I sat back down on the couch after Sasha took the photograph. The crescent mark remained in my mind, a clue I'd left behind without knowing it, a thread that could unravel everything.

I let the silence settle before I moved.

Victor Rhodes had made his move. He'd exposed my trap, photographed me, and let me go. The simplest explanation was leverage: he wanted the photograph under his control and wanted me to wonder how he'd known.

And now he was waiting.

I looked at my phone. No messages. No calls. No voicemails.

The silence was worse than a threat.
