The new season was called "The One Who Walked." And that day, I was going to talk about Elise Marchetti.

I leaned into the microphone, my voice carrying a promise I intended to keep. The recording light glowed red in the dim studio — a converted storage room in my apartment, soundproofed with foam panels that Sasha's brother had installed the previous year.

"Elise was twenty-eight years old. She worked at a bookstore in Portland, lived alone with a cat named Miso, and on the night of October 14th, 2020, she was last seen leaving her shift at nine-thirty-seven PM."

I paused. Let the silence breathe.

"Her body was found three days later in Forest Park. Strangled. The medical examiner noted defensive wounds, evidence of a struggle, and something else — something the police never released to the public."

That's the hook. The unreleased detail. Sasha found it buried in the original case file, a single sentence buried in paragraph fourteen of the detective's narrative. A detail that only the killer would know.

"The prime suspect was identified within forty-eight hours. A man named Victor Rhodes. He was questioned, released, and never charged. The case went cold. Elise's parents are still waiting for answers."

I let my voice drop slightly, the way I'd practiced.

"This season, we're going to find those answers. Together."

I reached over and tapped the spacebar. The recording software clicked off. I sat back and let the silence return.

The first episode would drop the next day. By then, Victor Rhodes would know I was coming for him.

Good.

A text from Sasha appeared: "We're ready. Same place."

I grabbed my jacket and headed out.

---

The secure location was a basement office beneath a laundromat in Southeast Portland. The machines rattled overhead, a constant white noise that drowned out listening devices. Sasha called it "the boiler room." She was the only one who knew the landlord, and the landlord thought it was a storage unit.

I descended the concrete stairs and pushed through the steel door.

Sasha was already at the table, her ledger open in front of her. The leather-bound book was thick with names and dates, each entry a guilty man who walked free. That night, the newest entry was written in red ink: "Victor Rhodes. Elise Marchetti. October 2020."

Priya Anand sat to her left, a coffee cup cradled in her hands. She was the medical examiner for Multnomah County, which meant she had access to every autopsy report, every toxicology screen, every sealed file. She had altered Marcus Webb's certification after my second kill, turning a suspicious cardiac event into a natural one.

Wren Delgado was in the corner, three laptops open, fingers flying across a keyboard. She was the youngest of us, twenty-seven, with sleeves of tattoos that disappeared into her hoodie. She could pull a person's entire digital footprint in under an hour. She had also built the encrypted archive that held my research and operational notes. I owned the root key; she administered everything beneath it.

"Ladies," I said, closing the door behind me. "We have a problem."

Sasha looked up. "The podcast?"

"The podcast is the solution. The problem is that Victor Rhodes is smart, connected, and he's been untouchable for six years."

Priya set down her coffee. "You think he'll come after you."

"I know he will. The question is when."

Wren didn't look up from her screens. "He's already searching. I set a ping on his name, his known aliases, his attorney's firm. Someone searched 'Nadia Cole true crime' about twenty minutes ago from an IP in Lake Oswego."

That was where he lived. A gated community with a view of the river.

"He's paying attention," I said.

"He'd be stupid not to," Sasha replied. She flipped open her ledger to a page near the middle. "I pulled the original case file. The detective who handled it retired three years ago, but he kept copies. Unofficial ones."

She slid a manila folder across the table. I opened it.

The first page was the crime scene report. Photos of Forest Park, yellow tape, evidence markers. Elise's body covered with a white sheet. I'd seen dozens of these, but that one hit differently. That one was personal.

"There's something in here," Sasha said, tapping her finger on the page. "Paragraph fourteen. The detective noted that the victim's hands were bound with a specific type of cord — a braided nylon rope, burned at the ends. That detail was never released to the press. The family doesn't even know."

I read the line. It was clinical, almost an afterthought. But it was the kind of detail that separated the killer from the curious.

"If Victor Rhodes knows that detail," I said slowly, "then we have him."

"Only if he admits it," Priya said. "And he won't."

"He might," Wren said, finally looking up. "If I can find the right pressure point. He's got a shell company, a trust fund, and a girlfriend who doesn't know about his hobbies. He also has a brother in Seattle who handles his finances."

"His brother knows?"

"His brother handles the money. Whether he knows where the money came from is a different question."

I closed the folder. "We need more. We need something that ties him directly to the scene."

Sasha leaned forward. "There's a detail in the forensic report. The cord used to bind her wrists — it was a specific brand. Military surplus. Only sold at three stores in the Pacific Northwest."

"And?"

"And one of those stores is in Lake Oswego. The owner keeps paper receipts that never get digitized."

I felt the thread tighten. "Can you get the receipt?"

"I can try. But I need time."

"Then take it. We have until the first episode drops."

Wren's phone vibrated. She glanced at it, then frowned.

"What?"

"Someone just accessed my dummy server. The one I set up to track who's looking at us."

"Victor Rhodes?"

"No. Someone using a VPN routed through Singapore. But they're good — they found the server in under an hour."

I felt a cold trickle down my spine. "He has his own network."

"Looks like it."

The overhead washing machine rattled into its spin cycle, a violent shudder that shook the light fixture. We sat in silence for a moment, each of us processing the implications.

"Okay," I said finally. "We proceed with the podcast. We release the first episode tomorrow. We let him know we're not afraid."

"And what do we do when he responds?" Priya asked.

"We respond harder."

Sasha closed her ledger. "I'll work on the receipt. Wren, keep tracking his digital footprint. Priya, I need you to look at the original autopsy — see if there's anything else that was buried."

Priya nodded. "I'll pull it tonight."

I stood up. "Good. I'll handle the rest."

As I headed for the door, Sasha called my name. I turned.

"Nadia. Be careful. This one is different."

"I know."

I was back in my apartment by nine PM. The first episode was scheduled to upload at midnight. I'd already written the script, recorded the narration, edited the audio. All that was left was to press publish.

I poured myself a glass of water and sat down at my desk. The folder from Sasha was open in front of me, the crime scene photos spread across the surface. I studied them the way a painter studied a masterpiece—looking for the brushstrokes that revealed the artist's hand.

Elise Marchetti had been strangled. The ligature marks suggested the cord had been pulled from behind, a method for controlling a victim. There were no signs of sexual assault, which made a sexual motive less likely without proving what the killer wanted.

Victor Rhodes was forty-one at the time. He had no prior record and no documented history of violence. A degraded male profile beneath one fingernail was consistent with him but not exclusive; a camera near the park caught a car matching his, not a readable plate. The evidence was enough to keep him a suspect, not enough to charge him after witnesses recanted and records disappeared.

The system failed Elise Marchetti.

I wouldn't.

My phone rang from an unknown number. I watched the caller ID flash three times before it went to voicemail.

I waited.

A few seconds later, the notification appeared: "1 new voicemail."

I tapped the icon and put the phone to my ear.

The voice was calm, measured, almost friendly. Deep, with a slight gravel to it. The kind of voice that could sell you a car or convince you to open your front door at midnight.

"Hello, Nadia."

I froze.

"I've been looking forward to this."

The line went dead.

I lowered the phone. The number was blocked. No name, no callback option. But I knew who it was.

Victor Rhodes.

He knew my name. He knew about the podcast. And he wasn't running.

He was hunting.

I pressed publish on the first episode. The upload bar filled, then vanished. The first shot had been fired.

Now I had to survive the war.
