"They responded within three hours, Nadia. Three hours. That's not a coincidence — that's a tip-off."

Sasha's voice was flat, clinical, but I heard the accusation underneath. Someone in my network had sold me out. The question was who.

I pressed my phone harder against my ear, pacing the length of the safe house's narrow kitchen. The linoleum was cold through my socks. "Walk me through it again. Every detail."

"I filed the fake lawsuit at 9:47 this morning. Named a shell corporation, used the burner email, routed through three VPNs. Standard procedure." Sasha paused. I heard papers shuffling on her end. She was in her office, probably with the door locked, speaking too quietly for her paralegal to hear. "By 12:55, Rhodes's legal team had filed a motion to dismiss. Not merely received it—filed it. That means they drafted it, reviewed it, and submitted it in three hours."

"Fast legal work isn't proof of a leak."

"Fast legal work on a Friday before a holiday weekend is proof of a leak." Sasha's voice sharpened. "I've been doing this for fifteen years. No firm turns around a motion that fast unless they knew it was coming. Someone told them to watch for it."

I stopped pacing. "Who knew about the lawsuit?"

"You, me, Wren, Priya. That's it."

"Priya doesn't know the details. I only told her I was running a distraction."

"Then Priya knows you're running something. And she knows who you're running it against. She's smart enough to connect dots." Sasha's voice softened, just barely. "I'm not accusing anyone. I'm telling you what the timeline means. You asked me to watch for irregularities. This is one."

I closed my eyes. The safe house smelled like bleach and old coffee. The windows were covered with cheap blinds that did nothing to block the afternoon light. I'd been here four days. Four days of waiting, of watching, of running scenarios in my head. And now this.

"Thank you, Sasha. I need you to do something else."

"Name it."

"Pull every communication you've had with me in the last two weeks. Emails, texts, calls. Check for anything unusual. Any message that feels off, any response that seems like it came from someone who knew too much."

"You think someone's reading our conversations."

"I think Victor Rhodes knows more than he should. And I think he's been getting information from somewhere inside my circle." I let that sit. "If it's not you, and it's not me, and it's not Priya, then it's Wren. And if it's Wren, we have a much bigger problem."

"Because Wren controls the digital infrastructure."

"Because Wren built the encryption protocols. She knows every back door, every vulnerability, every access point. If she's compromised, then everything we've ever done is exposed."

Sasha was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice was heavy. "You need to talk to her."

"I know."

"Call me after. I'll be here."

I hung up and looked at the phone in my hand. The screen was smudged with fingerprints. I wiped it against my jeans, but the marks only spread.

I scrolled to Wren's contact. My finger hovered over the call button.

Wren Delgado. Twenty-seven years old. Hired by a defense contractor at twenty-two, fired at twenty-four for "unauthorized system exploration" — which was corporate speak for "we caught her poking around files she shouldn't have seen." She'd been freelancing ever since. Encrypted everything. Used aliases for her aliases. Paranoid to the point of functional, which was exactly what I needed.

I called.

She answered on the first ring. "Tell me you're calling with good news."

"Are you alone?"

A pause. The sound of a door closing. "I'm in my workspace. It's secure. What's going on?"

"Sasha filed the fake lawsuit this morning. Rhodes's team responded in three hours. They were waiting for it."

"Shit."

"Yeah. Shit."

Another pause. Longer this time. I heard keys clicking in the background. Wren's habit—she typed when she was thinking, like the motion helped her process. "You think there's a leak."

"I think the timing is too perfect. I need you to check your logs. Everything. Access, data transfers, unusual activity over the last three days."

"Already on it." The clicking intensified. Then stopped. "Nadia."

"What?"

"I found something. I didn't want to mention it because I thought it was a glitch, but..." She trailed off.

"But what?"

"There were unusual access logs on my system at three AM this morning. Someone accessed the encrypted file server. Used my credentials."

"Your credentials?"

"My password. My biometrics. Everything matched." Her voice was tight, defensive. "But I was asleep at three AM. I have a sleep tracker on my watch. It proves I was in bed."

"Could someone have cloned your biometrics?"

"It's possible. Unlikely, but possible. Or..." She stopped.

"Or what?"

"Or someone has inside access. Someone who knows my systems better than I do."

"Who would that be?"

"No one. I built everything from scratch. There's no back door I don't know about." But she didn't sound sure. She sounded scared.

"Wren. Look at me."

"I can't look at you. You're on the phone."

"Then listen to me. I'm not accusing you of anything. But I need you to be honest with me. Is there anyone who could have accessed your system without your knowledge?"

She was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was small. "There's one person. The contractor who helped me build the initial encryption framework. Before I started working with you. He knows the architecture."

"Can you contact him?"

"I can try. But we didn't part on good terms. He might not talk to me."

"Do it anyway. And change your credentials. Everything. New passwords, new biometrics, new encryption keys. Assume everything you've used before is compromised."

"That will take hours."

"Then start now."

I hung up before she could argue. My hand was shaking. I set the phone on the counter and waited for it to steady.

One name down. Two to go.

I called Priya.

She answered on the third ring. Her voice was breathless, rushed. "Nadia. I was just about to call you."

"What's wrong?"

"The audit. They expanded the second pass to every case file I've signed in the last eighteen months." She paused. I heard her breathing hard, like she was walking fast. "They're going to find the altered certification on Marcus Webb. It's only a matter of time."

"Priya. Slow down. Tell me exactly what happened."

"The auditor came back this morning with a new list. No warning about the expanded scope." Her voice cracked. "I tried to stall. I said I needed to organize the files. But he insisted on starting immediately. He's already through the first quarter of the year."

"Did you ask for a lawyer?"

"I can't ask for a lawyer without looking guilty."

"You are guilty, Priya. We both are. The question is whether we can buy enough time to finish what we started."

She was quiet. I heard her stop walking. When she spoke again, her voice was barely above a whisper. "Nadia, I can't do this. I can't keep lying to their faces. The detective who came last week — he looked at me like he knew. Like he was waiting for me to slip."

"You didn't slip. You held it together."

"Barely. I held it together barely." She was crying now. I could hear it in her voice. "I have a daughter, Nadia. I have a life. If this comes out, I lose everything."

"It won't come out. I won't let it."

"You can't promise that."

"I'm promising it anyway." I softened my voice, just a fraction. "Priya, I need you to hold the line. Just a little longer. Can you do that for me?"

She sniffled. "I'll try."

"That's all I'm asking."

I ended the call and stood in the middle of the safe house, staring at the wall.

Three names. Sasha Okafor. Wren Delgado. Priya Anand.

One of them was feeding information to Victor Rhodes.

I ran through every interaction I'd had with them in the last two weeks. Every phone call, every text, every meeting. I replayed conversations in my head, looking for inconsistencies. For hesitations. For moments that felt wrong.

Sasha had been solid. Professional. She'd spotted the fast response immediately and flagged it. That didn't feel like a betrayal — that felt like loyalty.

Wren had been defensive. Evasive. She'd admitted to a security breach but framed it as a glitch until I pushed. And she'd mentioned a former contractor who knew her systems. Convenient, maybe. A ready-made scapegoat.

Priya was falling apart. Scared. Emotional. Vulnerable. A leak doesn't cry on the phone — a leak stays calm, stays collected, stays in control. But Priya's fear was real. I'd heard it in every word.

Unless the fear was an act. Unless she was crying to throw me off.

I closed my eyes until color flared behind them.

I couldn't trust my own instincts anymore. That was the worst part. Paranoia had poisoned everything. Every word, every gesture, every silence — I was reading them all through the lens of suspicion.

I picked up my phone again and scrolled through my contacts.

Sasha Okafor.
Priya Anand.
Wren Delgado.

Three names. One betrayer.

I whispered to the empty room: "I built this network to protect me. Now it's the thing that will destroy me."

The words hung in the air, heavy and final. I set the phone down on the counter. It glowed faintly, waiting for my next move.

I didn't know what that move was.

I couldn't trust anyone. I couldn't walk away from the network. I couldn't stop hunting Victor Rhodes without losing everything I'd built.

I pictured Victor watching and smiling because he had already won. The word "probably" belonged in that sentence, and paranoia kept trying to erase it.

I told myself he'd turned my own people against me without threats, bribes, or blackmail—just by being smarter and more patient, by letting me dig my own grave. It was a theory built from fear, not evidence.

The phone buzzed. A text from Wren.

"Found something. Call me."

I read the message twice, then called her back.

Because what else could I do?
