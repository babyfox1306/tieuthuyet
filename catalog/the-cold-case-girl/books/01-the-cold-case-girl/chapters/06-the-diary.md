The box was heavy. Not because of what was inside, but because of what I knew I'd find.

Sasha's storage room smelled like old paper and dust, the kind of smell that settles into furniture after years of case files and client records. She'd offered to bring the box to my apartment, but I'd said no. Some things you have to retrieve yourself, like you're collecting pieces of a crime scene you don't want anyone else to contaminate.

"The locks are original," Sasha said from the doorway. "I had it in evidence storage for three years before I realized what it was. The department was going to destroy it."

I ran my hand over the cardboard, feeling the tape residue where someone had sealed it and resealed it multiple times. "You didn't tell me you had this."

"I wasn't sure you were ready." Her voice was careful, the way people get when they're walking around a wound they can see but can't touch. "And I wasn't sure I wanted to be the one to give it to you."

I lifted the box. It was lighter than I expected, which made it heavier.

"I'll be in my office if you need me," Sasha said, and retreated.

I carried the box to a small conference room at the end of the hall. The blinds were drawn, the only light coming from a single lamp on the table. I set the box down and stood there, unable to open it.

Mara's handwriting was on the side. Small, neat letters in black marker: "M. Cole — Personal Effects."

She'd written it herself. Probably during one of her moves, when she was packing up her life and labeling boxes like she was organizing someone else's belongings. She always did that. Made everything look orderly even when chaos was spilling out of every corner.

I opened the box.

The smell hit me first. Not decay—the police had kept it in proper storage—but the smell of Mara's apartment. That specific combination of lavender incense, old books, and the vanilla candle she always burned. I'd forgotten that smell. I'd buried it so deep that finding it again felt like a physical blow.

Inside were the things you'd expect. A few books with dog-eared pages. A sweater I'd given her for her birthday, still folded. A photo album with a cracked spine. A small jewelry box that I knew held the silver necklace our mother had left us.

And a diary.

It was a simple notebook, the kind you buy at any drugstore. The cover was soft blue, worn at the edges, the spiral binding bent from being shoved into bags. I pulled it out slowly, my fingers trembling in a way I couldn't control.

The entries started six months before she died.

I sat down, the chair scraping against the floor. I opened to the first page.

"January 14. I think someone's watching the apartment. Probably just my imagination. I've been watching too many true crime docs."

I turned the page.

"January 22. Saw him again today. Same car, different street. I'm not being paranoid. I know what I saw."

My throat tightened. I flipped forward, scanning entries about work, about a guy she was seeing, about the stray cat she'd been feeding. Normal things. Mundane things. The daily life of a woman who didn't know she had less than a year to live.

Then I found it.

"March 3. A developer came to the house today. Nice suit, nice car. Said he was doing a survey of the neighborhood for a new project. Asked a lot of questions. How long had I lived here? Did I live alone? Did I have a roommate? I didn't like the way he looked at me. I told him I had a boyfriend who was a cop. He left after that."

"Later: I looked up the company he said he worked for. It doesn't exist."

I read the entry three times. Each time, the words settled deeper, like stones dropping into water.

A developer. A fake company. A man who asked questions about her living situation.

Julian Croft.

He'd been casing her. He'd been scouting her, learning her routine, finding the right moment. And she'd known something was wrong. She'd felt it, that prickle of instinct that tells you when a predator is circling.

But she hadn't called me.

She hadn't told me.

I pressed my fingertips against the page, feeling the indentations where her pen had pressed too hard. She'd written "doesn't exist" in darker ink, like she'd underlined it twice.

I closed the diary. My hand was shaking.

The grief came in a wave, not the neat kind that lets you cry and move on, but the kind that knots in your chest and makes it hard to breathe. I doubled over, my forehead touching the table, the diary still clutched in my hand.

She knew. She'd known something was wrong, and she'd handled it on her own, because that's what we both did. We handled things alone. We didn't want to worry each other. We thought we were protecting each other by staying quiet.

And it had gotten her killed.

I stayed like that for a long time, breathing through the ache, letting the grief wash over me and then recede. When I straightened, my eyes were dry. The tears would come later, in private, when I was alone in my apartment with no one to see.

I put the diary back in the box and closed the flaps.

I had what I needed.

Back in the conference room, I closed the box and carried it to my car. The diary was in my jacket pocket, close to my chest, like a second heartbeat.

I drove home in silence, the streets empty, the city lights blurring past. My fingers kept a restless rhythm on the steering wheel.

When I got to my apartment, I set the box on the kitchen table and pulled out the diary again. I opened to the March 3 entry and read it one more time.

"A developer came to the house."

He'd been there. He'd seen her. He'd asked his questions and left, and she'd known something was wrong, but she'd gone on with her life because what else could she do? You can't live in fear every day. You'd go insane.

But he'd come back. He'd found his moment.

And he'd killed her.

I closed the diary and set it aside. The room felt too quiet.

Victor Rhodes had already shown me he was digging into my past, looking for cracks, looking for leverage. I didn't yet know who he had hired or how far the search had reached. I only knew he thought he could find something that would make me back off.

He didn't know me.

He didn't know what I'd already survived.

I pulled up the Elise Marchetti case file on my laptop and started working. The diary entry was a clue, a piece of the puzzle, but it wasn't the whole picture. I needed more. I needed to understand how Victor Rhodes operated, how he chose his victims, how he covered his tracks.

I needed to find the evidence that would put him away.

Or the evidence that would justify what I was going to do to him.

The night stretched on, and I kept working. The diary sat next to my laptop, its cover soft and worn, a reminder of what I'd lost and what I was fighting for.

I didn't stop until the sun came up.
