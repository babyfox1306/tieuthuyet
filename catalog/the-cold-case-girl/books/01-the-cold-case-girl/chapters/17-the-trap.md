The car hit the curve at exactly 11:18 PM. I held my breath, waiting for the screech of metal, the splash of water. Instead, the car slowed. Smoothly. Deliberately. Then Victor Rhodes stepped out and looked straight toward the trees where I had hidden.

The night air went cold in my lungs.

I was thirty yards up the ridge, wedged between two pines, the GPS spoofing script still running on my phone. The brake fitting I had scored in the club garage should have failed under the pressure of that curve. I was supposed to hear metal, airbags, water—a quiet end to a man who had arranged Elise Marchetti's death and walked free.

Instead, Rhodes stood on the asphalt, hands in his coat pockets, his silhouette sharp against the headlights. He turned his head slowly, scanning the treeline like he was taking in a view.

Like he knew exactly where I was standing.

My thumb pressed into my left palm. Hard.

He raised his phone.

The flash was distant but unmistakable—a white burst that seared through the darkness and caught me in the lens. I didn't flinch. Couldn't. My feet were rooted to the frozen ground, every instinct screaming at me to move, but my body had locked into that moment.

He knew.

The meeting message had given him the ridge and the time. It had not given him the scored fitting or my observation point.

The phone came down. Rhodes tilted his head—that signature gesture I'd studied in a hundred hours of footage—and smiled. Not a smirk. Not a challenge. A calm, patient acknowledgment, like a chess player watching an opponent walk into a trap they'd seen coming from the opening move.

Then he got back in his car and drove away.

The taillights disappeared around the next bend, and I was alone in the dark with the sound of my own breathing and the cut on my arm that I hadn't noticed until now—a deep gash from a branch I'd pushed past when I scrambled into position. Blood soaked through my sleeve, warm and wet.

I forced myself to think.

The brake line had not even softened.

That meant one of two things. Either Rhodes had inspected the car and replaced the scored fitting, or I had worked on a decoy vehicle in the garage. Both required him to know the method before he drove to North Ridge.

I didn't have time to figure out which.

The safe house was six miles through the woods, an old hunting cabin that belonged to one of Sasha's clients—abandoned for years, off the grid, no paper trail. I had stashed a bag there two weeks ago. Change of clothes. Cash. A burner phone.

I couldn't take my car. He had my license plate, my face, and probably a tracker on the vehicle by now. I had to move on foot.

The first mile was survival. Branches tore at my face. The gash on my arm pulsed with every heartbeat, and I wrapped it with a torn strip from my shirt, cinching it tight enough to slow the bleeding. The moon was half-hidden behind clouds, and I navigated by memory, by the shape of the ridge line I'd studied on maps for three weeks.

The second mile was calculation.

Rhodes had a photograph. A clear, identifiable image of me standing on a ridge with my phone out, in the middle of nowhere, at the exact moment his car approached a curve that could have killed him. That photograph was evidence. It was leverage. It was a message.

But it wasn't a threat.

Not yet.

If he wanted me arrested, he would have sent the photo to the police before he drove away. Instead, he sent a text.

I felt the buzz through my jacket at mile three. I stopped, pulled out my phone, and saw his name on the screen.

"Victor Rhodes: I told you I studied people, Nadia. Better luck next time."

I read it three times. Each word was perfectly chosen. No anger. No triumph. Just a calm acknowledgment that he had seen through me, and that this was now a game he intended to win.

I kept moving.

Mile four. My legs were shaking. The adrenaline was wearing off, and the cold was seeping into my bones. The cut on my arm had stopped bleeding, but the makeshift bandage was soaked through, and I could feel the wound sticking to the fabric with every step.

Mile five. I saw the cabin.

It was a dark shape in a clearing, roof sagging, windows boarded. I had the key on a chain around my neck—a detail that felt paranoid three weeks ago, but now felt like the only smart thing I'd done all night.

I unlocked the door, slipped inside, and locked it behind me.

The air smelled like dust and rot. I didn't turn on a light. I stood in the dark, back against the door, listening to the silence. No footsteps. No car engines. No helicopters. Just the wind and my own ragged breathing.

I pulled out my phone. No service. Good. That meant no tracking.

I found a candle in my stash bag, lit it, and sat on the floor. The flame threw long shadows across the walls. I unwrapped my arm and examined the wound. Deep, but not arterial. I poured antiseptic over it—the burn was sharp and grounding—and wrapped it properly with gauze.

Then I called Sasha.

She picked up on the first ring. "Nadia. Tell me you're safe."

"The trap failed. He knew."

A pause. "Knew what?"

"The method. My exact position above the curve." I took a breath. "He took a photograph of me, Sasha. He has visual evidence of me at the scene."

"Did he call the police?"

"No. He sent a text. Taunting me."

"Then he's playing a longer game. He wants to control the narrative, not hand it to law enforcement."

"I know." I shut my eyes for a beat. "I need you to check Priya. Make sure she's secure. If Rhodes knows about me, he might know about her too."

"I'll call her now. What about Wren?"

I hesitated. The thought had crossed my mind—the possibility that the leak came from inside the network. But I couldn't afford to go there yet. Not without proof. Paranoia would tear us apart faster than Rhodes ever could.

"Just check on her. See if she's noticed anything unusual on her end."

"I'll handle it. Where are you?"

"Safe house beta. I'll stay here until I figure out the next move."

"Keep your head down. I'll call you in an hour."

She hung up.

I sat in the candlelight, staring at my phone. The text from Rhodes was still on the screen. "Better luck next time."

I closed the thread.

Then a new message appeared.

"Victor Rhodes sent an attachment."

I didn't want to open it. I knew what it was. But I had to see.

I tapped the screen.

The photograph loaded slowly—the connection was weak, the pixels assembling in chunks. First the outline of the trees. Then the shape of a figure. Then a face.

My face.

He had caught me mid-motion, phone raised, eyes fixed on the road below. The lighting was harsh, the shadows deep. I looked exactly like what I was: a woman in the dark, waiting for something terrible to happen.

His caption appeared beneath the image.

"For my files. See you soon, Nadia."

I dropped the phone.

It clattered against the wooden floor, and I didn't pick it up. I sat there, watching the candle flame while the cut on my arm bled through the gauze.

I didn't move.
