The notification lit up my phone at 4:17 AM. By 4:19, I knew Victor Rhodes had made his first public move.

I was already awake. I'd been awake for hours, replaying the voicemail until his words seemed to settle into my bones like frost. Sleep had been impossible. Every time I closed my eyes, I saw his face—not because I knew what he looked like, but because I could imagine it. Calm. Patient. Smiling.

The notification was from Sasha. A link. No context. Just a URL from a local news site I'd never heard of.

I opened it.

The headline hit me like a fist to the chest.

"Podcaster's Sister Was Murdered—Acquitted Suspect Died a Year Later. Is Justice Still Being Served?"

I read the first paragraph sitting up, my back pressed against the headboard, the phone's glow the only light in the room.

"Nadia Cole, host of the true crime podcast The Unheard, has been building a case against former prosecutor Victor Rhodes for the murder of Elise Marchetti. But what her listeners may not know is that Cole's younger sister, Mara Cole, was murdered four years ago—and the man acquitted of that murder, Julian Croft, died in a boating fire about a year later."

The article cited an anonymous source close to the investigation.

I stopped breathing.

"Sources say Cole's crusade against Rhodes may be driven by personal trauma rather than objective journalism. The question remains: can a victim's family member be trusted to deliver unbiased reporting?"

I read it again. Then a third time.

The phrasing was precise. Controlled. It didn't accuse me of anything directly, but it didn't have to. The implication was clear: I was compromised. My podcast was a vendetta disguised as journalism. Everything I'd built was suddenly suspect.

And Victor Rhodes had done it without getting his hands dirty.

I held still until the spike of adrenaline sharpened into focus.

I called Sasha.

She answered on the first ring, her voice low and controlled. "You saw it."

"I saw it."

"It went live at 3:52 AM. I got a ping from my media monitor at four. I've been reading it for the last fifteen minutes."

"Who wrote it?"

"Byline is Jessica Tran. Freelancer. She's done pieces for the local alt-weekly before. Nothing major. This is the biggest story she's ever landed."

"Which means someone fed it to her."

"Someone with access," Sasha said. "And someone who knew exactly what buttons to push."

I swung my legs over the side of the bed, the floor cold against my bare feet. The apartment was dark, the curtains drawn, the only light coming from my phone. "The article mentions the boating accident. That detail was in the report Sasha pulled from the Marchetti box. Rhodes has seen the same file."

"Or someone in his network pulled it for him. He was a prosecutor, Nadia. He knows how to access case files. He probably still has contacts inside the system."

"But he didn't go to the police. He went to the press."

"Because this is smarter," Sasha said. "Police would require evidence. Evidence he doesn't have. But the press only requires a reasonable suspicion and an anonymous source. He doesn't need to prove I killed Julian Croft. He just needs to make people wonder."

I stood up and walked to the window, pulling the curtain aside just enough to see the street below. Empty. Dark. The city was still sleeping, unaware that my world was collapsing in slow motion.

"What's the legal risk?"

Sasha was quiet for a moment. I could hear her typing in the background, probably pulling up the article on her own screen. "The article is protected speech. It doesn't accuse you of anything directly. It just raises questions. That's not defamation. It's journalism, even if the intent is malicious."

"But more could follow."

"Almost certainly. This is a test. He's seeing how you react. If you go quiet, he'll assume he's hit a nerve. If you respond publicly, he'll use your response to build the next piece. Either way, he's controlling the narrative."

"What do you recommend?"

I heard her exhale. A long, slow breath that told me she was thinking carefully. "We go dark on communications. No emails unless they're encrypted. No phone calls about anything sensitive. If Rhodes is watching you, he's probably watching your network too. The less we say, the less he can use."

"Priya has an audit coming up."

"I know."

"Wren has been pulling financial records for the past three months. If anyone traces those inquiries back to us—"

"Then we're done," Sasha said flatly. "But that's a worst-case scenario. Right now, we need to focus on damage control. I'll draft a statement for your podcast. Something about transparency and your commitment to objective journalism. We'll release it if the story gains traction."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we stay quiet and let it die. Most of these pieces get buried within forty-eight hours. The news cycle moves fast."

I touched the cold window. The sensation cut through the rising panic.

"Thank you, Sasha."

"Don't thank me yet. This is just the beginning. Rhodes is smarter than most of your targets. He's not going to make this easy."

I hung up and immediately dialed Wren.

She answered on the second ring, her voice alert despite the hour. "I already know. I've been tracking the article since it went live."

"Can you trace the source?"

"I tried. The email was sent from a burner account. The IP is masked through three different VPNs. Whoever fed this to Jessica Tran knew what they were doing."

"But the details in the article—"

"Are specific," Wren finished. "The dates connecting the boating fire to Mara's murder, and the suggestion that you had private knowledge of both. Each fact is public somewhere. The assembled pattern isn't. Someone has been building it for Rhodes."

I closed my eyes. The implication settled into my chest like a stone.

"Someone in my network."

"I'm not saying that," Wren said quickly. "I'm saying that someone with access to the information exists. It could be Rhodes himself. He was a prosecutor. He has resources. He could have pulled the case file, interviewed witnesses, pieced it together."

"But the article doesn't mention the network. It doesn't mention Priya or Sasha or you. It only mentions me and Mara."

"Because that's all Rhodes needs. He's not trying to take down the network. He's trying to take down you. If you fall, the network falls with you."

I opened my eyes and looked at my reflection in the dark glass. Hollow. Exhausted. But still sharp.

"Can you monitor for more leaks?"

"Already set up alerts for every major local news site, plus a few national ones. If anything else drops, I'll know within five minutes."

"Good. And Wren—"

"I know. Stay quiet. Stay dark. I've already scrubbed my recent traffic. No one's tracking me."

I ended the call and set the phone on the windowsill. The article remained open beneath a row of unread messages. Its headline turned Mara's death and Julian's fire into one insinuating question.

Justice.

The word was supposed to mean something. It was supposed to be the reason I did what I did. But standing in the dark, watching my reflection fade into the glass, I wasn't sure what justice meant anymore.

Priya called.

I answered, and her voice came through in a rush, high and breathless. "Nadia. The audit. It's been escalated."

I felt my stomach drop. "When?"

"Yesterday afternoon. My supervisor informed me that the county medical examiner's office has received a formal request to review my recent death certificate signatures. Someone flagged an inconsistency."

"Who?"

"I don't know. The request was anonymous. It came through the official tip address, and the office accepted it for review."

Victor Rhodes was the first name fear supplied. I had no evidence yet that he had created the review rather than learned about it and exploited it.

"Priya, listen to me. Take a breath."

She did. I heard it, a shaky inhale that told me how close she was to panic.

"The review is standard procedure," I said. "They do this all the time. Random audits. Quality control. It doesn't mean they've found anything."

"But what if they do? What if they look closely at Marcus Webb? I signed off on that cause of death. If anyone compares my certification to the preserved samples—"

"They won't."

"You don't know that."

"No," I said. "I don't. But I know you. You're careful. You've been doing this for years. If there was a problem, you would have caught it before it became a problem."

She was quiet for a moment. I could hear her breathing, slow and controlled, trying to match my calm.

"I'm scared, Nadia."

"I know."

"This is different. The other targets, they were criminals. They had no one looking for them. But Rhodes is a former prosecutor. He knows how the system works. He knows where to look."

"Which is why we're going to rebuild the plan for Thursday."

Silence.

"What?"

"I'm putting Thursday back on the table."

"Nadia, that's insane. We're not ready. Wren hasn't finished the financial trail. I still need to verify his medication schedule. Sasha hasn't vetted the disposal site."

"Then we work faster."

"Or we wait. We let this blow over. We go dark, let Rhodes think he's won, and then we strike when he's not expecting it."

"He's already expecting it. That's the problem. He knows I'm coming. He's building a public case against me. Every day I wait, he gets stronger. More evidence. More leaks. More pressure."

"So you're going to kill him faster?"

"I'm going to finish what I started."

Priya was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice was quiet. "I didn't sign up for this. I signed up to help you punish men who escaped justice. Men who deserved what they got. Not to put my career on the line for a revenge mission."

"This isn't revenge."

"Then what is it?"

I looked at my reflection in the glass. Hollow eyes. Sharp jaw. A woman who had already crossed every line she thought she had.

"It's survival."

I hung up before she could respond.

The article's headline had turned my life inside out in less than two hours.

Victor Rhodes had made his move.

Now it was my turn.

I called Sasha back. She answered on the first ring.

"Thursday night," I said. "Rebuilt plan. I need everyone ready."

"Nadia—"

"I know the risks. I know Thursday is aggressive. But Rhodes is building momentum. Every day I wait, he gets closer to exposing me. To exposing all of us. I can't let that happen."

"And if it goes wrong?"

I looked at my reflection one last time. "Then it goes wrong. But I'm not going to sit here and let him destroy everything I've built."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "I'll call Wren and Priya. We'll start preparing."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Just make sure you survive this."

I ended the call and set the phone down.

The room was quiet. The city was still sleeping. I did not know where Victor was or which response he expected. That uncertainty was part of the pressure.

The article looked designed to slow me down—to make me panic, retreat, disappear.

It proved Victor knew my past, my sister, and the shape of my grief. It did not prove he understood what I would do next.

I picked up my phone and opened my encrypted notes.

I typed a single line.

"Thursday night. Rebuilt plan."

The cursor blinked in the empty space beneath the words.

Waiting.

I began to write.
