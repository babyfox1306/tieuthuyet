"You think the bell chooses? Watch who’s left standing," Elena whispered, knuckles white against the edge of the archive table. She lined up the soot-darkened slips—each marked with a name, each ending with a signature that, now in sequence, felt less like tragedy and more like arithmetic.

A single fly battered itself against the glass. The old records room hummed with silence, punctuated by the faint scrape as Elena shuffled the pages. She forced herself to count the windowpanes before she spoke again, needing the ritual to steady her thoughts. Twelve, left to right, then back. Control, always.

Across the table, Gideon’s tie sat perfectly straight, but his jaw pulsed. He hadn’t spoken since she’d confronted him with the discrepancies, with the way the chain of soot-names mapped almost too perfectly to the surviving sign-in sheets and hidden attendance logs. He just watched her hands, his index finger tapping against his lips, a metronome of anxiety he was incapable of stopping.

"You’re not listening," Elena said, voice cool as steel filings. "Every victim. Every name. It’s not a lottery—never was." She slid the stack forward, forcing his eyes to flick away. "Let’s run it again. Edith’s death led to Martha, Martha to Colin, Colin to Father Hewer. The only pattern is necessity. Not fate."

Gideon gave a half-laugh, far too sharp. "Necessity. Or desperation. You always want a pattern because a pattern can break." He did not touch the papers. His hands stayed firmly on his side of the table, as if evidence could stain.

She ignored him. The tip of her thumb traced the watermark embedded in the thick, pre-war register paper—her old habit, the one Ada had teased her about. She would have said, You rub at things to see if the truth will come off on your fingers. Elena wished, bitterly, that it worked that way.

Instead, she reached for the most recent sheet—Ada’s own name, written rigid and slanted in soot, the last death before the pattern had upended itself again. Elena’s skin prickled as she compared the curve of the “A” in the soot with the corrections in the burial ledger. The handwriting matched, line for line. Ada had altered the records—her mother’s script visible now in the margins where new names had been inserted, old ones struck out and rerouted across the page.

She set the two side by side, pushing them toward Gideon like a challenge. "My mother made herself the chain’s endpoint, then faked the record to keep me out."

He shot her a look, part pity, part calculation. "Not every mother would go that far."

"She did," Elena said. This was not up for debate. She felt the weight of it settle between her ribs—a cold, dense certainty that left no room for nostalgia or forgiveness.

She flipped to the attendance sheet from the week of the fire. Names missing, erased, a tidy sideways cross marking their absence in black ink that bled through to the next page. "You know what’s odd?" Elena asked, voice brittle now. "Look at the sequence—each new victim, the next person named, always the one with the access, the information, the key. Like the mechanism needs to keep moving, and it only moves forward."

Gideon said nothing. He was scanning the stack, looking for some obvious flaw in her reasoning, but his breathing had gone shallow.

She jerked her chin toward him. "Don’t try to tell me this is random. The compulsion—whatever it is—it’s methodical. A chain of testimony, not a curse. THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME. But it doesn’t choose blindly."

"You’re—" Gideon stopped himself, glancing toward the old filing cabinet as if half-expecting the dead to step out. "If you’re right, none of us were ever safe. Not if we knew anything."

"I’m not guessing," Elena replied. "The chain advances because it must. Each link adds a piece of the past—something suppressed, or erased, or locked away. The next person is always the one who can take the testimony another step. The chain is a mechanism for restoring lost testimony. That’s why it started with the erased children."

She snapped on a pair of gloves—her own, worn leather, not the pale archives-issued ones that always felt theatrical. "Gloves are for people with something to hide," she muttered, thinking of Ada’s quick hands, always bare, never afraid of dust or ink.

Gideon’s mouth twitched, almost a smile. "You sound just like her."

Elena didn’t dignify it with a response. Instead, she brought out the signature she’d been withholding: a margin of the death ledger, sealed with a drop of red wax. A smudged thumbprint—far too small, the whorl unmistakably that of a child, pressed into the cooling wax.

She held it up. "Find me any official in Morrow Wick with hands that size."

Gideon looked away. "It could have been staged."

"Or it could be a remnant of the original testimony—the lost names. The records are littered with accidents, erasures, but this," she tapped the wax, "this is deliberate. A child’s print in the margin, right at the point where the official record ends."

He straightened his tie, twice, fingers trembling. "So you think the chain can be tricked? That by restoring the names, it’ll stop?"

Elena caught the edge of sarcasm in his voice—not fear, but the dismissal of someone who could not imagine giving up control. "I think the chain ends when the record is whole. Not before."

He stared at her, gaze suddenly liquid, dangerous. "You should leave this alone, Elena."

"You’d like that," she said, her tone flat as the ledger. "But you know what happens if I try restraining the next potential victim? I tested it with Martha. I locked her in the surgery, told the constable not to let her out. You know what happened?"

His fingers drummed faster. "What?"

"Martha found a way out—the constable let her go, then tried to recreate the last witness’s movements himself. The compulsion isn’t personal. It infects whoever’s able to keep the chain moving. Physical barriers don’t work, just redirect the current." Elena’s eyes narrowed. "As long as the knowledge exists, the chain can’t break."

Gideon licked his lips. "You’re obsessed."

She almost laughed. "That’s rich, coming from you. I’ve seen the chamber plans, Gideon. I know why you sealed it. I know you’re still moving documents, hiding what you can."

He squared his shoulders. "I have a duty to protect what’s left of this town. You’re the threat, Elena, not the bell."

She leaned in, dropping her voice. "Then why are you here after midnight, with the ledger boxes open and the matches on your desk?"

His eyes went icy. "You don’t know anything."

She savored the lie—if only because it was so careless, so obviously false. Elena stood, methodical, counting each step as she circled to the cabinet he’d left ajar. Inside: more ledgers, some half-burned, attendance logs, burial permits. At the back, a sealed envelope marked "CHILDREN—UNCONFIRMED."

She reached, but he was faster, slamming the drawer shut and twisting the key. The clatter echoed like a pistol shot.

"Enough," he snapped.

"Not nearly," Elena countered, letting the tension settle between them like the dust of a ruined church. She pressed a palm to the surface. "The names you’re protecting—they’re the engine, not the product. Without the testimony, there’s nothing for the chain to resolve."

He stepped closer—far too close, the kind of proximity that in another context might have unsettled her. Now it was only a force to be measured, catalogued, resisted.

"You’re playing with something you don’t understand," he said quietly, almost pleading. "Ada did what she had to. So did I."

"Ada tried to save me," Elena murmured, surprised at the ache the words summoned. She fixed her attention on the thumbprint. "You’re just trying to save yourself."

For a moment, neither moved. Outside, the clock above the archive door ticked over—2:10 a.m. Seven minutes.

She broke the silence, voice clipped. "If you destroy those ledgers, the chain will only find another way. That’s the logic. The testimony migrates with necessity, not with preference."

"Then maybe necessity needs to burn," Gideon said. And for the first time, his gaze met hers—direct, unsparing.

He shoved past her, moving to the side table where he’d stacked the oldest, most fragile logs. The ones with names crossed out, marks hastily corrected in Ada’s script. Flicking open a box of matches, he struck one with a savage snap.

Elena reacted on instinct, lunging for the files, knocking a register to the floor. The wildflower pressed in its center fluttered out, a ghost of scent curling through the dust. But Gideon already had two ledgers pressed together, match poised above the yellowed pages.

She stepped between him and the bin, blocking his arm with her body. "If you burn them, you end nothing. The chain will just hunt the next witness. Me. Maybe you."

He bared his teeth, a silent snarl. "We were supposed to settle this years ago."

"You settled nothing," Elena bit out. She seized the nearest ledger, clutching it to her chest. The wax seal scraped her palm—the child’s print, the last untouched evidence.

Then the bell sounded. Not the clear, mechanical chime of the archive’s old clock, but a distorted, impossible clang—from somewhere distant, buried and raw. It was earlier than before: 2:13 a.m. The sound vibrated through the walls, frog-marching dread down Elena’s spine. The bell’s tone shifted, climbing, as if straining toward something final.

Gideon’s eyes widened. He dropped the match. For a moment, Elena saw raw fear—not for himself, but for the ruin of secrets, the end of control.

The flames licked the edge of the oldest ledger. Smoke curled, acrid, filling the tiny room with memory and threat. Elena flung open the window, but the bell’s peal drowned out every other sound.

As the fire caught, pages curling black, Gideon lunged again—this time to snatch the sealed envelope, shoving it beneath his coat.

The bell’s song grew jagged, urgent. Elena stood frozen, the child’s wax-impressed thumbprint burning her palm through the ledger’s cover, the air stifling with smoke and looming consequence.

And then the bell rang again, louder, off-kilter—a warning and a summons, all unfinished testimony gathering for its final accounting.

Elena bolted for the burning record, but Gideon was already at the doorway, eyes hollow, the envelope clutched tight to his chest as the bell kept ringing and the fire began to devour their last proof of who had been lost.

The chain was nearly complete—and Elena had only minutes to decide what testimony remained to be restored, before the bell named her next.
