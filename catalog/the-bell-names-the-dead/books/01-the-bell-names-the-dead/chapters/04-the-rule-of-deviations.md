"How many times will they follow the same steps before someone refuses?" Elena muttered, pinning another deviation on her wall with a brutal push of the tack. The evidence board was a patchwork of her sleepless logic—columns of names, time-stamped photographs, and the crude soot rubbings of handwriting matched to their neat, living signatures. Red string was unnecessary; she trusted no theater, only the sharp logic of repetition.

She stepped back, surveying three columns labeled with the victims’ initials: M.R., C.P., and L.F. Each death was a shadow of the last, uncanny in its mimicry, but always—always—one step off. One deviation. One deliberate crack in the ritual.

Her thumb worried the watermark on Ada’s last official letter as she replayed the cycles in her mind, snapping each step into place. This wasn’t folklore—it was choreography, performed with the detachment of a sleepwalker. But sleepwalkers didn’t leave evidence.

Her phone buzzed against the table, an alert from the town group chat. Another resident parroting the same phrase, as if it held the weight of a benediction: THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME.

No one questioned how the bell could choose anything, with its blackened carcass gathering dust in the museum basement, its clapper sealed in evidence. Elena felt bile rise and counted the panes in the archive’s grimy window: one, two, three, four, five, six. Logic cut panic down to manageable size.

She turned back to the board, her fingertip tracing the path from each victim’s last hours to the fatal dawn. M.R.—Miriam Rowley, the florist. The night she died, she had locked herself into her greenhouse, just as the previous victim had, but the deviation: she smashed the glass on her seed cabinet, scattering soil and seeds in a pattern Elena had matched to a 1974 church seating plan. No one else would have noticed the shape—except someone looking for patterns, desperate for proof.

C.P.—Colin Pryce’s little-known cousin, not the sergeant but the baker, Charles. Charles had reenacted Miriam’s final night, delivering bread at dawn to the same four addresses. But instead of the fifth, he’d stopped at the war memorial and left a scorched roll marked with a sideways cross: the same symbol Elena had found in the ledger.

Then L.F.—Lorna Finn, the church’s former caretaker. She followed Charles’s dawn round, but where his last act had been the offering at the memorial, Lorna veered at a different moment, sneaking into the locked vestry and laying a single wildflower—violet, just like the one Elena found pressed in the burned ledger—on the pulpit. Her fingerprints had branded the stem into the dust.

Each victim, compelled to echo the last, but always with a single, clean deviation. A clue left in plain sight, only visible if you believed in logic over terror.

She flipped through the sheets of her notes. Ada’s neat handwriting—her mother’s hand, pinched and formal even in the margins—hovered at the edge of every anomaly: countersignatures, register corrections, the odd sideways cross jammed onto an official page. Ada had known. Maybe not everything, but enough.

Elena’s thumb pressed hard against the watermark. If Ada had been afraid, she’d buried it beneath protocol—burying the truth right alongside those names.

She stared at the timeline she’d constructed: 1974 at the top, the fire, the missing attendance records, the chain of deaths starting up again thirteen months after the first anniversary, and then every year since—each time, someone with access, knowledge, or blood ties.

Impossible to ignore that every deviation pointed backward—toward Saint Orison’s, toward the night the bell last rang in its tower.

She paused, catching her own gaze in the window’s reflection. The archive’s fluorescent lighting threw her features into harsh relief: eyes rimmed red, jaw set too tight. This was not a town haunted by ghosts; it was a town condemned by consistency. Every time they called it accident, or fate, or providence, it only grew stronger.

She laid a fresh photograph on the board—this one of the municipal safe in the hall. A wisp of scorched paper clung to the inside, as if someone had slammed it shut on a burning page. Most would have missed it, but Elena’s training was for small things: fibers, marks, the lies people told when they thought no one was watching.

That safe had held the original church keys, until the inventory vanished.

A dull thud echoed beyond the stacks. Elena’s entire body twitched. She tucked her thumb away from the watermark and moved, silent and practiced, toward the archive’s back hall where the church records were kept in rolling stacks.

The air there was thick with the ghosts of paperwork—yellowed forms and brittle receipts, the scent of old ink and surrender.

She kept to the shadows, breath shallow. There, by the filing cabinets, was Gideon Rusk—his back to her, hunched, tie already askew. He rifled through folios with the speed of someone both desperate and careful, pausing now and then to extract a page, photograph it with his phone, then tuck it into his briefcase.

Not destroy—the archives’ rules were sacred, even to their petty tyrant. But reposition. Obfuscate.

Elena eased forward, shoes soundless. "You’re working late, Rusk."

He straightened, but not quite enough to face her. His fingers hovered at his lips, then dropped to his tie, straightening it with quick, nervous tugs. "Inspection. There were rumors about misfiled death certificates. If these records fell into local hands—"

"Meaning me?" She kept her voice shark-sharp.

"Meaning anyone outside proper authority," he replied, defaulting to his favorite armor of plausible deniability.

She circled him, letting him see her writing pad and the pressed wildflower, her badge in her other hand. "You know the phrase everyone’s repeating out there?" She let the silence hang. "THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME. But maybe the records do that for it."

He finally met her eyes, if only for a flicker. "You’re not seriously suggesting—"

She cut him off, zero patience for his softly patronizing cadence. "I’m suggesting that every time the chain breaks pattern, someone leaves a clue they hope someone like me will find. You wouldn’t want that kind of pattern noticed, would you?"

He snapped his briefcase closed. "Your responsibility is to the living, Miss Voss. Not dead children and burned ledgers."

She measured him, pulse flat. "If that’s your version of comfort, it’s as hollow as the bell tower."

He left without another word, his shoes thudding out a rigid, martial beat against the stone.

Elena waited until the echo faded, then slipped behind the cabinets to check the space he’d been working. A single folder was misaligned—Saint Orison’s, 1974-75. She slid it out. One document was missing: the list of vestry key holders.

Another piece missing. She made a note, then returned to her board.

She knelt beside a battered cardboard box she’d rescued from Ada’s effects the week before. Ada’s correspondence was a labyrinth—so many layers of correction, scribbled-out sentences, lines of apology and guilt wrapped in the language of officialdom.

Elena sorted through the pile, flipping open envelope after envelope. Some contained nothing but bank statements, others invitations to church picnics long past. But one thin letter—half burned along the right edge—caught her attention.

She unfolded it with steady hands, scanning. Ada’s hand, but hurried, the lines rushed, something anxious threading between the words.

"…forgive the alteration, but this must stay hidden until the proper witness can see. The key is not in the register, nor in the safe, but sealed inside Saint Orison’s pulpit. If you find this, follow the wildflowers. Trust only the sequence…"

The rest was blackened—a scorch mark running over the final lines. Elena brushed a fleck of ash from the margin. The key inside the pulpit. Not magically protected, just inconveniently placed, out of reach. And the wildflowers—again, always the wildflowers.

She scanned the reverse of the page, her finger pausing at a faint mark—a sideways cross, identical to the one from the ledger. Clue C003: the cross always appeared near any mention of hidden evidence or names. Ada had used it as a signpost, a breadcrumb for the right eyes.

The message was unfinished, like nearly everything Ada had left behind. Elena’s mind ticked through possibilities: the pattern of the wildflowers, the hidden key, the locked chamber.

She stood, pinning the burned letter to her board, staring at the puzzle she’d built. Every deviation was planned. Each victim left not just a name, but a sign—wildflower, bread, cross—clues encoded in ritual deviation. Not random. Never random.

Her phone vibrated again, an automated message from the town council.

THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME.

She looked out the dark window, counting windowpanes automatically, thumb grinding a groove into the watermark on Ada’s letter. Now she would have to enter Saint Orison’s herself, find the pulpit, search for the key—before the next name was written in soot.

But as she turned off the desk lamp, the echo of footsteps drifted from the hallway—slower this time, measured, as if someone waited just outside the archives, guarding the way to the church.

Elena froze, adrenaline tightening every muscle.

The next deviation wouldn’t wait for dawn.
