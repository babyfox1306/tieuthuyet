"If you want the truth, you’ll need to burn your gloves," the archivist mutters as Elena Voss enters the records room, his breath condensing in the cold air despite the humming heater. The man doesn’t look up, just nudges a battered cardboard box across the table with two nicotine-stained fingers, dust blooming off its lid.

She ignores his theatrics. Anything to keep him out of her way. "Gloves are for people with something to hide," Elena says, dropping her satchel on a spindly chair. A minute spent counting the windowpanes—six across, three high, none clean—before she forces herself to move.

He slouches into the shadow between two filing cabinets, watching as she pries open the box. Paper and ash spill out: registers, scribbled notes, a cracked magnifying glass, and—almost hidden—a ledger burned along the edges, the paper gone brittle and dark.

Morrow Wick’s archive is a labyrinth of bad lighting and bureaucratic neglect. Rows of cabinets hunker under years of dust, corners blooming with pale mold. Elena glances at the door, counting the locks—two, three, and the clang of the iron key settling in her pocket. No one will surprise her here.

She slides the ledger onto the table. The cover bears a scorched cross and a date in faded ink: 1974. Her thumb rubs the edge, feeling for a watermark that should not have survived the fire. It hasn’t. What she finds instead is stranger—a pressed wildflower sandwiched between pages, petals perfectly preserved, as if the fire that blackened the rest of the volume had spared this fragment out of spite.

The archivist huffs. "Never seen that before. Fire’s a selective thing." He licks a finger and flips a loose page back into place, like he’s marking territory.

"I’m sure that’s the explanation," Elena says. It isn’t.

The ledgers are arranged by week, every entry starting with the same official header: Saint Orison’s Church. She flips hastily, hunting for the attendance records from the week of the fire. Columns of names slide past—some written in neat, looping hands, others in jagged block capitals. But several are just… gone. Blank spaces where names should be. Shadows of erased ink, the pressure marks faint under her palm.

"Was this ledger ever officially restored?" Elena asks, not looking at him.

He shrugs. "Everything that survives is here. The rest is rumor."

She isn’t fooled. One page near the center bears a charred bite, almost circular, nibbling into the signature lines. Her pulse spikes—not with fear, but with something sharper: recognition. Here and there, signatures have been overwritten, ink doubling back over itself. And among the survivors, she spots a pattern—one official’s name, always angling left, with the flourish of a trailing “V.” Sometimes it’s Ada Voss, sometimes just “A. V.”—but always in the same hand. The dates line up too neatly to be coincidence.

There’s more. In the margins, a cryptic mark: a line bisected by a smaller stroke, like a cross fallen sideways. It appears only on the most altered pages, never on clean entries.

Clue C001. Elena leans closer, brushing ash from the paper. The mark is new. Faint. Someone added it after the fire—after the records were supposed to be sealed.

She feels the archivist’s gaze pinching her, so she asks, "What’s the policy on these notations? Unauthorized amendments?"

He gives a little cough. "If it’s not in the register, it’s not real. That’s the rule." But his eyes won’t meet hers, flitting instead to the blackened flower pressed under the ledger’s cover.

The church bell’s echo still haunts her: the impossible chime at 2:17 a.m., hollow and metallic. Its memory rattles against her logic, sparking every time she looks at the ruin of the Voss signature.

She presses on. The death register is next, its entries bracketed in rigid cursive: date, time, method, witness. Elena finds the most recent victim—a name she knows, written in soot on a wall. The handwriting is meticulous, looping just like the signature she’s seen in the ledger, only now it’s smeared, faint as a ghost.

Every victim’s entry contains a deviation—a detail out of sequence with the others. One arrives at the church an hour early. Another skips the walk along the green. A third writes a name not where the eye falls first, but behind a cupboard door, as if hiding from the inevitable. This isn’t accident. It’s method.

She flips back and forth, comparing the soot-written names with the official signatures. Sometimes, they match so perfectly it’s as if the same hand dictated both. Sometimes, the soot name corrects a flourished error in the register, as if the victim, compelled, must set the record straight.

Ada Voss’s name appears again and again, not as a victim, but as an official witness. And every time, the record is altered—it’s subtle, a hesitation in the script, as if the pen paused mid-stroke. At the bottom of one page, a fragment of a letter clings to the binding: “If anyone asks, I was home that night. I did nothing.” The i’s aren’t dotted. The handwriting matches Ada’s from thirty years ago—anxious, hurried, forced into official neatness.

There’s evidence here, but not proof. Yet.

Elena scans the file for her own mother’s name, throat tight. Ada Voss is everywhere in the margins, initials in the shadows of erasure, the same looping “V” cropping up in the aftermath of every death. Elena’s hand closes into a fist, thumb digging into the pressure point beneath her fingernail. She’s not superstitious, but she’s not blind.

She lets the ledger fall open to a charred section, the wildflower resting between pages. Its stem is pinched but whole. She tugs it free, holding it up to the light. How could it have survived when the ink and even the paper didn’t? Something about the resilience of small, unremarkable things unsettles her more than any ghost story.

A tap at the door. Gideon Rusk’s silhouette looms in the glass, his tie already half-straightened as if he’s been preparing this performance. Elena doesn’t rise.

He enters with a clipboard tucked to his chest, lips drawn thin. "Miss Voss, I wasn’t expecting you so early," he says. His gaze falls on the ledger, then darts away. The index finger of his free hand begins its compulsive rhythm against his mouth.

"Surprise," Elena replies, dry. "Thought I’d have a look at the records before they wander off again."

"These archives are in fragile condition," he says. "The fire compromised most primary documents. You understand the need for discretion."

"I understand," Elena says, "that every time I ask for an unredacted file, it’s already gone missing or been 'compromised.'"

He hesitates fractionally, then slides a folder across the table. "These are the only files available for public review. The rest are restricted by order of the council." His voice is pleasant, plausible. She’s heard it a thousand times from men who believe obstruction is a polite art.

She doesn’t touch the folder. "What exactly am I not supposed to see, Mr. Rusk?"

He straightens his tie—again. "There’s no conspiracy here. Just preservation protocols. I do hope you’ll respect the professional boundaries of this investigation. There’s already… enough distress."

Elena’s hand hovers over the ledger. "You mean the distress of being locked out of the truth? Or the distress of unexplained deaths met with bureaucratic shrugs and missing paperwork?"

His cheeks flush. "The bell is a local trauma. We don’t need more rumors, Miss Voss."

She almost laughs, but it comes out as a sharp exhale. "Rumors are the only thing that survive in this town."

Gideon’s eyes flicker over the charred ledger, the wildflower, her poised pen. "You should leave that where you found it," he says quietly. "The past is more dangerous here than you realize."

She snaps the ledger closed, the smell of ash lingering. "The past doesn’t frighten me, Mr. Rusk. Incomplete records do."

His mouth tenses, but he says nothing further. He lingers as if expecting her to give up the search. When she doesn’t, he leaves, the door shutting with a low, reluctant click.

Alone, Elena turns to the remaining piles—notes, correspondences, ledgers she suspects Gideon would burn if he could. Her gaze hooks on another anomaly: a death certificate signed by Ada Voss, but the date is wrong—marked two days before the actual event. The signature is rushed, almost an afterthought, and in the upper margin, a faint imprint: a line and a cross, the same as in the ledger. The pattern is tightening around her, each clue a thread in a web she refuses to believe, yet cannot ignore.

She pulls out her phone, snapping photos before someone decides to remove this evidence too. Each flash gives her a sense of order; each document, a foothold against the wave of superstition pressing at the back of her mind.

If the chain of deaths is really random, why do the same hands keep cropping up in the records? Why does Ada Voss’s name hover at the edges, never as a victim, always as a witness—altering, signing, erasing? And why do the soot-written names so precisely mirror the signatures in the registers, down to every flourish and hesitation?

She runs her thumb over the charred edge of the ledger, the wildflower pressed back into place. There’s a fragility to the evidence—the sense that one careless move might erase everything. Elena refuses to be the frightened woman huddled before the unknown. She’ll be the one to corner the truth, even if it means grinding her nerves to dust in the process.

Later, she leaves the archives, locking the door three times, counting each click. Her mind flickers through every anomaly: Ada’s anxious script, the cross-mark, the signature patterns, the wildflower. The bell’s echo seems to follow her down the hall.

At dawn, the news arrives.

Sergeant Pryce pounds at her door, eyes ringed with exhaustion. In his hand, a photo—this time, the soot-written name scrawled not on a wall, but inside Saint Orison’s vestry. She stares at the image. The vestry was locked. Only council officials have keys. The handwriting is unmistakable: it’s hers.

Before she can protest, the world narrows to a single, chilling certainty.

The chain is tightening. And now, whatever force writes the next name… knows where to find her.
