"If you break the seal, the town will burn again," Gideon hisses as Elena presses the blueprint flat under her palm.

She doesn’t flinch, but the blueprint shudders beneath her hand—a century-old vellum trembling with the echo of an argument as old as the church itself. Gideon’s breath is sharp and hot against her ear, the scent of too-strong tea and something sour, like paper left damp and molding.

She doesn’t look up. If she does, he’ll see the tremor in her eyelid, the tick that only comes when she’s this close to something real. Instead, her thumb rubs a nearly invisible watermark—a pair of wings, moth-eaten at one tip—over and over. Gideon crowds her space, both hands braced on the battered drafting table.

"Move," Elena says, hard and flat. Her voice could scrape paint. "I have the council key. You have ten minutes before the constable starts asking why you’re here."

"Ten minutes to what?" His words are clipped, the consonants brittle as snapped twigs. "To satisfy your compulsion? Those doors were sealed for a reason, Elena."

She spreads the blueprint wider. The pulpit, vestry, nave—the usual landmarks—but beneath, a ghostly overlay in faded pencil: a spiral staircase, marked only EXIT OF FIRE and, lower, an unlabelled rectangle, x-ed out in red. Her pulse knocks against her teeth.

"Who marked this?" she murmurs, tracing the rectangle.

"Does it matter?" Gideon’s finger darts out, jabbing the section with the ‘X’. "You’re not opening that chamber. Not tonight, not ever."

She lets out a short, humorless breath. "Did you bring your own hammer, or do you just plan to obstruct with petty threats?"

His tie is askew, undone at the knot—a detail she files away. He only does that when cornered and out of plausible denials.

He leans in. "If you push this, Elena, whatever you think you’re uncovering—"

"You’ll what? File a complaint with yourself?"

He straightens, lip curling. The threat is empty, but he moves to block the exit, shoulders squared. Beneath the flickering fluorescent lights, his shadow warps across the blueprint, blacking out the spiral staircase.

She shoves the blueprint into her bag and slips past him, heels clacking on the parquet. The air between them tightens, the silence threading itself so thick it crackles.

Down in the nave, the church is a cavern of hollow echoes and burnt wax. Elena counts the windowpanes before moving—a discipline, a litany against dread. Seven to the left, six to the right. She refuses to rush.

"Don’t," Gideon calls from above, his voice ricocheting off stone.

She doesn’t answer. The key is warm in her fist, smudged with soot that’s ground deep into the grooves. The pulpit, the confessionals, the battered choir stalls—she catalogues each as she passes, anchoring herself. The basement door is behind the altar, camouflaged in plain wood, its lock old but recently oiled.

She glances back. Gideon’s silhouette hangs in the doorway, a scarecrow judge.

"Official business," she announces, loud enough to carry. "Town records. If you interfere, Sergeant Pryce will have questions—"

Gideon steps into the nave but makes no move to descend. His hands are in his pockets, but his shoulders are braced. "THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME," he mutters, almost to himself.

Elena unlocks the door—three turns, deliberate, each click ringing too loud—and steps into the darkness. The smell hits first: earth, old stone, and a low tang of scorched copper. She finds the switch by memory. The bulb stutters, then casts a sickly pool of light down a cramped stair.

She closes the door behind her, clicks the lock for good measure. Three times, out of habit.

On the third tread, she stops. The wall to her left—bare brick—shows a patch cleaner than the rest. In the slant of the trembling bulb, a faint outline emerges: a child’s hand, pressed into the dust, only visible at a certain angle. The fingers are too small, the wrist too thin. It’s old, but not ancient.

She reaches out, wipes her thumb across the outline. Nothing lifts. It’s not soot, not chalk. Just pressure, preserved by neglect. She counts the bricks—twenty-one, left to right. The same as the steps from pulpit to nave.

She pulls the blueprint from her bag, flattening it against a crate. The rectangle is here, exactly where the odd echo of the wall runs. She taps her pen against the spot. Hidden door. No new mortar, but a hairline seam—recent, maybe two years at most.

Someone resealed this. Not in 1974—recently.

She flips through her notebook, cross-referencing the repair invoices she’d stolen from the parish office. Two signatures—both in the same slanted hand. The trailing V. Ada’s mark, overwriting her own name.

Elena swears under her breath. If Ada tried to open the chamber, who closed it after?

She kneels, running the key along the seam. The lock should be here. But there’s nothing but smooth brick and a tiny iron disc, flush to the stone, disguised by old paint.

From the head of the stairs, Gideon’s voice floats down, forced casual. "Still nothing? I told you, there’s no mechanism left."

She ignores him. Her thumb finds a depression beside the disc—a circle the size of a coin, black with soot. She fits the key, twists. For a moment, resistance, then a click as something heavy shifts.

But when she pulls, the wall does not move. She tests the brickwork again, harder. The mechanism inside grinds, then jams.

"Sabotaged," she mutters.

"Give it up, Elena." Gideon’s tone is different now—urgent, almost pleading. "It’s just dust and rot. There’s nothing to find down there."

She stands, facing the child’s handprint. Her mind spins. If Ada risked everything to record names in soot, why would she reseal the chamber? Unless someone forced her—someone with more authority. Like Gideon’s father.

She snaps photos with her phone, capturing the handprint and the iron disc. Every detail. Physical evidence. Real, not rumor.

Upstairs, glass shatters—somewhere near the vestry. Gideon curses, his footsteps retreating. She pockets her phone and blueprint, heart rattling faster.

The hum of the light changes, a low drone. Elena unclips a utility knife from her bag—habit, not hope. If the lock is jammed, brute force is next.

She wedges the blade beneath the disc, leverages. Metal screeches, the seam creaks, but the mechanism holds. She presses, sweat prickling. Another click, but now the whole wall jerks and a crack opens—a breath of colder, fetid air curling out.

Above, voices echo, urgent and sharp.

"Where’s the register?" Gideon barks.

"You moved it—don’t lie," a woman's voice retorts—Martha Bellamy. Elena's ears sting with recognition.

"This isn't the time—" Gideon hisses.

Elena steps back, breathing in dust and the coppery tang of ancient fire. The wall groans—half open, half stuck.

She pockets the key, but the soot stains remain on her skin. She wipes them on her skirt: black smears, physical proof. Not enough.

From the nave, a chant rises—low at first, then growing, fractured voices twining together.

"THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME. THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME."

It ripples through the stone, catching in Elena’s chest like a fist. The phrase infects the air, each repetition more frenzied.

She sets the blueprint down on a crate, anchoring it with a brick. If she’s forced to leave, she’ll have a record. Enough to press charges, maybe—if she lives past dawn.

The light in the stairwell flickers fitfully, cycling between yellow and white. Something electronic—a phone alarm—goes off above. Shouts. The chant surges.

The bell starts to ring.

Not the dull, tinny jangle from the archives’ clocktower. Not the broken chime she’d heard in memory, but a full, body-thrumming toll, impossible, volcanic.

2:17 a.m.

She feels the vibration through her knees, her jaw, the bones of her feet. Her phone screen glitches, time flickering, then stabilizing on the hour.

A new cycle. Elena grits her teeth, refusing to look at the stairs behind her. If she turns back, she’ll see who the bell has named.

And then the lights die.

Total black.

Her heart scrabbles up her throat. She counts windowpanes, but there are none here, just walls and the suffocating weight of the sealed chamber she can almost—almost—reach.

Above, feet pound the nave. Someone—Gideon—shouts her name, but it’s muffled, distorted by stone.

A lock snicks. Metal grinds. She lunges for the stair, but the basement door is already banging shut. A key turns in the lock, slow and deliberate. She shoulders the door, but it doesn’t budge.

"Let me out—" Her voice splinters, rough and furious.

On the far side, someone—Gideon, or someone else—gathers breath. She hears the retreat of footsteps, the echo of the phrase:

"THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME."

A sudden, cold draft snakes down the stair. The hair on her neck stands.

She turns, back pressed to the door, as the bell tolls again and the seam in the wall exhales a whisper of fire and dust.

Farther down, in the black, something moves.

The key doesn’t fit the inside lock.

Her only light is her phone, its battery at 2%.

A new death has started. The ritual isn’t waiting for sunrise.

And someone—someone still alive—has just locked her in.

She’s not alone in the dark.
