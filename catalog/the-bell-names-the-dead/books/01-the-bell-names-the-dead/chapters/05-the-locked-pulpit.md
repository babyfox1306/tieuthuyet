"You shouldn’t be here past vespers," Gideon Rusk warned, but Elena Voss’s hand was already halfway up the inside of the pulpit, groping blindly past splintered wood and centuries of dust. Her knees ached from kneeling on the uneven tile, but she ignored the discomfort, teeth clenched tight as she searched for the irregularity Ada’s letter had promised. The sanctuary was cavernous and dark, the only illumination coming from the steady sweep of Gideon’s torch at the far end of the nave. His footsteps echoed, even and predatory.

"Close the door if you’re so concerned about intruders," Elena called back, voice clipped. "Or is this the part where you confiscate my notebook again?"

Gideon didn’t answer right away—she heard the faint click as the door latched, then the unmistakable rhythm of him straightening his tie, a signal of nerves he never quite managed to mask. Elena’s hand caught on something rough beneath the pulpit shelf. She withdrew it, wiped dust on her trouser leg, then tried again, pressing her fingertips flush against the grain.

The church was colder than she remembered. Rows of pews stretched away, their ends carved with wildflowers—lilies, poppies, bluebells. Wildflowers, just as Ada had written. Elena traced the pattern from memory, counting the petals on each next relief: eight, then six, then five. The third flower down, bluebell, just above where the preacher’s knees would rest. She pressed, feeling for a catch.

"You’re not listening," Gideon said, moving closer. "This is still an active investigation. Nothing leaves this building without—"

"Official documentation and seventeen forms. Yes, I’ve heard the speech," Elena muttered. Her thumb brushed along the underside, caught on a seam, and pushed. The wood gave the faintest click. She felt rather than saw a compartment slide open, too small for a book, too deliberate for a reverend’s prayer handkerchief.

Elena extracted a slender iron key, pitted with age, and something else: a scrap of paper, folded and pressed flat, yellowed at the edges. Her pulse spiked.

Behind her, Gideon’s shoes scuffed, suddenly near. "What is that?"

She closed her fist around the prize, rising. "A failed attempt at security. Or did the bell forget to schedule its own safety deposit box?"

He narrowed his eyes, gaze flicking from her hand to the pulpit and back. "You realize this is tampering. I could—"

"Report me? File another ambiguous memo? You think bureaucracy’s going to save anyone?" Elena shot him a sardonic look, then moved past him, careful to keep her back straight. She could feel Gideon’s presence bristling behind her as she made for the vestry door.

He matched her pace, voice dropping low. "People are frightened, Elena. This—obsession of yours—it stirs up things best left buried."

"The bell doesn’t care about your sentimentality. Or your need for order." Elena forced a smile that didn’t reach her eyes. "THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME."

Gideon bristled, a tic developing at the corner of his mouth. "That’s just folklore."

"Is it?" She stopped in the shadow of the alcove, shoulders squared. "How many names have vanished from the records, Gideon? How many more until you admit this is orchestrated?"

He reached for her arm, instinctive, but stopped short, fingers curling instead around his tie. His gaze darted aside. "Saint Orison’s is closed to the public. I could have you removed."

"You could try." Elena slipped the key into her pocket, then unfolded the note with her free hand. She didn’t give him the satisfaction of flinching as he hovered close, blocking her exit with a calculated stance.

The scrap of paper was addressed in Ada’s hurried script, though the bottom corner was torn away. A list, incomplete: names she recognized—her own, crossed out, then beneath, faint and barely decipherable, a single line: Restore the children. Their truth lives beneath.

A cold ripple ran through her. Ada’s hand, unmistakable; the same sideways cross at the margin. Ada’s secret wasn’t just the attendance ledger. She’d tried to bring the erased children’s names back, and someone—Elena didn’t want to guess who—had stopped her. The restoration was never finished.

She folded the note, careful not to meet Gideon’s eyes.

He studied her in silence, tension radiating between them. "You’re chasing ghosts," he said at last. "Nothing good comes from reopening graves."

Elena wanted to laugh, or maybe scream. She forced composure, rubbing her thumb over the watermark embossed on the edge of Ada’s note, grounding herself in paper and ink, not superstition. "If I can prove it, it happened. No matter how many times you seal a door or shred a file."

"Careful. The more you dig, the more likely you are to become the subject instead of the investigator." Gideon’s tone was almost soft, the threat half-buried.

She didn’t answer. Instead, she turned on her heel, striding for the vestry and the shadowed hall beyond. She knew the church by heart—had mapped every useless storeroom and bricked-over stairwell in the first summer after her mother died. But tonight, the angles felt sharper, the shadows deeper.

She stopped at the archive drawers behind the altar, the ones Ada had always kept locked. The iron key fit the central slot with surprising ease. Elena inhaled, bracing for rot or dust, and tugged.

The drawer slid open. Inside, carefully preserved beneath a cracked glassine envelope, was a folded set of architectural plans and a bundle of brittle paper tags, each printed with a child’s name and a birthdate. Seven tags—no, only five legible, two with names scorched out. She lifted one, the name almost erased but for the faintest outline of ink: Mary D., b. 1965.

Beneath the tags, the architectural plans were stamped in blue ink: Saint Orison’s Church. Basement Level—Proposed Repairs, 1975. There, between the curling edges, a sketched rectangle labeled: Sealed Chamber — Access Restricted.

Elena’s heart hammered. The chamber was real. Whatever was hidden there, Ada had known about it. Maybe finished her restoration inside, or failed to get that far. She counted the surviving names, then the empty spaces where labels should have been. The pattern fit the chain—the missing, the erased, the chosen.

A soft exhalation behind her—barely a breath, but enough to remind her Gideon hadn’t left. She stiffened.

"Find what you were looking for?" he said, too smooth.

"Enough," she shot back, not turning. "More than what’s in your official version, certainly."

He moved to block her path, standing between her and the open nave. "That drawer is not for public access. I’ll need you to surrender any documents—"

"You’ll need a warrant," she retorted, snapping the drawer shut and pocketing the plans and tags. "Don’t pretend you haven’t destroyed worse."

His lip curled, but he didn’t pursue the accusation. Instead, he leaned forward, voice dropping. "You keep pushing, Elena. You won’t like where it leads."

She held his gaze, refusing to blink. "THE BELL CHOOSES THE NEXT NAME," she whispered, deadpan, daring him to contradict her.

For a heartbeat, the only sound in the church was the distant creak of wood settling—the place itself responding to their standoff. Then Gideon turned, swiping a hand across his tired face, the facade momentarily slipping.

"You’re trespassing," he repeated, but it was empty now.

Elena brushed past him, resisting the urge to glance back. If she hesitated, he’d try again—block the door, question her authority, slow her down with process and threat. But he didn’t follow, only stood in the gloom, a sentry at the edge of something neither of them could name.

She navigated the echoing corridors, her mind racing. Ada had tried to restore the erased children’s names—had risked her own reputation to do it—but Gideon, or someone with his authority, had forced her to stop. The secret wasn’t just in signatures or marks. It was in the gaps, the names that never returned.

Why these children? Why this place, this chamber?

At the narthex, Elena paused to refasten her coat, her hands shaking despite her efforts to appear calm. She reached for the key in her pocket, checking its teeth out of habit—a tic, a compulsive reassurance. The metal, clean before, was now blackened with soot, the ridges lined with fine charcoal dust.

Odd. She rubbed the key with her sleeve, smearing black across the fabric, and held it up to the failing light.

Then she saw it—a new carving, fresh, almost raw, gouged into the wooden doorframe beside the church exit, just at eye level. The name was unmistakable, the letters uneven but deliberate:

Elena Voss.

She read it twice, fighting down the cold spike of dread. The key’s soot. The name, still flaking black. The ritual was a step ahead. The next victim—her own name, etched where none could miss it.

By the time Elena looked up, the nave was empty.

And somewhere, faint as a memory, the bell began to chime.
