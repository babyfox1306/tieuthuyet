"Who else heard it this time?" Elena snaps, the question stinging the air before her feet have touched the floorboards. Her voice is sharper than the cold, slicing through the blue-dark hush that blankets Morrow Wick at this hour. For a moment, she lets her palm hover over the patchwork quilt, bracing for the echo—a child’s habit, listening for denial or comfort. Neither arrives. Only the steady tap of the old radiator, impersonating a heartbeat.

The bell’s peal still vibrates in her chest, impossible, unmistakable, as real as the first breath after a nightmare. 2:17 a.m.—she doesn’t have to glance at the clock. Every soul in town knows that time. The church bell, long since dismantled and hidden in a council vault, should be nothing but an entry in the records her mother once fussed over. And yet, tonight, it rang. Elena’s thumb finds the watermark on the folder stacked by her nightstand, rubbing it until heat blooms beneath her nail. If I can’t prove it, it didn’t happen. Superstition is for the frightened, not the responsible.

She counts the square panes of glass before standing. Four across, three down. Twelve. Not thirteen, never. The self-imposed ritual steadies her pulse, at least enough to cross the groaning floor. Moonlight fingers the edge of her desk, silvering the municipal ledger still open from yesterday. Elena clicks off the bedside lamp and pads to the window, avoiding the rug’s frayed edge. Outside, porch lights flicker on one by one, wary as eyes opening in the dark.

Down the street, Mrs. Portman’s silhouette huddles on her porch, shawl tight as a straitjacket. Two more doors creak open. No one speaks. No one dares. The bell always demands silence, as if words might call its attention. Elena notes these responses, cataloguing dread, unwilling to name it fear.

A faint, oily fingerprint smears her window latch—a detail her tired mind snags on. She wipes it away with the edge of her sleeve, brow furrowing. No one could have reached this latch from the ground. She files the anomaly away, the way her mother taught her: collect facts, do not speculate.

Elena rechecks the deadbolt, then the security chain. Three times, as always. Her mother’s rules. Ada Voss, whose fingers left ink stains on every sheet of the death register, who whispered old warnings after the church fire—never trust the silences that follow the bell. Elena forces her mind away from the memory. Sentiment distracts. Shuffling to the kitchen, she pours water into the kettle, the motion mechanical. There is comfort in procedures, even when the world mutinies. 

The kettle’s click shivers through the quiet, a solitary sound bridging past and present. Outside, a car halts on the gravel—headlights slashing yellow and thin against the dark. Elena recognizes the battered estate car even before the driver’s door opens. Gideon Rusk emerges with bureaucratic precision, his overcoat buttoned to the throat, tie already slightly askew. He taps his index finger against his lips, scanning the street, eyes never quite landing anywhere. Always observing, never participating.

He is at her gate before she unlocks it, the two of them separated by the low iron bars. Elena’s hand is steady, but she notices the minute tremor in his, a twitch as he straightens his tie before speaking.

"Miss Voss." His tone is all county-official neutrality, but the edge is there—condolence, warning, jurisdiction. "I presume you heard it."

"If you mean the bell, yes, along with half the town. Don’t bother asking if I imagined it." She steps aside, motioning him toward the stoop, but he lingers outside the boundary, as though the threshold itself is infected.

He glances past her into the hallway, searching for evidence of panic or disorder. Finding none, he exhales, barely meeting her gaze. "We should keep this brief. There’s protocol."

"Your protocol, or the town’s?" Elena’s voice is clipped, a dry crackle beneath the politeness.

"Both. I need to verify your whereabouts—"

"You know exactly where I was. You always do, don’t you?" She notes the resentment in her own tone, refuses to apologize for it. He never allows her near the records unsupervised, never lets her examine the old church files alone. Tonight, he seems even less inclined to trust.

Gideon sighs, tapping his finger once against his lip. "This isn’t about you, Elena. I’m just—"

"Doing your job." She interrupts, refusing to cede an inch. "Well, so am I. My mother was never wrong about these things." The name Ada Voss lands between them, soft as a threat.

He looks away, fusses with the lapel of his overcoat. "I suggest you stay away from Saint Orison’s tonight. No need to inflame local hysteria."

Elena’s lips thin. "You think I’m going to break into the church? There’s nothing left but scorched stone and mold."

"Nevertheless." He straightens his tie again, the gesture so habitual it might be a nervous tick. "We’ll maintain a perimeter."

"A perimeter for what? A ghost? Or just superstition?"

His eyes narrow, mouth nearly curled into a frown. For a heartbeat, she almost expects him to argue. Instead, he produces a thin notepad, scribbles something she can’t quite see, then closes it with a snap. "Let’s be clear. There are procedures. I’ll handle the records. You keep your observations to yourself. Understood?"

Elena resists the urge to laugh. "You’re not as subtle as you think, Gideon. People notice when the council clamps down after every bell."

His silence is almost an admission. Then, footsteps echo from further up the street. Elena looks past him, heart snagging against her ribs.

A figure wanders the cobblestone, barefoot and shivering, nightgown trailing in the mud—Annabel Croft, the apothecary’s niece. Her eyes are wide, glazed, fixed on something distant. She pauses at the corner, turns, and walks precisely the same path taken by Daniel Wright the week before. Elena recognizes the sequence: the slow spiral toward Saint Orison’s crumbling nave, the pause at the broken fence post, the muttered, wordless prayer. The choreography of doom.

She glances at Gideon, but he’s already moving, waving off Sergeant Pryce, who looms uncertainly near his cruiser. "Don’t intervene," Gideon says, voice taut. "Let her be."

Elena wraps her cardigan tighter, watching Annabel cross the threshold of the ruined churchyard, always one foot on worn stone, always pausing to touch the scorched bell tower’s foundation, just as Daniel Wright had done. The neighbors watch too, some hiding behind curtains, others pressed against rain-speckled glass. It’s a town-wide vigil, silent and helpless.

"Is she awake?" Elena murmurs.

Gideon’s jaw flexes. "Does it matter?"

She wants to retort, but all that comes is a gnawing sense of helpless anger. Annabel moves with grim precision, each step echoing what came before. Not an accident, not a pattern, just a compulsion that will play to its end.

Elena turns away, unable to watch Annabel vanish into the church’s black maw. "How much longer will you pretend this doesn’t matter?"

Gideon’s answer is lost in the hush that falls over the street. Windows close. The town folds in on itself, waiting for sunrise and the verdict it brings.

Elena returns inside, double-checking the locks again. One, two, three. She pours herself tea, hands steadier now, and pulls her mother’s old records from the shelf. Ada had always said, records are truth and shield both. But the registers are fragmentary, pages torn out, entire lines redacted in Ada’s spidery hand. Even the death log from 1974 is half erased, most names gone or blurred into illegibility.

She finds herself running her thumb over the watermark on the birth ledgers, a futile reassurance. The kettle gurgles in the background, carrying thoughts of her mother’s nervous instructions: Never trust the church’s silences; never leave a window unlatched after a bell.

Elena glances again at her window. The fingerprint is gone now, but a faint oily residue stains the brass latch. She presses her forefinger to it, then wipes her hand on her robe. Someone—or something—had touched it tonight. But why would anyone try from outside, unless they needed a way in? No footprint below, just churned earth and frost.

Dawn presses weak light through the glass. Elena lets herself drift along the edge of sleep, head on the kitchen table, ears tuned to the hush outside. A clock chimes softly—her own, not the bell. She stands, breathing in the metallic tang of morning.

Her phone vibrates once, displaying a message from Martha Bellamy, the local doctor— Are you all right? Call me when you wake. Elena hesitates, drafts a reply, then deletes it. She takes solace in the routine: kettle, ledger, lock, window.

She slips on her coat and steps outside just as the eastern sky bruises pink and blue. The street is empty, serene in that deceptive way only Morrow Wick manages—a stillness that suffocates more than it reassures.

She descends the steps, about to fetch the morning paper, when she sees it. Written across the topmost step, in a trembling, soot-black hand, is a name.

Her own.

Elena Voss.

And the soot is still damp beneath her fingertips.
