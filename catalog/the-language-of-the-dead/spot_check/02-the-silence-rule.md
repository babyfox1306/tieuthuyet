Dawn broke grey over the sealed summit, and the silence from Amara Okafor's room was the loudest thing Sofia Bellini had ever heard. No one said her name at breakfast. No one said anything at all. The eleven remaining students sat around the long oak table staring at plates of untouched food, watching the steam rise and die without lifting a fork.

Sofia's coffee had gone cold an hour ago. She watched the surface congeal and felt the weight of every unspoken sentence pressing against her ribs.

Dmitri Ivanov broke the silence first. He always did. Where others felt grief, he felt a problem to solve, and problems demanded solutions. He pushed his plate away and stood, tapping his knuckles against the wood the way a professor might call a lecture to order.

"It is the translation," he said. His voice carried the flat certainty of someone who had spent the night building a theory instead of mourning. "Amara's words at breakfast were twisted beyond recognition. She said she was happy to be here. The replay said she was terrified. The system did not just mistranslate her. It inverted her meaning entirely."

Pierre Leclerc set his fork down carefully. "That is a single data point. You cannot build a rule on one corpse."

"Then give me a better theory." Dmitri's gaze swept the table. "No one has one. We are scared and silent and waiting to die. That is not a plan. The system kills whoever's words are mistranslated the most. Amara was changed the most. She died first. It is the only logic that fits."

Sofia watched them argue. Yuki Tanaka was trembling beside her, hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking. Pierre was shaking his head, running a hand through his dark hair. The others were looking at Dmitri like he had handed them a rope in the dark.

It was a terrible theory, Sofia knew. They were building a physics of death out of grief and guesswork. But it was a structure, and a structure was better than the void Amara had left behind.

"If it is a translation game," Sofia said, standing so abruptly her chair scraped against the floor, "then you need someone who can play it better than the system."

All eyes turned to her.

"Someone who can act as the filter," she continued. "A designated speaker. Whoever talks the most takes the most risk. So let that person be me. I will speak for us. I will translate for us. I will make sure the words coming out match the words going in."

Dmitri narrowed his eyes. "You are volunteering to be the voice of the group?"

"I am the best translator in this room," Sofia said flatly. "You know it. I know it. Fear makes languages sloppy, and sloppy gets people killed. Let me carry the weight of the words so the rest of you don't have to."

Yuki reached across the table and squeezed her hand. Yuki's fingers were cold, but her grip was fierce. "She is right. Sofia can do this. She hears things the rest of us miss."

Pierre nodded slowly. "Someone needs to lead. If it is her, I trust it."

Dmitri looked at Sofia for a long moment. His jaw tightened, then relaxed. "Fine. But if you slip, if one word comes out wrong, we will all burn."

Sofia held his gaze. "I will not slip."

She sat back down and felt the weight of eleven lives settle across her shoulders. It was heavier than she expected.

The morning passed in careful silence. Everyone spoke in whispers, if they spoke at all. Dmitri wrote rules on the whiteboard. No unnecessary conversation. No slang. No idioms. Speak only in your native language, and keep every sentence so plain that no machine could possibly misunderstand it.

Sofia found Elias van Doren in the east corridor after the meeting, standing alone beneath the power cables that ran along the ceiling. They were thick grey lines, pulsing with a faint rhythmic hum that seemed to match the building's breath. She followed one with her eyes. It snaked along the molding, crossed a junction box, and ended at a panel that was not wired to any visible socket. The power simply existed. It fed itself.

He did not turn when she approached, but she saw his fingers tap against his thigh. Once. Twice. Three times. A rhythm he used to steady himself.

"You should be in the common room with the others," she said softly. "It is safer to stay together."

Elias turned slowly. His eyes were dark and tired, ringed with shadows that suggested he had not slept at all. He looked at her for a long moment, and then his lips pressed together in that anxious line she had learned to recognize. He was measuring his words, deciding which ones were safe to shape.

He mouthed them carefully so no sound could escape.

"Thank you."

Sofia's throat tightened. She crossed the space between them before she could think about it. Her hand lifted, hesitated for a fraction of a second, and then pressed flat against the center of his chest, over the folded plane of his sweater.

His heart hammered beneath her palm. Fast. Scared. Alive.

She felt the tremor run through him, the barely contained tension of a boy who had spent his whole life swallowing words because speaking cost him too much. She wanted to absorb it. She wanted to take the weight of his silence and carry it for him. That was her job. That was who she was.

"Don't thank me yet," she whispered. "We don't even know if this theory is right. We don't know anything."

He raised his hand and covered hers. His fingers were cold. His pulse was not. He held her there for a moment longer than necessary, and then he stepped back, letting her hand fall.

The space between them felt colder.

Sofia turned and walked away before she could do something reckless. She did not look back.

The test came that afternoon.

Lea March volunteered before anyone else could. She was Norwegian, quiet, with steady hands and a voice that did not shake when she stood in front of the classroom microphone.

"One phrase," Dmitri instructed. "Simple. In your native language. Say it clearly and do not repeat yourself."

Lea nodded. She took a breath. "Skyen er klar."

The sky is clear.

The microphone above her head pulsed green. The recording light glowed red, held for a beat, then returned to green. The system had captured her voice. Now it was waiting.

The group held their breath.

Nothing happened. No alarms. No distortion. The room stayed silent.

Lea let out a shaky exhale. Pierre grabbed her hand. Yuki buried her face in her arms and cried with relief.

Sofia did not move. She stood at the back of the room, watching the ceiling cables hum, waiting for the dawn.

Dawn came slow and grey.

The group gathered in the dining hall, standing in a loose circle around the speakers embedded in the walls. Lea stood in the center, pale and trembling, holding Pierre's hand.

The speaker crackled.

Lea's voice filled the room, clean and precise, untouched by distortion.

"Skyen er klar."

The silence that followed was the kind that broke people open. Lea let out a sob and collapsed against Pierre. The group exhaled as one, relief flooding through them like a wave.

Dmitri nodded once, sharp and satisfied. "The theory holds. Simple words survive. The system only punishes complexity."

Lea smiled, having survived the perfect translation.

But Sofia could not let the relief settle. She spent the rest of the day replaying the recording in her head, listening for something that felt wrong. That night, when the others had gone to their rooms, she crept to the audio lab and pulled up the wave file.

She slowed it down. She isolated the background frequencies. She scraped the silence at the edges of Lea's voice.

Her fingers went cold.

There was a word buried beneath the static. A whisper tacked onto the end of the file, stretched thin and almost inaudible. A voice she did not recognize.

"Translator."

It lingered for a second longer than the rest.

Only Sofia heard it.
