"If there’s a mistake in the record, we have to correct it—before it kills someone else," Elias van Doren says, voice trembling.

The words crash through the hush of the tribunal chamber, shattering the brittle composure that had settled after the previous session. Tomasz’s fingers freeze over the keys of his terminal. Magda looks up, her mouth pressed into a thin, unreadable line. Even Chief Adjudicator Rasmus, stolid as a courthouse pillar, lets his pen falter mid-signature.

Sofia’s gaze finds Elias at the doorway—his familiar, awkward silhouette outlined by the glass, one hand wrapped around the strap of his satchel as though it’s the only thing keeping him anchored. The stutter in his voice is a tremor in the surface of a pond: barely visible unless one knows where to look, catastrophic beneath. He is not supposed to be here yet. He was meant to recover longer. He never does what he’s told.

Her pulse does not race. If she feels anything, it is a dry, sardonic thought: Of course they brought Elias back for this, as if sincerity alone could disinfect poisoned water.

"Elias, you shouldn’t—" Magda begins, but he cuts her off with a raised hand that wobbles at the wrist.

"No. Please. There’s—there’s something wrong," Elias says, taking one careful step inside. He looks smaller than she remembers. Older, somehow, not by age but by attrition—a candle trimmed too many times. "I’ve reviewed the witness deaths. Three since last week. All— all after corrections to their transcripts."

A hush knits itself over the room—no one dares break the thread.

Rasmus’s tone is meant to reassure, but it hangs like a warning. "You think system error is at fault, Mr. van Doren?"

Elias’s adam’s apple bobs. "I don’t know. But the sequence is odd. The changes aren’t authorial—none of the witnesses requested edits. It’s as if the system rewrites itself. And each time, the next morning—"

"A tragedy," the adjudicator supplies quietly. "Stress does strange things to survivors. You know that as well as anyone."

Sofia lets a breath slip out, slow and measured. The old script. The gentle, inexorable denial of institutional guilt.

Elias flinches at the word survivor, then straightens, desperate and unsteady. He scans the room for an anchor and finds her.

"Sofia was there," he says, the words thick with faith. "She knows how to keep people safe—even at the summit. She did nothing wrong."

There’s a waver in his conviction—not of belief, but of hope. As if his faith in her is the only thing keeping his world from dissolving.

Sofia’s answering glance is liquid steel. Her voice, when she speaks, is gentle—everything she is meant to be: "Elias, I appreciate your trust. I have only ever tried to ease others’ pain. If there’s a flaw in the process, I’ll help you find it. We all want the truth, don’t we?"

She does not add: Especially if I shape it with my own hands.

Rasmus nods, relieved to let her take the reins. "Miss Bellini, if you notice irregularities, please bring them to us. Until then, we’ll trust the system unless proven otherwise." He does not see how deeply their trust cuts both ways.

Pierre Leclerc sits in the far corner, eyes narrowed, his pen hovered but unmoving. He watches Sofia, not Elias—a serpent with patience.

The session resumes with the next witness, a trembling man in his late forties who can’t stop rubbing his thumb over the seam of his folder. His name—Janek—echoes dully through the chamber. His testimony is patchwork: half-formed phrases, apologies, details that deflate mid-sentence.

He falters, searching for the word for smoke—choking, billowing—and finds only silence.

Sofia tilts her head, her words as soft as a silk scarf drawn over a bruise. "Janek, can I help? You said you saw shadows before you smelled smoke. You were near the west stairwell?"

He latches onto the phrasing like a lifeline. "Yes. Yes, before the alarms. There was shouting, and then—"

"And you guided others down the stairs," Sofia finishes, her tone quietly triumphant. "That was brave."

The record will show: Janek, witness, led others down the west stairwell during the initial confusion. It will not show that Sofia’s voice distilled his memory, that her guiding hand shaped the official truth.

Magda’s eyes flick to Sofia, concern knitted between her brows. She doesn’t interrupt. Of course not. They all believe Sofia’s interventions are mercy.

Tomasz, behind the keyboard, hesitates—not typing. His pen rests against the edge of his notepad, capped. For a heartbeat, the blue ink of his last correction glistens wetly. The note’s margin is crowded with a series of identical, tiny marks—corrections in blue, each almost too neat to be human.

Sofia studies the marks out of the corner of her eye. No one’s handwriting is quite so uniform. No one remembers making them.

She files this detail away. Later, it will mean everything.

"Do you remember seeing Nadia Petrescu?" Rasmus asks abruptly, referencing the files stacked at his elbow. "Her disciplinary hearing is noted in the archive. Did she have contact with your group on the day?"

Janek shakes his head fervently, eyes darting. "No, sir. I— I only knew her from reputation. She was already gone from the project by then. I’m sorry."

Rasmus seems satisfied. The hearing is a shadow, an echo—nothing more for now.

Pierre Leclerc’s pen resumes its motion, scrawling a question mark beside the name Petrescu. Sofia watches the rhythm, the slow, deliberate circling. She wonders whether he writes to remember, or to prepare for war.

The official record absorbs everything.

Elias has not sat down. He stands beside Magda, shifting his weight from foot to foot, fidgeting with the strap of his bag. Occasionally his sleeve slips, revealing a nervous thumb pressed white against fabric.

"You’re shaking," Sofia observes quietly, during a lull in the proceedings.

He doesn’t look at her—can’t, not yet. "I wish I could trust the system," he says. "But that’s not enough, is it?"

She leans in, words for his ears alone. "You can trust me."

The tribunal watches, but only for the surface. The air between them is charged—a silent flicker of some deeper current: skepticism and faith entwined, the beginnings of something neither can admit. A glance, heavy with memory, passes between them. For a full second, time pauses. Sofia allows herself to believe she is the benevolent force he needs her to be.

Then a harsh ring breaks the moment—Tomasz’s terminal, signaling a notification.

He scans the screen, brow furrowing. "There’s been another incident," he reports, voice muffled by the weight of implication. "Renata Faber. She—her heart failed, according to the report. She testified three days ago."

A chill passes over the chamber. Rasmus clears his throat, thumbing through pages. "Was there a correction to her testimony?"

"The system logged a revision," Tomasz says, tracing the blue ink margin with a trembling finger. "But she never signed for it. The change is… oddly phrased. Almost too precise."

Pierre leans forward. "Does it alter her meaning?"

Tomasz hesitates, glancing at Sofia unconsciously. "It—reworded her fear. Minimized her warnings. But the statement is clearer, I suppose. Less emotional."

Sofia soothes the room with a gentle assurance. "Perhaps the system seeks clarity. Sometimes our memories are kinder when trimmed of panic."

Elias’s eyes lock on hers—uncertain, pleading. He wants to believe her, but doubt gnaws.

Rasmus brings the hearing to a close, his tone forcedly brisk. "We will review the protocols for transcript correction. Until then, Miss Bellini, Mr. van Doren—your cooperation remains vital. Dismissed for today."

People file out, some exchanging worried glances, others relieved the burden of decision has shifted back to the algorithm.

Sofia waits until the room empties. She retrieves her own copy of the transcript from the table—pages still warm from the copier. She flips through, searching for the place where Janek’s words have been set down.

Her name appears, as expected, in parentheses: (Sofia Bellini, intervening to clarify witness statement.)

But a few lines down, an entry in the same measured, blue-inked clarity stands out:

(Sofia Bellini: "No one will ever know the truth if we keep cleaning up the record for them.")

Her blood goes cold.

She never said those words.

She reads them again, pulse steady but vision suddenly crystalline with suspicion. The phrase is too sharp, edged with defiance—nothing she would risk before this panel. The handwriting, the ink—identical to the corrections she spotted earlier.

Someone has spoken for her. Someone with the authority—and the access—to rewrite the truth.

A surge of calculation flickers through her. If someone else is altering the record, she is not the only force shaping reality here.

Outside, footsteps echo against marble. Voices fade. But in the bright silence of the tribunal chamber, Sofia lingers, finger tracing the impossible line, heart beating with a dangerous new awareness.

If she is not the only one writing history, then her greatest tool—and her greatest threat—has just become invisible.

She looks up, every sense sharpened.

Because if the record can echo with words not her own, then there is someone else in the system with a pen as sharp as hers.

And she is no longer certain who the next casualty will be.
