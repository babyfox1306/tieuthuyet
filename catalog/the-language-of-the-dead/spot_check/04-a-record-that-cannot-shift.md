This is the line that won’t erase — Sofia Bellini built the system. Say it again, for the record.

The words rang, sharp as a gavel, inside the Tribunal’s frost-lit archive. Pierre Leclerc read them aloud, voice brittle, pausing only to let the surveillance mic catch every syllable. His throat tasted of old paper and burnt adrenaline. The room shrank around him, walls lined with case folders warped at the corners, as if pressed between the spines of books that never existed.

He pressed the record button. The red light blinked, insistent. For the record. For the story the system demanded he tell.

"Case Addendum: Code C002," Pierre dictated, careful, almost reverent. "Evidence tendered by Pierre Leclerc, Inspector. Document: System Integration Log, itemized by Sofia Bellini. Signature and authentication code match database entry. Chain of custody unbroken since initial tribunal review."

His finger, ink-stained, hovered over the terminal’s enter key. He hesitated. There was a private superstition in him now—one that spat in the face of protocol—never log an accusation before you’re sure. But the pattern circled, tighter, everything funneled toward her.

Sofia Bellini built the system. Say it again.

He pressed enter. The file pulsed green: C002, Official.

Outside, the corridor’s security glass distorted the world into static lines and gray movement. Pierre checked his notepad, the edges soft with use. He scrawled a note: C002—operational schema, Bellini’s hand evident. He felt that old relief, bitter as cheap gin, seeing how the evidence made its own cage.

He thumbed through the folder, pausing on the integration log. The digital signature: S.BELLINI-ARCH. He could almost hear her voice in the sterile recitation printed beneath.

Every trace pointed to her. Every safeguard, every algorithmic fork, every irreversible verdict—her stamp at the bottom, steady as death.

Pierre’s chest tightened. It couldn’t be this clean. But the system didn’t care about his discomfort. It wanted story, and it wanted it simple: Sofia Bellini built the system. Say it again, for the record.

He shut the folder and walked, brisk, to the interview chamber. The door recognized his ID and hissed open.

Magda Lorenz—no, Magda Leclerc—sat rigid on the far side of the table, hands folded so tightly her knuckles blanched. Her greying hair was pulled into a braid that looked painstaking and recent, as if this morning she’d braided it only to feel her own hands at work.

"Magda," Pierre nodded, voice low. "Thank you for agreeing to speak. This is a formal record."

She offered a ghost of a smile, eyes flicking to the camera in the corner. "No one really agrees, do they? Not when the record is involved."

Pierre pulled out his pen, set it down flat. The shape of it—tapered, scarred—felt like a charm against the Tribunal’s relentless script.

"Tell me about Sofia Bellini. You testified against her at the first tribunal," he started, measured.

Magda’s gaze dropped to the table. "I remember the summons. My statement was… thorough."

He waited, let the silence stand.

She exhaled, brittle. "But what you read in that transcript—that isn’t what I said. Not all of it."

Pierre’s heart thudded. "Be specific. Did you see her author the system?"

Her hands twitched, as if some old electric current ran through her wrists. "I… I saw her name everywhere. On the screens, the folders, every procedural memo. When something broke, they said, ‘Bellini’s fault.’ When it functioned too well, same thing. She was the system as far as the record cared."

He pressed. "Did you ever see her at the core terminal? Did she author those lines of code?"

Magda’s lips thinned. "Would it even matter if I hadn’t?" She shook her head. "By the time I arrived, the record already had its story. Every note I made, every afterthought—by the next day, it vanished. Or it was rewritten to fit. I tried to protest. The system logged my objection as confirmation."

Pierre’s pen tapped, impatient. "But you signed your testimony. You swore an oath."

She met his eyes, and something hollow blinked in them. "And yet here we are. My signature on a script I can’t change."

There was a silence. Pierre steadied, voice turning clinical. "Do you fear Sofia Bellini?"

Magda shook her head slowly. "No. Not her. It’s the record I fear. Once it’s written, it bites. And it never lets go."

He wrote that down. The system wants Bellini guilty—the system never lets go.

He pulled the folder toward her, thumbing to the new page: C002. "Read this. Tell me if anything is wrong."

She scanned the page, blinking. Pierre watched her eyes catch on the signature, the date, the damning language—Sofia Bellini: Design and Implementation, Authenticated.

Magda handed it back, hands shaking. "It’s perfect," she said, and Pierre heard the edge. "Perfect as a lock. There’s no air left in it."

He watched her, uneasy. "Would you change your testimony now, if you could?"

She swallowed. "What difference would it make? The record writes over the world. It wrote over me."

Pierre’s jaw tightened. "But the evidence—"

"Evidence is only what survives the system’s teeth," Magda said, almost a whisper. "Now you’re the one feeding it."

He snapped the folder shut, voice sharper. "Thank you for your clarity." The phrase felt hollow before it left his tongue. He dismissed her, watching her figure shrink as she left, shoulders curved as if bracing for something colder than this room.

He sat alone, pen in hand, folder open to the signature detail: the edge of the tribunal folder, warped, as if it had spent years sandwiched between volumes that never existed. The corners all curled inward, too uniform to be random.

He leaned back, rubbing his thumb over the corner. A suspicion. How many iterations had this archive endured? How many times had these same folders been rewritten, pressed flat then bloated anew?

The air flickered as the door clicked open once more. Sofia Bellini entered, her steps perfectly metered, posture immaculate. She touched her wrist once, lightly, pausing as if reciting her own entrance.

"Inspector Leclerc." Her voice was precise, free of tremor. She slid into the seat across from him, hands clasped, eyes cool.

"Sofia Bellini," Pierre said, letting her name hang like a blade. "You are on record for the integration schema, the code audit, the fail-safe sequence. Every critical path. C002 confirms your authorship again. Do you dispute any part of this?"

She turned her gaze to the folder, mouth set in a disciplined line. "No. I recognize my hand in every document. Every parameter, every safeguard, every potential for unintended consequence was mine to consider."

A pause, deliberate. She touched her wrist again, as if checking her pulse against the metronome of the Tribunal’s need for certainty.

"Yes," she said. "I built the system. I accept responsibility for its design and its failures. This is a matter of public record and personal conviction."

Pierre narrowed his gaze. "You accept responsibility for the Summit tragedies as a direct result of this system?"

Her lips twitched—almost a frown, quickly drowned in resolve. "Cause and consequence are the same, here. The system bears my name; its legacy is mine. I have never denied this. Any evidence to the contrary is a distraction."

Pierre leaned forward, his pen tip tapping the table. "You show no regret. No hesitation."

Sofia regarded him, almost studying his face for deviation. "Regret is irrelevant to the outcome. The record requires clarity, not apology."

He felt the old irritation surface. "The world outside this room can’t erase what happened, Bellini."

Another precise pause. "No one can erase what’s written, Inspector. One can only add. That is all the record allows."

Pierre almost smiled at the absurdity. "Even when it convicts you?"

She nodded, unflinching. "Especially then. My conviction is the keystone of the system. The record is incontrovertible."

The phrase rang in his memory. He’d heard it before—everywhere her name appeared.

He watched her, eyes darting for some crack in her certainty. But she remained perfect, the embodiment of the system’s logic: measured, guiltless, absolute.

"One last question," he said, softer now. "Did you know, as you built it, what the system would become?"

For the first time, Sofia paused too long. Her eyes flicked to the folder, then back up, and for a fraction of a second, the architect seemed hollowed out—empty, like a house with the windows painted on.

She straightened, mask returned. "The system is the sum of its instructions. No more, no less. My intent is irrelevant to the facts."

Pierre closed the folder, feeling suddenly cold. "Thank you, Ms. Bellini. For the record."

She rose, offering no further statement, and left as she had entered—silent, precise, unyielding.

Pierre remained, alone in the bright pitiless light. He gathered the folders, stacking them carefully. They all bore the same warped corners, the same hollow weight.

He moved to the filing terminal, fingers tracing the spine of C002. The official record was tightening, crushing all ambiguity. Sofia Bellini. Architect. Guilty. It spun in his head, a verdict’s refrain.

As he slid the folder into its assigned slot, he noticed something—a page in the transcript newly inserted. The top corner bore two date stamps, stamped one over the other: one from today, crisp black, and beneath it a faded date impossibly old, years before the integration logs themselves.

His breath stalled. He ran a finger across the layered ink. The older date should have been overwritten, yet it lingered like a bruise beneath the new time. Impossible. The system did not allow re-dating. No one could backdate tribunal evidence, not even—

He froze, mind racing. If one date belonged to the present and the other to a past that could never have seen Sofia Bellini’s hand, then what else in the record might not fit?

For the first time, Pierre’s certainty trembled—not at her guilt, but at the thing beneath the surface. The system was shifting, or refusing to shift. And he wasn’t sure which should frighten him more.

He leaned closer, decoding the blurred overlap of years, his thoughts a whirl of dread and hope.

He had just planted the system’s most damning clue against Sofia Bellini. But the page in his hand was proof that the record itself could not decide what story to tell.

And in the chill hush of the archive, Pierre realized: either the world was about to crack open, or he was about to vanish inside its story for good.
