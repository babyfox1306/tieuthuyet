"Would you trust her with your life?" the chief adjudicator asks Elias van Doren, the tribunal chamber silent and expectant.

Elias’s breath catches, just audibly enough that the microphone might catch it if anyone cared to listen. All eyes are on him—except Sofia Bellini’s, which are gentle, unwavering, and already waiting for his answer.

He presses his thumb hard against the table’s edge as if the tactile pain will summon clarity. Trust her. Trust Sofia Bellini, the last surviving witness of the summit, now the tribunal’s benevolent oracle. He remembers the day she pulled him from the smoke, her hand firm, her voice never rising above a murmur. He remembers the transcript—the lines that were never his, and how she soothed him anyway.

He can feel the weight of their gaze, but it is her gaze he cannot escape.

"Yes," Elias says, softly, stammering over the single syllable as if it costs him everything. "I do."

The tribunal exhales—a communal relief, like a dam holding for another day. Chief Adjudicator Rasmus leans back, hands folded, eyes sweeping the semicircle of officials. "Let the record show that Mr. van Doren affirms his faith in Miss Bellini’s integrity and capabilities," he intones, and Tomasz’s keyboard chatters obediently.

But there is a quiver beneath the surface, an electric current that stings rather than soothes.

"Thank you, Mr. van Doren," Sofia says, her tone warm as sunlight through stained glass. "Your faith is not misplaced. Our only enemy is confusion." She inclines her head, offering the tribunal her signature blend of humility and gravitas—the witness who comforts her judges.

A sharp, nervous cough cuts through the moment. Magda Lorenz, Sofia’s mentor, leans forward to whisper into Rasmus’s ear, her hand trembling just enough for Sofia to notice. Sofia marks the rising tension, the way one of the junior officials—Kappel, his name is Kappel, glasses forever smudged—keeps glancing at the evidence case by the wall. That hairline crack, she observes, has crept a few millimeters further since yesterday. She files this away. Everything shifts around the pressure point.

Kappel stands abruptly, his chair shrieking. "There’s—s-sir, the casualty index is—" He fumbles with a tablet, the screen flickering with error codes. "N-names are disappearing from the system queue. I—I don’t know how to—"

Rasmus’s patience is brittle today. "Compose yourself. Nothing can disappear without a trace—"

"Let me help," Sofia says, rising with gentle authority. Her heels hardly make a sound. She crosses to Kappel, her presence a calm in the tremor of his panic. "May I see?"

Kappel blinks at her, grateful and desperate. Sofia takes the tablet, her touch light, words fluid and unhurried. "It’s all right. This is just a rendering error on the secondary display, not the main archive. See?" She switches a setting with a practiced flick, the screen clearing. Her voice is low enough only Kappel and perhaps Elias can hear: "You haven’t lost anyone, Mr. Kappel. The system only misrepresented the queue, not the substance. An easy mistake—common under stress. No harm done."

The room calms as if she’s administered a sedative. Kappel sags in relief, nearly apologizing to his own hands. Sofia returns the tablet with a reassuring smile, her words smoothing his humiliation into something manageable. "We're all under great strain. You acted at once—exactly as you should. You keep the process safe for everyone."

Elias watches this with a complicated ache that knots his throat. How easily she absorbs anxiety, how naturally she edits not just words, but the very emotion of the chamber. Merciful, yes—but the mercy is sharp, and each act binds another layer of trust around her. Or is it control? His doubts are a stone in his stomach, but the room sees only her kindness, her capability.

Chief Adjudicator Rasmus does not miss the way the officials relax. "Order restored, thanks to Miss Bellini’s presence of mind," he says, not without a shadow of irony. "Let the transcript capture that as well."

The system’s printer chuffs in the corner, spitting out another sheet. The scent of ozone is faint, but Sofia notes it, the way the others never do.

Pierre Leclerc enters then, purposeful as always, carrying a slim folder. He does not wait to be recognized. "Adjudicator," he says, "there are anomalies in the archive code. It appears the auto-correction protocol has been rewritten several times in the last forty-eight hours—by whom, it does not say. Some code segments reference external routines that no longer exist."

Rasmus eyes him, then Sofia. "Meaning, Monsieur Leclerc?"

Pierre glances at Sofia, calculation in every line of his posture. "Meaning, the archive is not as unbreakable as we claim. There are ghost entries. Deleted logs. The source is—"

"—Obscure," Rasmus finishes, shutting him down with the authority of the bench. "This is not a technical hearing, Monsieur Leclerc. If you have substantive evidence—"

Pierre bristles. "You asked for transparency. Here it is." He places the folder on the desk, careful but insistent.

Sofia catches his eye. Their duel is always silent, always civil. She allows herself a small smile: she feels for him, really. He is searching for a snake in a world of borrowed skins, and he doesn’t even know which venom to fear.

Rasmus waves a hand. "Tomasz, review those findings. Report any cause for concern."

Tomasz, pale and hurried, nods, already stacking his own pile of CORRECTIONS printouts. His anxiety is a physical thing, curling his shoulders in on themselves.

Magda Lorenz clears her throat. "Perhaps, before we chase ghosts, we should attend to the living. Mr. van Doren’s account remains incomplete, and Miss Bellini’s is our only continuous thread. I recommend we finalize the survivor’s sequence—while we still can."

The phrase hangs in the air: while we still can.

Sofia’s pulse flutters, but outwardly, she is tranquility personified.

Elias’s name is called. The silence that follows is thick, expectant.

He stands slowly. The microphone flickers green. "Elias van Doren. S-survivor, summit, present."

Rasmus’s tone softens, performing sympathy. "You have already spoken to Miss Bellini’s conduct. For the record: in your recollection, did she deviate from protocol or encourage recklessness during the evacuation?"

Elias glances at Sofia. Her eyes hold his—distinct, unwavering, soft as dusk after a storm. There is trust there, a silent request. She lets her gaze rest on him, steady, unguarded, as if she would let him rewrite her in this moment, if only he would.

He swallows. "No," he says, barely above a whisper. "She did not. She…she kept everyone calm. Even when the alarms failed, or when the route was blocked, she—"

"Please, elaborate," Magda says gently.

Elias hesitates, hands fidgeting at his sides. He wants to be careful—wants it so badly, the words almost stick in his throat. What is truth, here, when the record will shape itself around the strong, the merciful? He almost laughs. Instead, he looks at Sofia again. Her presence is a lifeline, but there is a tightness to it now—a weight pressing down.

"She made people feel safe," Elias says. "She got them to speak what they needed. And if they couldn’t, she…helped." Each sentence is both confession and shield; he cannot say more without betraying either her or himself.

"Remarkable poise, under the circumstances," Rasmus notes, satisfied. "Let the transcript show: Miss Bellini acted with prudence and compassion, enabling the orderly evacuation of survivors."

The printer whirs, another page offered to the archive.

Pierre huffs, dryly. "I’m sure the transcript will be grateful."

Sofia gives him a look that is almost affectionate—a teacher’s patience for a clever but stubborn pupil.

Magda, meanwhile, is searching her notes. "There was another issue—the witness profiles for those lost. Mr. van Doren, you were an observer for Nadia Petrescu’s section, were you not?"

The name—Nadia Petrescu—draws a ripple of attention. Only Sofia’s posture betrays anything, the fractional tightening of her hand around her chair’s arm.

"Yes," Elias says. "I was responsible for recording all testimony from her panel."

Magda produces a sheaf of printouts—worn, edges curled where anxious fingers have worried them. "We have fragments only," she explains. "The system purged much in the last cycle, but these remained."

She hands them to Elias with a nod. "You remember her voice?"

"I—I do," he says, but there’s a tremor. Petrescu’s words—odd, sharp, sometimes hurried—surface in his memory, but the written fragments are strange, uneven, like a stammer in code. He scans a note. "This doesn’t match what she said. This—sentence, here. I never wrote it, and I never heard her say it."

Magda looks at him with weary eyes. "That was not in the transcript before the correction?"

"No," he insists, glancing up. His unease is visible now, the fidget of his sleeve betraying him. "There’s something missing. A whole page—it goes straight from the arrival log to the incident report. Nothing about the warning she gave."

Tomasz steps forward, hands trembling. "There are always gaps, now. Not just in her file. The system—it isn’t infallible."

Pierre, watching, pounces. "And when will we admit that deletion is a kind of killing, too?"

Rasmus raises his hand, shutting down the growing discord. "This has become a technical matter. We need order, not speculation. Mr. van Doren, what you recall will suffice for now."

Elias, still holding the fragments, sinks back into his seat. Sofia’s eyes are on him—gentle, worried, but unreadable. She wants him calm, compliant. He feels the urge to apologize for doubting, the urge to say anything that will return him to her orbit.

The officials begin to file out, order restored, but nerves frayed.

Sofia lingers, offering a final smile to Kappel, a reassuring nod to Magda, a calm glance at Pierre before he sweeps out the door in frustrated silence. She controls with kindness—the touch that heals and guides, that edits and soothes. Each favor, a thread wound tighter. The tribunal breathes easier because of her; they can barely see how the walls have shifted.

As the chamber empties, only Elias remains, hunched over the fragments of Nadia Petrescu’s file, the papers trembling in his hands. The evidence case looms in the corner, the crack in the glass a jagged promise across its surface, wider now than ever before.

He orders the pages, checking and rechecking—one, two, three, four—then stops, mouth going dry.

A page is missing.

He looks up, confusion flickering into panic. The chamber is empty, except for Sofia Bellini’s shadow at the door, her gaze fixed not on him, but on the gap in the record—her expression a stillness sharper than any threat.

A single page is gone, and only she knows what it means.
