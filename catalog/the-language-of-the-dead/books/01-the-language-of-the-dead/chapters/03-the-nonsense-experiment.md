"If we stop speaking, we die," Dmitri Ivanov said, his voice a defiant scratch in the perfect silence of the summit. "So let's make the system choke on our words."

Sofia Bellini pressed her palm against her throat, feeling the frantic rhythm of her own pulse. Two days since Amara. Two days of choosing every syllable like a surgeon chooses where to cut. And now Dmitri wanted to detonate the entire operation.

"You don't know what it translates," she said, keeping her voice low. The silence window was still hours away, but caution had already become a reflex. "You don't know the rule. You're guessing."

"We're all guessing." Dmitri paced the length of the common room, his boots echoing against the wooden floor. He was sharp-edged, electric with the conviction that he was the only person in the room thinking clearly. "But I am the only one doing something about it."

Yuki Tanaka wrapped her arms around herself. "Amara did something about it. She spoke normally. She is dead."

"Amara followed the rules of the old world," Dmitri said. "This is a new world. The system runs on language. If we don't understand the engine, we don't survive. We need data."

"We need to stay alive," Sofia shot back.

Dmitri stopped pacing. He looked at her, and his smile was thin and cold. "Same thing. Eventually."

His plan was simple. Stupidly, bloodlessly academic.

At the sunset boundary, when the silence window began, he would speak. A string of nonsense syllables. Pure noise, unattached to any semantic meaning. If the system killed him, they would know it punished all sound. If it didn't kill him, they would know it required meaning to corrupt. And in the morning, the replay would tell them how the system interpreted his sounds.

"A controlled variable," Dmitri said. "Clean data."

"A human life," Sofia said. "Your life."

Dmitri shrugged.

Elias van Doren stood at the back of the room, his shoulders hunched, his hands shoved deep into his pockets. Sofia saw the tension in his jaw, the way his fingers tapped against his thigh in a desperate rhythm. He had something to say.

Tap, tap, tap.

The stutter locked his throat, but his eyes were screaming.

Sofia translated without thinking. It was a compulsion, an instinct she could not override. "Elias thinks the risk is too high. There are other ways to test the system. Ways that don't require us to volunteer for death."

Elias's eyes met hers. Grateful. Trapped.

The moment she said the words, a cold prickle ran down her spine. The system had recorded her. Sofia Bellini, speaking for Elias van Doren. Translating his silence into sound.

She was making him visible. And visible people died here.

But what else could she do? Let him drown in his own locked throat?

The sunset came like a held blade.

The twelve remaining students gathered in the main atrium. The sky outside was a bleeding wound of orange and red, the light casting long, monstrous shadows across the marble floor.

Dmitri stood apart from the group, alone at the center of the room.

"If I die," he said, "tell the next summit that the system is a sieve, not a wall. It doesn't block sound. It filters meaning."

Then he opened his mouth.

"Klathra brevin sotoru veshta. Karah din morath. Torah sistra kon vael."

The sounds were hollow, empty vessels. He had assembled them from dead languages and invented roots, careful to give them no semantic weight.

The building absorbed them in silence.

The sunset died. The silence window rose like a curtain.

Dmitri was still standing.

He let out a shaky breath. "The first step."

Sofia hated him for the relief in his voice.

None of them slept.

At dawn, the speakers emitted a low hum. The translation system was waking.

Sofia stood at the edge of the room, Elias beside her. She was aware of his warmth, the steady rhythm of his breathing. He was the only anchor in a world of loose sand.

The recording played.

Dmitri's voice, clean and unfamiliar. Then the twisting. The warping. The shimmer as the translation algorithm searched for meaning it could not find.

And then it found one.

The speakers spat out a single sentence, rendered in the common trade language, crisp and unmistakable:

"Sofia Bellini dies first."

The room went cold.

Everyone turned to look at her.

Yuki's hand flew to her mouth. Pierre stepped backward, his face pale. A murmur rippled through the group.

"The system knows her name."

"It chose her."

"She's the target."

Dmitri was staring at the speakers, his lips parted, his eyes bright with terrible excitement. "It interpreted my sounds. It found a pattern. It generated a threat."

"Against Sofia," Yuki said, her voice shaking. "Why? Why her?"

Fear curdled into accusation.

"She's the one who speaks for everyone."

"Maybe the system is punishing that."

"Maybe we shouldn't let her speak anymore."

"Maybe we should—"

Elias moved.

He didn't shout. He didn't argue. He simply stepped forward, placing his body directly between Sofia Bellini and the gazes of the other students.

His silence was heavier than any weapon. His jaw was set, his fingers still tapping a frantic rhythm against his thigh, but he did not waver. His eyes swept across the assembled group, daring them to say another word.

No one did.

Sofia felt her heart crack and mend in the space of a single breath.

She grabbed Dmitri's arm and pulled him into the hallway, away from the group.

"You used me," she hissed. "You knew the system needed a target. You fed it nonsense and let it aim at me."

Dmitri pulled his arm free. "I didn't aim it. The system aimed itself. That's the point, Sofia. The system chose you. Why? What does it see in you that it doesn't see in the rest of us?"

"I don't care what it sees. Next time you experiment, you make yourself the target. You tell the system to kill Dmitri Ivanov. You speak your own death into being."

"That's not how science—"

"I am not a variable in your equation." Her voice cracked, but she didn't care. "Amara was a person. I am a person. If you want to test the walls, you throw yourself against them. Not me."

Dmitri stared at her. For a fraction of a second, she saw something behind his arrogance. Fear. Real, cold, human fear.

"Fine," he said. "Next time, I speak my own name."

It was not a promise. It was a surrender.

The group dispersed, raw and shaken.

Sofia stayed in the archive room, the audio recording pulled up on the console. She played Dmitri's replay again. And again.

The translated threat was clear. But she was listening for something else.

A whisper. A shadow in the sound.

She filtered the audio, isolating the lower frequencies. The synthetic voice of the translation system was smooth, genderless, perfect.

Beneath it, barely audible, was a woman.

The sound was thin. Old. Strained. Like a voice recorded on wax cylinder a hundred years ago.

It spoke a single word.

"Listen."

Sofia's blood turned to ice.

She played it again.

"Listen."

The word was not part of Dmitri's utterance. It was not part of the translation. It was a ghost in the machine, a prisoner buried beneath the algorithm.

She turned off the console. The silence of the summit pressed against her ears.

A current of cold air brushed her cheek.

She looked up. The ventilation grille above her desk was sealed shut, welded into place. But the air was moving. Fresh air, carrying the faintest smell of old paper and dust.

Someone was in the walls of the summit with them.

Someone was speaking from beneath the translation system.

And they wanted Sofia to listen.
