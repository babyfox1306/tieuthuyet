The correction count for Nadia Petrescu was 47. Elias van Doren's was 63. Sofia Bellini stared at the numbers until they blurred.

The interpreter booth had unlocked three minutes after the early replay ended, releasing them into a corridor lit by pulsing red emergency strips. Now the others crowded around the control-room desk while the building hummed beneath their feet, steady as a machine digesting a meal.

Yuki leaned over Sofia's shoulder. "Are those all translations?"

"No." Sofia touched her throat, then forced her hand down. "That's what we got wrong."

The screen showed two columns pulled from different parts of the closed system. Nadia's figure came from the disciplinary archive. Elias's had appeared when Sofia connected his earpiece to the old correction terminal.

Sixty-three.

Not mistranslations. Not distorted words. Corrections.

Every time Elias had paused and Sofia had finished.

Every time his fingers had tapped against a table while she supplied the word he was fighting to reach.

Every time someone had looked impatient and she had stepped in, polished his meaning, made his voice smoother for everyone else.

Useful Sofia. Helpful Sofia. Sofia Bellini, professionally trained human eraser.

Dmitri planted both palms on the desk. "This proves nothing. Petrescu's file has hundreds of alterations."

"Three hundred forty-two words altered," Yuki said quietly. "But forty-seven recorded interventions."

Pierre looked up from the stack of copied logs. "Sessions."

Sofia's gaze snapped to him.

He slid one sheet free. "Each correction event has a timestamp. One official could change ten words in a single interruption, but the system marks that as one act. Someone takes control of the witness's meaning. Forty-seven times for Nadia."

"And sixty-three for Elias," Yuki said.

Elias stood across the desk, too still except for the fingers of his right hand. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap. His eyes stayed on the number beside his name.

Sofia wanted to tell him it might be wrong.

The urge rose instantly, viciously familiar. Give him an easier version. Put a soft edge around the blade before handing it over.

She bit her lower lip hard enough to taste blood.

No.

He deserved the blade exactly as it was.

Dmitri snatched the page from Pierre. "Then why is Petrescu here? She died decades ago."

"We don't know when she died," Pierre said.

"You know what matters?" Dmitri jabbed at the screen. "Amara was mistranslated. Lea's sentence was not. Our theory failed because we measured sentence accuracy, not cumulative distortion. The system kills whoever's words are mistranslated the most."

The statement landed with the clean appeal of a locked door. Simple. Familiar. Wrong.

Sofia looked at Lea's entry on the terminal.

The sentence about the chairs had received zero translation errors. Yet beside Lea's name was another figure.

Twenty-nine corrections.

Her stomach tightened.

"Lea asked us questions," Sofia said.

Yuki frowned. "What?"

"After Amara died. Lea panicked. She kept asking what the rules meant." Sofia pulled up the daily interaction logs. "We answered for her before she finished. All of us."

Pierre moved closer. "How many times?"

Sofia scrolled through the marked transcripts. Each intervention carried a narrow gray bracket. The system had catalogued interruptions that none of them had considered important.

Dmitri telling Lea what she was trying to ask.

Pierre explaining what Lea must have meant.

Sofia turning Lea's frightened fragments into calm, complete English for the group.

Twenty-nine acts of interpretation.

Amara's count had been twenty-four.

Lea's had risen past it the evening before her death.

The correction marks were not attached to inaccurate output. They were attached to ownership.

Sofia's locket caught against the edge of the desk as she bent closer. She fidgeted with the chain, trying to ignore the cold spreading through her chest.

"Open Nadia's original transcript," she said.

Pierre found the file. The terminal fought him with a frozen loading symbol, then displayed two versions side by side.

On the left, Nadia's testimony appeared in fragmented lines. On the right, the official record flowed in polished paragraphs.

Sofia scanned until she found the phrase from the damaged audio.

Nadia: The door was open before the meeting. Someone had already—

Official: The witness confirms she entered without authorization.

Nadia: That is not what I said.

Official record: The witness disputes the wording but agrees with the substance.

Sofia stopped breathing.

There it was. The fragment that had survived the corrupted tape. Not a correction of language, but a refusal that had been rewritten into agreement.

"Look at the event codes," Yuki said.

Beside every official interruption sat the same tiny symbol: a circle with a line driven through the shape of an ear.

Sofia pulled out her earpiece.

The symbol was molded into its translucent casing.

For days, that logo had rested against her skin. Not the mark of a translation company. Not accessibility technology. A warning label disguised as branding.

The correction log and the earpieces belonged to the same process.

Pierre stared at the symbol. "The system wasn't built from general language data."

"No." Sofia's voice came out flat. Sharp. "It learned from this."

She opened the metadata Yuki had recovered from the sealed archive. Training source: NP hearing corpus. Response target: intervention frequency. Identity degradation threshold: cumulative.

Nadia had tried to testify. Officials had turned every hesitation into permission to replace her words. The machine had learned that the danger was not broken grammar or imperfect translation.

The danger was being overwritten.

Sofia looked at Elias.

He met her eyes at once.

He always did, but only with her.

The room narrowed to the distance between them and the ugly number glowing on the screen. Sixty-three times. Her helpfulness had not protected him from the system. It had presented him to it.

"The system kills whoever has been spoken for the most — trained on Nadia Petrescu's rewritten testimony."

No one moved.

The hum beneath the floor seemed to deepen, as if the building had heard its own law spoken aloud.

Elias pressed his lips together. His tapping stopped.

Sofia hated that stillness more than panic. Panic fought back. Stillness accepted the blow.

Dmitri recovered first. "Count everyone."

Yuki took the left terminal. Pierre dragged a second keyboard closer. Names filled the central screen as they searched the correction database.

Sofia worked without speaking. The records were brutally precise. Every completed sentence. Every paraphrase. Every moment one person had claimed to know what another intended.

Yuki: sixteen.

Pierre: twenty-two.

Dmitri: eleven, despite his nonsense experiment. The system did not care that his sounds had become a threat. No one had spoken on his behalf afterward.

Sofia: thirty-eight.

Then Elias.

Sixty-three.

The highest living count by a margin so wide that Sofia's vision flashed white.

"No," she said.

The word escaped before she could stop it.

Elias looked at her.

She curled both hands around the desk edge. "There has to be a way to reduce it."

Pierre searched the archive index. "No negative values. No reversals."

"Then increase someone else's."

Yuki turned toward her slowly. "Sofia."

"If the target is the highest count, the rest of you can speak for Sofia until dawn."

Elias's voice broke on the first sound. "N-no."

She faced him. "That wasn't a request."

His fingers began tapping against his thigh, fast and uneven.

Sofia knew the rhythm. He was building a sentence, word by word, while everyone else waited. She had once thought filling the silence was kindness.

Now she let it remain his.

"No," he said again, stronger.

"Excellent argument. Very detailed."

His eyes flashed. The old spark between them survived even here, even with death performing arithmetic on a screen.

Yuki stepped between the terminals. "Moving the highest count would only choose a different victim."

"It would choose someone with a plan."

"It would choose you," Elias said.

The sentence came with a hitch between the last two words, but he finished it himself.

Sofia's throat tightened.

Dmitri pulled up the building schedule. "The next replay starts at dawn. Forty-one minutes."

A countdown appeared above the names.

00:41:00.

Then 00:40:59.

Pierre pointed to a block of system text. "There is a live-input stage before the replay. One registered speaker. The interface calls it witness confirmation."

"Of course it does," Sofia muttered. "Nothing says ethical technology like vocabulary stolen from an interrogation."

"If Elias gives his own statement," Yuki said, "maybe the system recognizes that no one is controlling his meaning."

"Maybe?" Sofia asked.

Yuki held her gaze. "There is no safe answer."

A sharp electronic click came from the speaker above them.

Sunset protocol ended. Dawn-processing status lights flickered amber around the room, permitting speech until the final silence phase.

Elias walked around the desk.

Sofia refused to step back. Guilt wanted to hollow her out, but guilt was lazy unless turned into action. She would not waste the remaining minutes drowning in it.

"I did this," she told him. "Not on purpose, but intention is a lovely little excuse people use after the damage becomes measurable."

He shook his head.

"Do not rescue Sofia from the truth."

His fingers lifted between them, almost reaching for her, then stopped. The space between his hand and hers felt more intimate than touch.

"You listened," he said.

"Apparently not enough."

"You... waited."

"Not enough."

"You saw me."

Her sarcasm failed her for one dangerous second.

Elias drew a breath. His lips pressed together, released, then pressed again. Each attempt seemed to pass through him physically, shoulders tight, fingers poised in the air.

Sofia stayed silent.

He deserved time.

The countdown reached thirty-seven minutes.

His gaze held hers with nowhere to hide. "You never made me feel broken."

Her chest ached. "The machine has sixty-three counterarguments."

"The machine is wrong."

"The machine is deadly."

"Still wrong."

Sofia looked at his hovering hand. "You should hate me."

His brow tightened, anger cutting through his anxiety. "Don't tell me what I should feel."

The rebuke struck exactly where it needed to.

She had almost done it again. Taken even his anger and translated it into something she could carry.

"You're right," she said.

He swallowed. His fingers tapped once against his thumb.

Then he spoke without a stutter.

"I love you, Sofia Bellini."

The words were quiet, complete, and entirely his.

No borrowed rhythm. No helpful ending. No hand reaching in to drag him across a pause.

Sofia forgot the countdown. She forgot the terminals and the watching group and the earpiece biting into her palm.

For one impossible heartbeat, there was only Elias looking directly at her, terrified but unflinching.

Her instinct screamed for an answer. Something clever. Something equally complete.

But this moment belonged to his voice.

She closed her fingers around the locket at her throat. "That was unfairly well timed."

A corner of his mouth moved.

Her own smile hurt. "And when dawn is over, Elias van Doren, there will be an answer. You will hear every word."

"When," he repeated.

"Yes. When."

The terminal chimed.

00:30:00.

A mechanical voice filled the control room. "Witness confirmation required. Select primary speaker."

Every name appeared on the screen.

Sofia reached for Elias's entry, then stopped before touching it. Choosing for him would be one more interpretation disguised as protection.

Elias stepped beside her and pressed his own name.

The circle-and-ear symbol flashed across every monitor.

PRIMARY SPEAKER: ELIAS VAN DOREN.

Dmitri swore under his breath. Pierre began checking microphone levels. Yuki found a control that delayed automatic translation, but only during the first statement.

"One chance," she said. "After that, the system processes whatever it hears."

Sofia removed Elias's earpiece.

He let her hold it, but she did not touch him. Not when even care could become another claim.

The ceiling lights dimmed. A red line illuminated the path from the terminal to the central speaker grille.

00:10:00.

The building locked the doors with a heavy sequence of bolts.

No escape. No second room. No place beyond the reach of the microphones.

Elias took his position on the marked tile.

Sofia stood behind the glass partition with the others, every muscle demanding that she cross the room and give him the words that might save him.

She kept her mouth closed.

00:01:00.

The warning tone began, one pulse per second.

Elias lifted his chin. His fingers tapped against his leg, shaping a rhythm only he understood.

00:00:10.

Sofia pressed her hand to the glass.

He looked at it, then at her.

00:00:05.

The dawn countdown began. Elias van Doren stood in front of the speaker, his eyes on Sofia, ready to be the one who speaks without help.
