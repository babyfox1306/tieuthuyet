"This page is growing veins," Clara said, and the sleeve split wetly down its sealed edge.

She had been holding the sun-charred fragment from the fourth day's test, the one she had slipped into a Mylar sleeve to protect it from further damage. The transparent polyester had separated along the heat-sealed border, not from pressure but from something inside. The paper's surface had lifted into fine vertical threads so thin they caught the low light like spider silk. They moved. Not with the draft—with their own slow crawl, threading through the gap in the sleeve and reaching toward the nearest dry surface.

Clara set the fragment on the worktable and peeled the sleeve apart. Inside, the charred paper had softened at the edges, the fibers unspooling into black strands that branched and re-branched. They had laced the protected margins of the fragment while the loose charcoal ash an inch away remained perfectly inert. No movement. No connection. Only the sleeved fragment had drawn the growth. The ash lay in a small pile where she had swept it two days ago, and not a single grain had shifted.

She checked her fingertips for ink stains, found none, and straightened the corner of the folder beside her. The watch in her pocket had wound itself to its limit already. She pulled it out, flipped the lid, and watched the seconds hand sweep past the Roman numerals without registering the time. The habit of cataloguing every detail pressed against her panic like a hand against a cracked window.

The rescued folder from the second day sat on the far end of the table, its acid-free board darkened along the bottom edge from prolonged contact with the table's moisture. She had separated and sleeved those pages thinking she was isolating contamination, giving the clean records a chance to survive the flooded observatory's rot. She had treated each sheet with the same care she would have given any historical document: gentle dry-cleaning with a soft brush, rehousing in archival polyester, labeling in pencil with the original shelf location and approximate date. The folder was the most meticulously preserved set in the entire archive.

She opened it.

The interior was a root network.

The pages she had saved lay stacked in order, but the black vascular growth had turned the stack into a single organic mass. Veins of dark ink had punched through all four sleeves, connecting sheet to sheet, folder board to folder board, and spreading outward into the table's wood grain. The density of branching here dwarfed anything she had seen in the unsorted piles. The roots had thickened to the width of her smallest finger, and at the center of the cluster a kind of pulp had formed, as if the paper had partially dissolved and re-formed into something closer to living tissue. She could see the original handwriting faintly through the roots, the entries she had tried to save, now almost completely absorbed.

Clara's breath went shallow. She did not reach in to touch it. She did not need to. The pattern was legible.

She set the folder aside and turned to a neglected scrap she had left on a lower shelf three days ago, a torn half-page she had deemed too damaged to warrant preservation. It lay uncovered on the wood, its edges curling from humidity. She picked it up with tweezers. The black stain was present but thin, a few faint branches that had desiccated and stopped. No active growth. No breakthrough. The paper was still paper.

She pulled a second scrap from her original sorting pile, another rejected page that had never seen a sleeve. Same result. The black stain had spread only as far as the water damage had carried it, then dried into a brittle film that crumbled when she touched it with the tweezers. The fibers had separated but not into active strands—just decay.

She selected a third scrap, a leaf that had been lying in the same damp corner as the rescued folder but had never been handled. It was equally stained but the branching was anemic, the threads thin and broken.

Clara turned back to the sleeved pages. She chose one that she had cleaned, numbered, and sealed in polyester. She laid it beside an unsleeved page of similar age and condition. The difference was unmistakable. The protected sheet hosted a dense, living branch structure that extended beyond the original stain. The unsleeved sheet held a stain, nothing more—a passive trace of the ink's presence, not an active network.

She checked another pair. Same result. Protected records drew stronger branching. Every time.

She picked up the sleeved page and brought it closer to the light. The roots on its surface had developed tiny tendrils that lifted individual grains of charcoal ash from the surrounding dust, arranging them along the margins of the page in neat rows, like punctuation without words. The level of intentionality in the arrangement was unmistakable. The ink was using the charcoal to write something—or remember something—that required a material heavier than paper.

She set the page down. Her hands were shaking.

The watch in her pocket had wound itself again, and she pulled it out, flipped the lid, stared at the face without reading it. The seconds hand swept past the Roman numerals as if nothing had changed. But everything had changed.

Preservation did not isolate contamination. It fed it.

The paper she had protected had not stayed clean because she had sleeved it. It had become a conduit. The roots had used the preserved environment to strengthen themselves, to bridge from sheet to sheet, to build tissue structure. The fragments she had ignored—the ones she had considered too degraded or unimportant—those had starved. They had dried. They had stayed inert because she had not given them the protection that the ink needed to grow.

Clara set the watch on the table and pressed both palms flat against the edge, feeling the grain of the wood. The moisture in the air clung to her sleeves. The observatory's archive was not a collection of records. It was a cultivated feeding ground. And she had been the gardener.

A sound escaped her throat—not a word, just a thin, cut-off exhale. She bit the inside of her cheek until the taste of blood reminded her to breathe.

She walked the length of the table to the far shelf where she had stacked the unsorted materials from the lower chamber. The stack had been her backlog, the items she had not yet had time to process. She had meant to clean and sleeve them next. Now she stared at them as if they were fuses.

Her eye caught a label she had overlooked. It was stamped on the edge of a rusted metal shelf bracket, partially obscured by a fold of water-stained paper. "LM-1847-09 — Lower Mezzanine — Peat Core Accession." Below it, in faint pencil, a note: "Submerged shelf. Not inventoried per curator request." She pulled the paper aside. The bracket led downward, past the current waterline, to a shelf that should have been visible if the water level had been lower. But the water was high, and the shelf was deep enough that she had not noticed it before. She crouched and peered into the dark water sloshing between the floorboards. There was something down there—a dark mass, submerged, possibly a box or a tied bundle. She strained to see but the water was opaque, the sediment thick. She traced the bracket with her finger and felt a sequence of numbers carved into the metal: 1847-09-12. A curator's code she had never seen before. She made a mental note of the reference, filed it in the part of her mind that still catalogued everything, but did not reach in. The water was cold and utterly black.

She straightened and wiped her hands on her trousers. Her fingertips came away clean this time.

She returned to the table and looked at the folder again, the one with the root mass. She had to choose. She had to act. She knew now that every page she preserved gave the ink more structure. But she could not bring herself to discard the records—to let them rot, to let them be forgotten. That was the core of her. And the roots knew it.

The charred fragment lay on the table, its new roots still reaching across the Mylar surface. The sunbeam from the observatory's broken dome panel had shifted across the floor while she had worked, and now it fell across the corner of the table, lighting the dust motes in the air. If she placed the fragment in the light, the roots would freeze. The cellulose would char. The preservation would end.

She reached out and picked up the fragment. The roots did not resist. They threaded over her fingers, searching, expectant. She carried it to the sunbeam. The light was warmer here, almost white. She raised the page toward the beam.

Her hand stopped.

Not because her muscles gave out. Not because the roots held her. But because something else—something deeper than instinct—refused to let the page meet the light. She had spent her entire adult life protecting written things. She had never destroyed a document. She had never wanted to. And even now, knowing that this page was feeding the corruption, she could not complete the motion.

The sunbeam stayed an inch from the paper's edge.

Clara's arm trembled.

And the roots climbed over her knuckles. They moved slowly, deliberately, spreading across the back of her hand, tracing the lines of her veins. They did not hurt. They did not tighten. They simply recognized her refusal.

She stood frozen, the page held in the light's edge but not consumed, the roots wrapping her hand in silent gratitude.

No one told her to burn it. No one forgave her if she did. No one stopped her when she protected it anyway.

She lowered the page.

The sunbeam fell empty on the table.

And Clara Vance, archivist, pulled the infected fragment against her chest and held it like a thing she had been asked to keep safe.
