The safehouse was cold, but the silence between them was colder. Sloane Mercer pressed her thumb into the palm of her left hand and felt the answer rise through her bones. The rhythm was there, buried beneath three months of stolen memory, waiting for her to stop fighting long enough to listen.

She did not know when she had started the motion. It was a reflex now, her thumb pressing small circles into her palm, the same unconscious gesture she had used for weeks. But this time, her fingers found a pattern. A sequence. Three slow circles, two fast, a pause, then three more. Her body repeated it without permission.

Lucian stood by the window, his back to her, scanning the street below. The rifle lay disassembled on the table between them, its components gleaming under the single lamp. He had not spoken since they reached the safehouse. The capture of Mara had carved a silence into him that Sloane did not know how to breach.

“Lucian.”

He turned, one hand still resting on the window frame. His eyes were flat, watchful, the same look he had worn when Rook's men had closed around Mara and he had been forced to pull her away instead of charging in.

“I know where the key is,” she said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. The rhythm in her palm had unlocked something, a muscle memory that did not belong to her conscious mind. She pressed her thumb into her left wrist, tracing the familiar circle, and felt a surge of certainty.

“Where?” His voice was low, stripped of everything but the question.

“Here.”

She crossed the room to the bookshelf. It was sparse, a few old paperbacks and a brass lamp, the kind of anonymous furniture that came with a safehouse. But her feet stopped in front of a specific spot, her hand reaching for a gap between two books. Her fingers found a seam in the wood panel behind them.

She pressed her thumb against the seam and traced the circle. Three slow, two fast, three slow. A click sounded inside the wall, soft as a whisper.

A section of the panel swung open.

Nested inside was a compartment lined with gray felt, the same material that lined her jacket pocket. She had noticed that detail weeks ago, the way her coat seemed to have a hidden pocket she never remembered using. Now she understood why. Her past self had chosen the lining for this hiding place, knowing her future self would recognize the texture.

Inside the compartment lay a single key card. It was unmarked except for a faded logo on its corner. A shipping container. A terminal.

She picked it up. The plastic was warm against her fingers, as if it had been waiting for this moment.

“Full ledger storage,” she said, turning it over. “This is the access. The rest of it is at the shipping terminal.”

Lucian moved closer, his footsteps silent on the worn carpet. He studied the key card without reaching for it. “Rook will have Mara there. He knows we’ll come for her.”

“He’s counting on it.” Sloane slid the key card into her jacket pocket. “That’s the trap. He wants us in the open, chasing the ledger so he can close the net.”

“And you’re going anyway.”

It was not a question.

She met his eyes. “She’s my sister. She did this for me. She let herself be shot, let the world think she was dead, so I could bring down Rook’s network. I’m not leaving her in his hands.”

Lucian’s jaw tightened. He said nothing for a long moment. Then his hand lifted, not to stop her, but to brush a strand of hair from her face. The touch was light, almost hesitant, as if he was not sure he still had the right.

“You wrote the binding condition,” he said. “You ordered your own death if Rook ever restored the obedient identity. Do you still trust that decision?”

Sloane’s throat closed. She remembered the words from the evidence she had pieced together. CLIENT TERMINATION AT SUNRISE. And beneath it, the true condition: Lucian must kill Sloane at sunrise only if Rook restores the obedient identity. She had written those words in her own hand, three months ago, planning for a future she could not remember.

Did she trust it? She did not know the woman who had made that decision. But she knew the woman who had staged her sister’s death, erased her own memory, and hidden a key card in a felt-lined compartment. That woman had been willing to die to stop Rook.

“I don’t know if I trust the decision,” she said. “But I trust that I made it for a reason I can’t see yet.”

Lucian’s hand slid to cup her jaw. His palm was warm against her skin, rough with calluses, and the tenderness in the gesture made her chest ache. She did not pull away. She leaned into his touch, closing her eyes for a single breath, letting herself feel the weight of his hand, the fragile safety of this moment before the world broke open again.

When she opened her eyes, his face was inches from hers.

“Then I’ll follow you,” he said. “To the terminal. Through whatever comes after.”

She wanted to say something, to promise him that she would find a way out of the condition she had written. But she had no words for that. So she simply pressed her hand over his, holding it against her cheek for one more heartbeat.

Then she stepped back.

“We need to move. Rook won’t wait for sunrise.”

They gathered what they needed. Lucian reassembled the rifle with practiced efficiency, his hands moving over the components in a rhythm as familiar as Sloane’s wrist circle. She checked her weapon, a compact pistol she had taken from the safehouse’s cache. It was lighter than she preferred, but it would do.

The safehouse door opened onto a narrow alley. The air was cold, heavy with the damp of pre-dawn. Streetlights cast pools of orange light on the wet pavement, and the city beyond was quiet, the kind of hush that came before the first birds.

Sloane did not look back at the safehouse. She did not think about the felt-lined compartment or the key card in her pocket. She thought about Mara, tied to a chair in some shipping container, waiting for her sister to come.

They took a car from the safehouse garage. Lucian drove, his eyes fixed on the road ahead, while Sloane stared out the passenger window, tracing the circle against her palm again and again. Three slow, two fast, three slow. The pattern felt like a prayer.

The shipping terminal appeared on the horizon, floodlights cutting through the pre-dawn dark. Cranes rose against the gray sky like skeletal towers, and rows of containers stretched into the distance, a maze of steel and shadow.

Sloane Mercer checked her weapon. The weight of it was familiar, grounding.

“We get Mara,” she said. “Then we burn the whole thing down.”

Lucian only nodded, eyes on the floodlit maze ahead. The key card sat hard against her thigh through the coat pocket—proof she already carried what Rook would kill to keep buried. She checked the magazine once more and let her breath settle into the same three-two-three rhythm her palm had been counting since the garage.

Outside, the street waited. Mara was still out of reach. Sunrise was still coming. And for the first time since the rooftop, Sloane Mercer knew which direction to run.
