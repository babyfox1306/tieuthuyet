The safety-deposit box contained a single envelope, but the handwriting on the front was her own, the letters slightly widened—as if written by a woman who expected never to read them again.

Sloane traced the ink with her fingertip. The vault basement hummed with recycled air, the fluorescent lights casting everyone in shades of guilt. She did not look back at Lucian, but she felt his presence in the weight of his silence.

"I don't remember choosing this code," she said. "Mara chose it. Our mother's birthday, reversed."

"You chose her," Lucian said. "You trusted her with the key."

The words hit harder than she expected. She had trusted Mara. She had made her sister an accomplice. And then she had made her a target.

Sloane slid the envelope open.

Inside was a spiral notebook, cover worn, the corners bent from being carried through too many pockets. She opened it. The first page was her handwriting.

Project Vertigo — Final Sequence.

She read the plan she had written. The words were clinical, precise. The mind of an investigator constructing a perfect trap. The pages were filled in sections, the handwriting changing from controlled to urgent and back again. She read about the discovery of Rook's network. The seven altered files. The corruption at the heart of the agency. The decision to bait a trap.

She had written the plan in stages. The first draft was careful, measured. The second was ruthless. The final version was the one she had followed.

Mara will give the mirror-key signal. Three fingers, a reflex. It means she consents to the play. Ana Castillo taught her the rhythm. It is unbreakable. Under stress, she will do it. Lucian will see it through the scope. He will know she is alive. He will follow the contract.

Sloane's thumb pressed against her own wrist. The mirror-key. A muscle memory. A signal. Her sister had agreed to be shot. She had agreed to play dead. And Lucian had seen it, had known she was alive, and had fired anyway because Sloane had asked him to.

The layers of the deception were staggering. She had planned her own sister's staged death. She had erased her own memory. She had crafted a trap for Rook that required her to believe her own lies.

I will not remember. That is the only way it works. Rook will believe he is designing the game. I must let him think he is winning. The trap is only complete if he never sees it coming.

She turned the page. The postscript.

If I am reading this, the trap has sprung. If I am reading this, I have lost myself. And Rook thinks he has won.

Sloane traced the words with her fingertip. Her past self had known. She had known how deep the fall would be, and she had written herself a rope.

The relief that Mara was alive was sharp, immediate, followed by something colder. She had made her sister a target. She had made Lucian a tool. She had made herself the bait.

You were capable of this.

She shut the notebook. Her hand was shaking.

"Time," Lucian said.

She looked up. He was standing at the vault door, body angled toward the corridor. Someone was coming.

"How many?"

"One. Armed."

Sloane tucked the journal into her jacket. The photograph slid out, and she caught it. Mara's face. The teardrop track dried on the glossy side. She must have cried over this. She didn't remember the grief, only the evidence of it.

The operative stepped into view. Lucian moved before the man could raise his weapon. A grunt. A body hitting the floor.

It was over in seconds.

Lucian turned to her. "We have to go."

"Wait."

She walked to him, the journal heavy against her ribs. She stopped close enough to see the rapid pulse in his throat, the way his jaw tightened.

"How long have you known?"

"Since you gave me the contract."

"And you didn't tell me."

"You didn't ask."

"I am asking now."

He looked at her. The corridor was cold, the operative's body a shadow at his feet, but the heat in his eyes was a different kind of trap. She read the cost there. The lines around his mouth. The shadows beneath his eyes. He was not lying to protect himself. He was protecting her from a truth she had written with her own hand, in a notebook she could not remember filling.

"You knew she was alive," she said. "You knew it was a play."

"I knew the contract you gave me. I followed it."

"You let me grieve."

"I grieved too."

His voice cracked on the last word. She saw it then. The loss he carried. He had lost the woman who wrote the plan. The woman who had loved him. The woman who had erased herself to save them both.

"I am sorry," she whispered.

"For what?"

"For making you carry it alone."

He did not answer. He did not have to. The silence was the answer.

"What aren't you telling me?" she asked.

"Something you aren't ready to hear."

"Try me."

"Not yet."

"The contract," she said. "The one I gave you. What did it really say?"

He met her eyes. "Client termination at sunrise."

"That is the surface. What is the clause?"

He did not answer. The silence was louder than any confession.

"You cannot tell me," she said. "I asked you not to."

"You forbade me to."

"And you are loyal to a dead woman's command."

"I am loyal to the woman who gave it. The same woman who is standing in front of me, asking questions she does not want the answers to."

The words hit like a blow. She wanted to argue. She wanted to force the truth out of him. But she knew, with a certainty that settled in her bones, that he was right. She was not ready. Not yet.

She held his gaze. The silence stretched, and in it, she saw the love he carried, the patience he had, the grief he refused to show. He loved the woman who had written the plan. He trusted the woman she had become to finish it.

The weight of that trust was unbearable.

"Sloane," he said, voice low. "You trusted me then. Can you trust me now?"

She wanted to say no. She wanted to say she trusted no one, that the only person she could rely on was the stranger she saw in the mirror.

Instead, she whispered, "I am trying."

His thumb brushed her jaw. A ghost of a touch, gone before she could lean into it. But the contact was enough.

"We finish this," she said. "We find Rook. We find Mara. And then you tell me everything."

"And then I tell you everything."

Her phone vibrated against her hip.

She pulled it out. Unknown number.

One line of text.

Did she tell you that?

Sloane Mercer's blood went cold. Not because she received an answer. But because she knew now, for the first time, that she had built this entire night to beat Cassian Rook. Every record she had cracked, every breadcrumb she had followed, every truth she had uncovered—he had laid them out for her. The notebook was not a secret she had kept from him. It was a message he had allowed her to find.

Step seven. Her rebellion was his design.

She looked at the photograph of her sister. The teardrop track. She had known the cost, and she had paid it anyway.

"Come on," she said. "Let us go play my game."

She did not wait for his answer. She stepped over the operative and walked toward the vault exit, the journal tucked against her ribs.

Lucian followed. He always followed.
