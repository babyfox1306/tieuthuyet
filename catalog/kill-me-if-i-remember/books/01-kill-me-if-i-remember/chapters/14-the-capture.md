Lucian Vey had walked into worse traps, but never with the woman he was supposed to kill pressed against his back.

Her breath came in short, controlled bursts against his spine. He felt the tension in her shoulders through the thin layer of their jackets. The warehouse stretched ahead in a grid of pale concrete, every ten feet painted with a white numbered square. Rook had turned the whole building into a game board. Lucian counted the digits as they passed. Sixteen. Seventeen. Eighteen. The numbers climbed toward the far wall where a second-level office overlooked the floor.

“This is wrong,” Sloane whispered.

She was right. The place felt hollow. Echoes bounced off the corrugated ceiling in patterns that didn't match their footsteps. Lucian had spent twenty years reading rooms like this one, and every instinct told him they were already inside the trap.

He stopped near column twenty-three. The grid line beneath his boot read K-9. Military warehouse notation. Someone had stenciled the coordinates with care, the way a planner marks a staging area before an operation.

“Rook knows we're coming,” he said.

“He was always going to know.” Sloane moved past him, her silhouette sharp against the dim emergency lights. She held the SIG he'd given her at the safe house, muzzle low but ready. “That doesn't change what we need. Mara is here.”

Lucian didn't argue. He had seen the sister's hand rhythm through the scope. He had watched Sloane's face when she recognized the firing sequence in those fingers. The truth had cracked something open inside her, and now she moved like a woman who had stopped doubting her own broken memory.

They advanced through the grid. The warehouse smelled of rust and hydraulic fluid and something chemical he couldn't place. Overhead, the lights hummed at a frequency that set his teeth on edge.

Thirty-two.

Thirty-three.

The office door at the far end stood open. A single bulb burned inside, throwing a rectangle of yellow light across the painted floor. Lucian signaled for Sloane to hold. He scanned the catwalk above, the shadows between the storage racks, the gap beneath the roll-up doors.

Nothing moved.

He had almost convinced himself they had guessed wrong when the first shot came.

It wasn't aimed at them. The round punched through a window panel high in the far wall, and the sound cracked like a whip across the open space. Lucian dropped low, pulling Sloane behind a steel support beam. He counted the seconds until the follow-up.

Six seconds.

Seven.

The second shot came from a different angle, lower, clipping the corner of the beam. Metal fragments stung his cheek.

“They're herding us,” Sloane said.

She was right again. The shots were too wide. Whoever was pulling the trigger didn't want to hit them. Not yet.

“Stay low. Move toward the office.”

They crawled through the grid, using the painted numbers as cover markers. K-11. K-12. The concrete scraped against Lucian's elbow, and he felt the old ache in his right shoulder from a wound he'd taken in a different war, a different contract. He pushed it down.

They reached the base of the office stairs. The metal grate trembled as footsteps sounded above. Heavy boots, at least three men. And something else—a lighter tread, someone resisting.

Sloane heard it too. Her hand found his forearm, fingers digging in.

“That's her.”

Lucian didn't tell her to stay. He didn't have to. She was already moving up the stairs, two at a time, the SIG trained on the open doorway.

He followed, three steps behind, rifle braced.

They cleared the landing together. The office was a glass box with a desk, a filing cabinet, and a man in a black tactical vest standing with his back to them. Beyond him, in the corner, Mara Mercer knelt with her hands zip-tied behind her.

Sloane's sister. Alive. Bruised. Alive.

The man turned, and Lucian saw the earpiece, the coiled wire, the microphone clipped to his collar. He was a relay. A voice box.

“Good,” the voice came through the man's speaker, crackling with static. “I wanted you to see this part, Vey.”

Cassian Rook.

Lucian's finger moved to the trigger, but the man in the vest reached down and hauled Mara to her feet. He pressed a pistol against her temple.

“Don't,” Sloane said. Her voice was low, stripped of everything but command. “You don't have to do this.”

The man didn't answer. He wasn't the one making decisions.

Rook's voice filled the room again. “I've been watching the board all night, Sloane. You've played well. The broadcast was clever. The ledger fragment—I'll admit, I didn't expect you to find that piece so quickly. But you forgot the eighth contract.”

Lucian shifted his aim past the man in the vest, scanning for the real threat. Sniper positions. If Rook was talking, he was buying time for a shooter to get into place.

“The eighth contract doesn't exist,” Sloane said.

“You want to bet your sister's life on that?”

The man in the vest jerked Mara forward. She stumbled, but her eyes found Sloane, and she gave a small shake of her head. Don't.

Lucian understood. If Sloane reacted, if she gave Rook the opening, they would lose the sister and both of them.

The warehouse lights went out.

Not all of them. The emergency strips stayed on, casting everything in amber gloom. But the office bulb died, and the glass box turned into a reflection gallery—nothing but shadows and the pale outlines of their own faces.

Lucian moved before the light died completely. He hooked Sloane around the waist and pulled her back toward the stairs. She fought him for half a second, then understood. The office was a kill box. They couldn't stay.

They hit the floor of the main warehouse as the first automatic burst raked the office above them. Glass shattered. Drywall dust rained down like snow.

Lucian pushed Sloane behind a row of metal shelving units. “You stay here.”

“The hell I will.”

“You stay here because if you go back up there, he'll use you to bait Mara into doing something stupid. You know I'm right.”

He saw the war in her eyes—the woman who had ordered her own death, who had erased her memory to protect this moment, versus the sister who had just seen her only family alive after believing her dead for hours.

The sister won.

He could see the decision in the sudden set of her jaw, the way her fingers curled around the SIG. She wouldn't stay hidden. But she would wait for the right moment.

That was all he could ask.

He moved through the grid, using the numbers to navigate. K-16. The gunfire had stopped. The silence that followed was worse.

A door slid open at the far end of the warehouse. Hydraulic screech. Footsteps. More than one person, but measured, unhurried.

Lucian pressed himself against a concrete pillar and watched.

Four men emerged from the loading bay. They fanned out in a standard sweep pattern, covering each other's angles. Professional. Rook had hired serious muscle.

Behind them, Mara walked with her hands still bound, flanked by the man in the vest. Her face was calm, but Lucian saw her hands moving in small circles at her sides. The hand rhythm. The mirror key. She was giving them something, even now.

He didn't have time to read it.

The sweep team was closing in on his position. He had maybe thirty seconds before they pinned him.

Lucian made a choice.

He broke cover, firing three rounds toward the loading bay to draw their attention. The shots went wide—he meant them to. He wanted the team to come to him, not find Sloane.

The plan worked.

The four men pivoted and opened fire. Lucian dove behind a stack of wooden crates as rounds chewed through the pine. Something sharp sliced across his right side, and the world went hot with pain.

He ignored it. He had been hit before.

But the blood was wet and fast, and when he tried to lift his rifle, his fingers didn't respond the way they should.

Sloane appeared beside him.

He hadn't heard her move.

“You're hit,” she said.

“I noticed.”

She pulled a knife from her boot and cut away the fabric of his jacket. The wound was a gash along his lower ribs, bleeding steadily but not arterial. He had maybe ten minutes before the blood loss made him sloppy.

Sloane pressed a folded bandage against the wound. Lucian hissed through his teeth.

“Hold still.”

“They're moving Mara out.”

“I know.” Her voice was tight. “I can't help her if I'm carrying you.”

She tied off the bandage with quick, efficient movements. Her hands were steady. He watched her face in the dim light—the concentration, the anger, the fear she refused to show. She was beautiful in the way a drawn blade was beautiful. She was going to break his heart or save it.

Their eyes met.

For a moment, the warehouse, the gunfire, the ticking clock none of it existed. There was only the weight of her palm against his ribs and the knowledge that he had already chosen, hours ago, when he first read the binding condition.

“You should have left me up there,” he said.

“I don't leave people behind.”

“You wrote a contract that says otherwise.”

She flinched. He saw the hit land, and he regretted the words the second they left his mouth.

“That's not fair,” she whispered.

“No. It's not. I'm sorry.”

She finished the bandage and sat back on her heels. Above them, the loading bay door screeched open again. The engine of a vehicle rumbled to life.

Mara was being taken.

Sloane stood. Her jaw was set, her eyes hard. “We're getting her back.”

Lucian pushed himself to his feet. The wound tugged, but the bleeding had slowed. He could move. He could fight.

The warehouse lights flickered back to full power, and the speaker system crackled to life.

Rook's voice filled the space, calm and amused, the tone of a man who had already won.

“I'm not going to insult you by pretending I didn't see that coming, Vey. You're predictable. That's your flaw. You always choose the noble wound over the clean shot.”

Lucian scanned the ceiling for cameras. There had to be at least one.

“I've got your sister, Sloane. She's comfortable. She'll stay that way as long as you cooperate.” A pause. “But we both know you won't cooperate. You never could.”

The vehicle engine revved outside.

“You have until sunrise, Vey. Choose the woman or choose the contract. Either way, you lose.”

The speaker cut out.

Lucian stood in the numbered grid, bleeding into a bandage Sloane had tied with her own hands, and felt the weight of every second between now and dawn.

She was watching him. Waiting.

He had nothing to give her but the truth.

“I'm not choosing the contract,” he said.

Sloane's breath caught. She didn't speak.

The loading bay door was closing. The rumble of the truck faded into the night. Mara was gone, and the clock was ticking, and Lucian Vey had just told the woman he was bound to kill that he had already broken the one rule he had never broken before.

He had chosen her.

Now he had to decide what that meant before the sun came up.
