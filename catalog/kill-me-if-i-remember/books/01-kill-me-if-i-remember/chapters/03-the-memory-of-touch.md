The lock was digital, but her thumb moved across the keypad before her brain caught up—a code she didn't know she remembered.

The door clicked open.

Sloane stood in the hallway of her own apartment, hand still extended, staring at the keypad. Ten digits. She hadn't hesitated. She hadn't thought. Her thumb had moved while her mind sat stunned in the backseat of her own skull.

"You knew the code," Lucian said from behind her.

She didn't turn. "My apartment."

"You couldn't remember the last three months ten minutes ago. The code is a pretty recent change."

She hated that he was right. She pushed the door open and stepped inside.

The scent hit her first. Coffee. Stale, sitting too long in a pot she didn't remember brewing. She crossed the living room in three strides and found the carafe on the counter, half-full, a ghostly skin of oil floating on the surface. She touched it. Cold.

When? She tried to force the image. Her hand pouring the coffee. Her lips blowing across the steam. Nothing. Just the same static she'd found every time she reached for the missing months.

"Someone's been here," she said.

"You've been here."

She shook her head. "I don't remember being here."

Lucian moved past her, scanning the space with a predator's economy. He didn't touch anything. He didn't need to. His eyes catalogued every deviation from a baseline he'd clearly memorized.

She hated that too. How long had he been watching her? How deep did the surveillance go?

Focus.

She started with the kitchen. The counters were clean, but not sterile. A single mug in the sink, the coffee ring dried into a brown halo. She opened the refrigerator. Containers of takeout with dates from the past week. All within the gap. All food she didn't remember ordering, let alone eating.

A faint scratch mark caught her eye on the doorframe to her home office. Low. At wrist height. As if someone had stood exactly there and measured against the wood. She traced it with her fingertip. The groove was fresh. The paint hadn't had time to yellow.

She had an absurd thought: I left this for myself.

"Sloane." Lucian's voice was low. He stood by the window, looking down at the street.

"What?"

"Nothing yet. Stay calm."

"I am calm."

"You're doing it."

She looked down. Her thumb was pressing into the inside of her left wrist, tracing a tight, deliberate circle. Over and over. The skin was starting to redden.

She stopped. Forced her hand still.

But the ghost of the motion lingered. A cadence. A rhythm she couldn't shake.

She looked at her wrist. The skin was unmarked except for the faint pink irritation. No scar. Nothing to suggest why the motion felt so specific, so practiced.

It's not a tic, she realized. It's training.

The thought struck her like a physical blow. She had assumed it was a nervous habit, born from the shock of the rooftop. But the precision of the movement, the uniform pressure, the deliberate rotation—it had the feel of a muscle memory drilled by repetition. A reflex she'd coded into her own body.

Why? What did I train myself to do?

She turned her wrist over, examining it as if the answers might be written in the veins beneath her skin.

She pressed her thumb into the same spot again. Deliberately this time. She closed her eyes and let her hand move. One rotation. Two. The motion was smooth, practiced. She could feel the rhythm in her bones.

She opened her eyes. "My memory isn't just gone."

Lucian waited.

"Something took it. Someone. This isn't trauma amnesia. This is induced. I wasn't in an accident. I wasn't attacked. I was wiped."

Lucian's expression didn't shift, but something in his posture changed. A tension that hadn't been there a moment before.

He knew something. She could feel it.

"You're going to tell me I'm paranoid."

"I'm going to tell you you're probably right."

That stopped her. She had expected deflection. Evasion. Instead, he stood there, still as a statue, and confirmed her worst suspicion with four words.

She looked away before he could read the fear in her eyes.

The rest of the apartment told the same story. A laptop warm to the touch on the coffee table—she opened it, found a browser history from two days ago, searches she didn't recognize. A pair of shoes kicked off by the sofa, the soles worn in a pattern that didn't match her gait.

Someone had lived here while she was asleep. That someone was her.

She went to her bedroom. The bed was made, but not perfectly. A single pillow dented, the sheets tangled at the foot. She'd slept here. Eaten here. Existed here for three months, and she had no record of it.

I must have hidden something.

The thought was pure instinct. A survival impulse from a version of herself she couldn't reach. If she knew she was going to lose her memory, she would have buried a message. A breadcrumb.

She started searching. Drawers. Closets. The false bottom of a jewelry box. Nothing.

She was about to give up when her fingers brushed the underside of the nightstand drawer. A piece of tape. She pulled it open, reached underneath—

"Don't." Lucian's hand closed around her arm.

The contact was electric.

For a single, disorienting second, she wasn't in the apartment. She was somewhere dark. A room with no windows. His palm against her skin, warm, grounding, as her own hands shook with a fear she couldn't name. She could hear her own breathing, ragged and fast. And his voice, low and steady, telling her something she couldn't quite hear.

The flash was gone before she could hold on to it.

She looked down at his hand. His fingers were wrapped around her forearm, just below the elbow. The pressure was firm but not harsh. Deliberate.

She looked up at him. His face was unreadable, but his eyes—

His eyes were the same as they'd been in the vision. Dark. Intense. Focused entirely on her.

"What's in the drawer?" she asked.

"A booby trap," he said.

She blinked. "What?"

"You rigged it. If the drawer opens more than an inch, the bottom drops out. Whatever's in there gets lost."

She pulled her arm free and crouched down. She looked at the drawer. She saw the thin wire now, almost invisible, running from the side rail to the back panel.

She'd done this to protect something from someone.

Including herself.

She worked the wire loose with a careful precision that felt like muscle memory. The drawer slid open without incident.

Inside, nestled in a hollow she'd carved into the wood, was a slip of paper.

Her handwriting.

A string of numbers, arranged in a sequence that didn't match any date or code she recognized. Fourteen digits. A fragment. An address? An account number?

She picked it up. Her fingers trembled.

C002.

She wrote that. She knew she wrote that. The pressure of the pen, the slant of the letters, the way she'd crossed the zero with a small line—it was hers.

But she had no idea what it meant.

"What is it?" Lucian asked.

"A piece of something I hid from myself." She folded the paper and slipped it into her pocket. "A key I don't know how to use yet."

She stood. Her mind was racing, piecing together fragments that refused to connect. A contract in her own hand. A sister dead on a rooftop. An erased memory. A coded message. A trained reflex in her wrist.

What did I do, Sloane?

She walked to the window, needing air, needing space from the suffocating weight of her own ghost.

The street below was empty. Dark. The city was sleeping through the worst night of her life.

But not everyone was asleep.

A figure stood across the street, half-hidden in the shadow of a tree. Still. Watching.

She knew that posture. She knew that stillness.

Cassian Rook.

Her blood went cold.

He didn't move. He didn't run. He stood exactly where he was, like he wanted her to see him.

And then the streetlight caught the edge of something in his hand.

A rifle.

And the glint of the scope confirmed what she already knew in her bones.

He wasn't here to talk.
