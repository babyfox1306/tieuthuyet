He had watched her breathe often in the old life. Now every breath Sloane Mercer took reminded Lucian he was the one who had to end it.

She lay motionless on the narrow cot, her eyes closed, one hand curled near her throat. A shallow cut marked her temple from their escape, but her breathing remained steady. Lucian had counted each rise of her chest until the numbers became another form of punishment.

His rifle rested across his knees.

The contract remained inside his coat.

CLIENT TERMINATION AT SUNRISE.

Five words in Sloane's handwriting. Five words that had followed him through gunfire, locked doors, and three months of watching her become a stranger behind familiar eyes.

He should have been checking exits.

Instead, he reached toward her.

His fingertips stopped beside her cheek. One inch separated them, perhaps less. Distance had always been easier when measured through a scope.

Sloane shifted.

Lucian froze, but her eyes stayed closed. His knuckles brushed the loose strand of hair near her cheek, moving it away from the cut at her temple. The touch was light enough to deny and intimate enough to hurt.

Her face turned faintly toward his hand.

"Lucian," she murmured.

Not Vey.

Not assassin.

Lucian.

She said it with the old familiarity, as if his name belonged in her mouth. His chest tightened before discipline closed over the reaction.

He withdrew his hand.

Sloane's left thumb moved against the inside of her wrist. One small circle, traced without thought.

Lucian watched it complete.

Then he looked away.

The old print shop they had entered after the ambush had been abandoned long enough for dust to soften the machinery. Metal letters filled shallow drawers along one wall, arranged in reverse alphabets. An obsolete press stood near the rear exit, its rollers stained with ink that had dried years ago.

Temporary shelter. Two ways out. No exposed windows.

It should have been enough for nine minutes.

At minute eight, something blinked beneath Sloane's coat.

Lucian set the rifle aside and crossed to her without sound. A red point flashed once near the torn hem, so faint it would have vanished beneath any stronger light.

Beacon.

He slid a knife under the seam and lifted the device free. It was smaller than his thumbnail, sealed in dull gray resin. No maker's mark. No exposed switch.

Rook preferred tools that looked anonymous.

Lucian held it between two fingers and listened. Beyond the print shop walls, the city produced its usual mechanical hum, but a second rhythm threaded through it.

A vehicle slowing outside.

Not stopping.

Mapping the signal.

Lucian crushed the beacon beneath the flat of his knife. The resin split with a muted crack. Inside, a filament glowed and died.

Too late. The signal had already transmitted.

Behind him, Sloane spoke. "If that was the building's security system, your technique lacks finesse."

Her eyes were open now.

Lucian turned. "You were out."

"Temporarily unavailable." She pushed herself upright and touched the cut at her temple. "There is a difference."

"Not to a bullet."

"Does every conversation with you end in ballistics?"

"Most begin there."

Her gaze dropped to the broken device beneath his knife. The last trace of disorientation left her expression.

"What is it?"

"A tracker."

"On me?"

"On your coat."

"That distinction isn't comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Sloane stood too quickly. Her hand caught the cot frame, but she recovered before he could move toward her. The look she gave him warned that any offer of help would be treated as an insult.

Good. Anger kept her sharp.

"When was it planted?" she asked.

"During the ambush. Or before."

"That narrows it down to every useful possibility."

Lucian picked up his rifle. His fingers settled around the focus ring and turned it exactly one quarter rotation away from clarity.

Sloane noticed.

She noticed everything when fear was trying to make her careless.

"There," she said.

His hand stopped.

"You do that before you refuse to answer."

"I haven't refused anything."

"Then answer this. Who could track that beacon?"

"Rook."

The name changed her breathing.

Not recognition. Not quite. But her body knew the danger before her mind could place it.

"Cassian Rook," she said. "The man whose name was hidden in my apartment."

Lucian checked the rifle chamber.

"What did he do to me?"

The question carried no plea. It came edged like a tool meant to pry open bone.

"I don't know all of it."

"But you know some."

"Yes."

"And you enjoy making me extract each fact individually?"

"No."

"Could have fooled me."

He met her eyes. "You asked who could track the beacon. I answered."

"With one name."

"It was the necessary answer."

Sloane stepped closer. "Necessary for whom?"

The print shop seemed to contract around them. She stood within reach, chin lifted, waiting for him to lie badly enough that she could punish him with the truth.

Three months ago, she had looked at him the same way across a steel table.

The room had been underground, windowless, and cold enough to keep fingerprints from lingering clearly on polished surfaces. Sloane had arrived carrying a black folder under one arm.

No greeting. No hesitation.

She placed the contract between them and turned it toward him.

CLIENT TERMINATION AT SUNRISE.

Lucian had read the line twice.

Then he had looked at her. "Whose sunrise?"

"Mine."

Her voice had been calm. Worse than calm. Decided.

He did not touch the paper. "No."

One corner of her mouth moved, humor without warmth. "That sounded almost emotional."

"It was a refusal."

"You don't refuse contracts because you dislike the target."

"I refuse badly constructed contracts."

"It is precise."

"It names you."

"Your literacy remains impressive."

He had wanted to tear the document in half. Instead, he read every clause she permitted him to see, searching for a defect that would make acceptance impossible.

The handwriting was hers. The paper stock came from her office. Her signature carried the small downward hook she used when signing under pressure.

"Tell me why," he had said.

"No."

"Sloane."

That had reached her. Her fingers tightened once on the folder.

Then she pushed a pen across the table.

"If you need affection to make you disobey, then whatever existed between us was less useful than expected."

Cruel, calculated, and aimed with perfect knowledge.

Lucian had picked up the pen.

She watched him sign.

Only after the ink touched paper did something in her expression fracture. It vanished so quickly another man might have missed it.

Lucian had not.

Back in the print shop, Sloane studied him. "You disappeared."

"Memory."

"One of mine?"

"One of mine."

Her sarcasm thinned. "The contract signing."

He said nothing.

"Did I look afraid?"

"No."

It was the truth, though not the whole shape of it.

"Did I explain why I wanted myself dead?"

"No."

Another truth sharpened down to what he could safely give her.

Her left thumb found her wrist. It began the circle, paused halfway, then completed it.

Lucian forced his attention to the rear door.

A scrape sounded in the alley.

Sloane heard it too. "Friend of yours?"

"I don't have friends."

"Healthy. Saves on birthday obligations."

He moved to the rear wall and checked the narrow gap beside the door. The alley appeared empty. A metal service box had been bolted beneath a broken vent, its paint blistered, its lock recently replaced.

The beacon had not only been calling hunters.

It had been guiding them somewhere.

Lucian opened the service box with the tip of his knife. Inside sat a cheap radio unit wired to a receiver. A green indicator pulsed in the same rhythm the beacon had used.

Sloane came up beside him. "A relay."

"A summons."

"For us?"

"For whoever carried the tracker."

A burst of static cracked from the receiver. Then a sequence of six tones played, followed by silence.

Lucian recognized the cadence.

A dead-drop authentication code.

Rook had used a similar structure years ago: signal, route, confirmation. Efficient and impersonal. The method let every participant know only the next movement.

Sloane tilted her head. "You know that sound."

"Yes."

"Rook?"

Lucian turned the rifle's focus ring back into alignment. "Stay here."

Her eyebrows rose. "Try again."

"The signal points to a dead drop. It may be watched."

"Then sending the armed man alone is what Rook expects."

"He also expects you to argue."

"Finally, something he and I agree on."

Lucian considered restraining her. He discarded the idea because she would fight, make noise, and probably weaponize one of the reversed metal letters in the printer's drawers.

He handed her the compact pistol he had taken during the ambush.

She checked the chamber automatically.

Neither of them commented on how quickly her hands remembered.

The receiver's six tones corresponded to press drawers along the wall. Lucian moved to the type cases and counted rows. Sixth cabinet, fourth drawer, second compartment.

Empty.

Sloane leaned close, examining the dust. "Not empty. Recently disturbed."

A clean rectangle marked the compartment's base.

She ran one finger along the wooden divider, then pressed it. The divider shifted. A brass catch released beneath the drawer.

Something dropped inside the cabinet.

Lucian caught it before it struck the floor.

A flat key card rested in his palm, matte white, with no logo or printed number. Its magnetic strip had been scored with three diagonal cuts.

C009.

Not a label. A placement code.

Sloane's gaze fixed on it. "That's what the beacon brought us to."

"Yes."

"So Rook planted a tracker, attacked us, then left a key card where we could find it."

"Likely."

"That is excessively organized. I already dislike him."

Lucian inspected the card's edges. No explosive compound. No contact poison. The strip had been altered, but not disabled.

The dead drop was intentional.

Rook wanted the card found.

Sloane took a reversed letter block from the drawer and held its polished side toward the magnetic strip. Fine iron dust clung to the block, drawn from the cabinet's old machinery.

"Give me the card," she said.

"No."

"Compelling argument. Give it to me."

"It could carry a secondary beacon."

"Then continuing to hold it seems bold."

He placed it on the cabinet.

Sloane sprinkled the iron dust across the strip. The particles shifted into narrow bands over the encoded sections, exposing interruptions made by deliberate magnetic burns.

Her focus became absolute. She read with her hands first, turning the card beneath the weak overhead lamp, tracing the cuts without touching the strip itself.

"These aren't damage," she said. "They're separators."

Lucian watched the alley through the door gap. "Between what?"

"Characters, maybe. The strip was rewritten in blocks."

She selected a thin metal spacing bar from the type drawer and dragged it beside the card. More dust aligned, revealing a short sequence.

Her thumb circled her wrist again.

This time, the motion matched the turn she used to rotate the card.

Lucian felt the pieces move toward something he could not stop.

Sloane arranged reversed letters from the printer's case, translating each magnetic block into a character. Her mouth tightened as the words formed.

"Rook expected me to solve this," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Lucian looked at her.

The question had too many answers, and every one of them could become a weapon in the wrong hands.

A sharp electronic chirp came from inside the broken beacon.

Not dead.

Lucian seized Sloane's arm and pulled her away from the cabinet as the receiver flashed red. Somewhere beyond the alley, an engine surged.

Their location had just been confirmed.

Sloane snatched the key card from the cabinet. "Then let's not waste his invitation."

She held the magnetic strip beneath the lamp. The iron dust had resolved into a location, clean and unmistakable.

The Federal Records Annex.

Sloane Mercer's old office.
