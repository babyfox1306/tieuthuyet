The crosshairs settled on a window three blocks away, but the hand that moved behind the glass was not the hand of a captive.

Sloane Mercer's eye stayed locked to the scope, her breath held in her chest as the rhythm began. Tap. Pause. Circle. Tap. Pause. Circle. She had seen that sequence before. She had written it into the margins of a journal she didn't remember keeping, a failsafe for a contingency she had never explained to anyone. The hand behind the glass ran through it again, unhurried, deliberate. It was the rhythm of a woman who was not being watched. It was the rhythm of a woman who had expected this call.

The window was smudged at the bottom, as if someone had pressed their palm against the glass to leave a message that condensation would erase. Mara had left her rhythm there, a ghost in the machine that was very much alive.

"Mara," Sloane whispered, the word pulling something loose in her chest.

Lucian stood beside her, his profile half-lit by the streetlamp below. He didn't look through the scope. He didn't need to. His hands rested on the rifle's stock, steadying the barrel, but his attention was fixed on her face.

"You knew," she said, her voice flat. "You knew she was alive."

"I knew what you wrote," he replied, his voice low, careful. "I didn't know if it would hold."

Sloane pulled back from the scope, the image of the hand burned into her retina. She pressed the heels of her palms against her eyes, trying to force the pieces to align. Her sister was alive. The shooting on the rooftop had been staged. She had staged it. The journal entry in the safety-deposit box had laid out every detail of the plan, but seeing the proof in motion was different. It was real.

"She's sending the signal," Sloane said. "The one I taught her. The failsafe."

Lucian nodded. "She's been waiting for you to find the rifle. Every night since the shooting, she's been at that window at this hour. I told her you would come."

"You told her." Sloane dropped her hands and stared at him. "You've been in contact with her the whole time."

"Not directly. I left instructions in the dead drop the night before the shooting. She knew to look for the scope. She knew to send the rhythm when she saw the crosshairs hit the glass."

Sloane turned back to the scope. The hand was still moving, fingers curling in a pattern that her own hands knew by muscle memory. Fifteen sequences. The base of a key. Mara was telling her the ledger was real. Mara was telling her where to look.

"Pull the contract," Sloane said.

Lucian reached into his coat and handed her the folded paper. She spread it across her knee, the worn creases catching the dim light from the street. The words stared back at her. CLIENT TERMINATION AT SUNRISE. Her own handwriting, steady and deliberate.

"I wrote this to fool him," she said, tracing the letters. "I needed Rook to think I was clean, that I had accepted the programming. The termination was the surface layer. Misdirection."

She looked at the paper, at the space below the signature. There had been another page. She could feel it in the gaps of her memory, a second sheet that contained the real terms. She couldn't remember the words, but she remembered the intention. It was a lock. A switch. Something that would only break if Rook pushed too hard.

"The real trap wasn't the sunrise," she said slowly, the realization crystallizing. "It was what the sunrise triggered. A condition. A choice. I forced you to choose between two versions of me."

Lucian's jaw tightened. He didn't confirm it. He didn't have to. The silence was confirmation enough.

Sloane pulled out the photograph they had taken from the dead drop. Seven faces. Seven men. She matched them to the corrupted file names in her memory, the ones she had read on the backup drive in the Federal Records Annex.

"These aren't random targets," she said. "These are witnesses. Rook used the contract system to eliminate them. He altered the target files so you would pull the trigger, and I signed off on every single one because the files looked legitimate. I built the machine he used to bury his secrets."

She ran her finger over the faces in the photograph. "Hoffman. Dray. Castellano. They knew about the weapons network. They were the only ones who could place Rook at the trafficking sites. And now they're dead, and Rook is still breathing."

"Seventeen contracts total," Lucian said. "Ten were genuine targets. Seven were altered. You didn't know about the corruption until you read the backup drive."

"But I knew something was wrong." Sloane looked up at him. "That's why I wrote the condition. That's why I gave Mara the signal. I knew Rook would eventually use the system against itself, so I built a failsafe." She tapped the photograph. "And I left a trail of breadcrumbs leading back to him."

A gust of wind swept across the rooftop, carrying the smell of rain and exhaust. Sloane shivered, though she wasn't sure if it was the cold or the weight of her own past pressing down on her.

She turned back to the scope. The hand was still there, still moving through the sequence. Fifteen repetitions, each one identical to the last. Mara was patient. She had been waiting for this moment for weeks.

Sloane pulled out her phone and pressed the record button, capturing the rhythm through the scope's lens. The audio was useless from this distance, but the visual pattern was unmistakable. Tap. Pause. Circle. She would send it to the number that her fingers knew by heart, the one she had coded into her phone during the missing months under a name she didn't recognize.

She lowered the phone and looked at the recording. Fifteen sequences of the same pattern. A fragment of the full key. Mara couldn't send the entire ledger without the trigger, but she could send the piece that told Sloane the ledger existed.

She pulled up a contact with no name attached, just a string of numbers. Her thumbs moved in a pattern she didn't consciously recall, composing the message. She attached the recording and hit send.

Then she turned to Lucian.

His eyes were on her, dark and steady. The exhaustion was visible in the lines around his mouth, the shadow beneath his cheekbones. He had been carrying this alone for longer than she could remember. He had watched her wake up on the rooftop, had guided her through the evidence, had let her discover the truth without forcing it on her.

The relief in his gaze was overwhelming. It was the look of a man who had been holding his breath for months and was finally allowed to exhale. She felt the same release spreading through her own chest, a warmth that had nothing to do with the cold air around them.

For a long moment, the rest of the city fell away. No contracts. No conditioning. Just the two of them on the rooftop, tethered by a conspiracy she had written and he had guarded.

She wanted to touch him. The urge was a physical pull, a gravity she had to resist. Instead, she let her eyes say what her hands couldn't. Thank you. I'm sorry. I'm still here.

He held her gaze, and the space between them hummed with a tension that was sharper than any weapon.

"The rhythm," she said, her voice rough. "It's a fragment of the key. Mara is telling me the ledger is intact. She's telling me Rook hasn't found it yet."

"Where is it?"

"I don't know. But she sent the base sequence. That's how I find the rest."

Sloane looked back at the scope. The hand was gone. The window was dark. The signal was complete.

She picked up the contract and the photograph, folding them back into her coat. Then she stood, her knees aching from the cold concrete.

"She sent the rhythm," Sloane said. "Fifteen sequences. That's the base of the key. I have to match it to the marks on the ledger."

Lucian rose beside her, the rifle hanging loose in his grip. "The ledger isn't here. It's in three pieces, scattered across the city. You hid them yourself."

"Then I have to find them before Rook realizes Mara sent the signal." Sloane pulled out her phone and checked the sent message. The recording had been delivered. The number at the other end would decrypt the pattern and send back the location of the first fragment.

She slipped the phone into her pocket and met Lucian's eyes.

"It's not sunrise yet," she said. "She's running out of time."
