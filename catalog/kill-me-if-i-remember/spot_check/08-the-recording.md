The voice on the recording was Cassian Rook's, relaxed, as if he were ordering coffee instead of ordering a man's death.

Sloane froze with one hand still inside the open steel box.

The recorder lay beneath the blackened metal, small enough to disappear beneath her palm. Its casing was silver, its edges worn smooth, and a single green light blinked against the vault's red emergency glow.

Beyond the door, the lock completed another cycle.

Lucian's rifle shifted toward the entrance. "Take it."

"You recognize him?"

"I recognize the voice."

That was not an answer. Lucian collected those the way other people collected ammunition, keeping them close and offering them only when the alternative was worse.

Sloane snatched up the recorder and the ledger fragment. "Then we agree this night needs better entertainment."

The outer door shuddered under an impact.

"Move," Lucian said.

She shoved the fragment into the inner pocket of her coat and ran beside him as he crossed the vault. The floor lights strobed red beneath their feet. A second impact struck the door, followed by the metallic scream of something being forced through the locking mechanism.

Lucian reached the service exit first. He slammed his shoulder against it, but the panel held.

"Locked?"

"Magnetic seal."

"Of course it is." Sloane looked at the keypad. Six black buttons. No labels. No visible power line. "This place has an unhealthy relationship with dramatic timing."

"Can you open it?"

"Ask me a more insulting question."

Her left thumb began rubbing across the inside of her right wrist.

Back and forth. A small, steady motion. The pressure of bone beneath skin gave her something measurable while the vault filled with the low mechanical grind of the failing door.

The keypad had no numbers, only shallow ridges. Not a code panel. A sequence panel.

Sloane dragged her fingertips over the surface. One ridge had a burr. Another was polished smooth. Repeated use. A sequence known by touch, not sight.

The answer moved through her hand before it reached her thoughts.

She pressed the third ridge, then the first, then held the fifth until the panel flashed white.

The service door released.

Lucian caught her wrist before she stepped through.

His hand closed over her right arm, careful to avoid the place her thumb still worried. "You knew that sequence."

"My hands are developing a more reliable memory than the rest of me."

A sharp crack split the vault. The outer door buckled inward.

Lucian released her. "Run."

They plunged into the narrow service corridor as bullets tore through the steel behind them. The first struck the wall near Sloane's shoulder, showering the passage with bright chips.

Lucian fired once over his shoulder. The answering silence lasted only long enough for them to reach the stairwell.

Sloane descended two flights, boots slipping on dust and old paint. Lucian stayed behind her, one hand occasionally catching the railing, his rifle angled toward the upper landing. He moved with the same controlled precision he had carried through every trap so far.

Then his step hitched.

Sloane turned. "You're bleeding."

"Keep moving."

"That was not a denial."

"Keep moving, Sloane."

The use of her name hit with an odd, intimate force. She hated that it worked.

At the bottom of the stairwell, she shoved open a fire door. The corridor beyond was dark except for a row of dead surveillance monitors. A side exit stood propped open with a wedge of folded cardboard.

Sloane stopped.

Lucian nearly ran into her. "What?"

"That door."

"Open."

"Convenient."

"Rook anticipated the pursuit."

"Rook anticipates everything, apparently. Seven altered files, seven eliminated witnesses, a vault with a hidden signal, and now a conveniently wedged exit." She looked at the cardboard. No dust on its upper fold. Fresh. "Either he wants us outside, or someone else wants us alive."

Lucian's gaze moved over the corridor. "Those are not mutually exclusive."

Another shot cracked through the stairwell.

Sloane pushed through the exit.

They emerged into an alley behind the annex, where darkness swallowed the building's security lights. Lucian grabbed her elbow and pulled her beneath a concrete overhang as two figures crossed the far mouth of the alley.

No uniforms. No shouted commands. Professional enough to be worse.

Sloane held the recorder against her ribs. The green light continued blinking.

Lucian leaned close, listening to the movement beyond the wall. His breath brushed the side of her hair. The proximity made an old sensation flicker behind her eyes: his body between hers and a threat, his voice low against her ear, the certainty that he would choose the dangerous angle without being asked.

The memory vanished before she could catch it.

"Safehouse?" she whispered.

"Three blocks."

"How many people know it?"

"Rook."

"That is not the number a woman wants to hear."

"I know."

They waited until the figures moved past the alley. Then Lucian led her through a service lane, across an empty loading yard, and into the basement entrance of an abandoned print shop.

The safehouse smelled of cheap cigarettes and bleach, but the voice recorder was clean, its metal casing polished by a hand that knew not to leave prints behind.

Sloane noticed that before she noticed the blood soaking through Lucian's sleeve.

The room held a scarred table, two chairs, a wall map stripped of its labels, and an old electrical panel. No windows. No clocks. Nothing that could tell her how long remained until sunrise.

Lucian locked the basement door and set the rifle within reach.

"Sit," Sloane said.

"I am fine."

"Your shirt is making a persuasive argument against that."

The bullet had grazed the outside of his upper arm. Not deep, but enough to stain his sleeve and leave a narrow line of blood down to his wrist.

He watched her open a medical kit from beneath the table. "You know how to treat a graze."

"I was a federal investigator before I became a murder suspect. The job came with several unpleasant electives."

She pulled the sleeve away from the wound. His muscles tightened under her fingers, but he did not flinch.

"Hold still."

"That was my plan."

"Your plans are terrible. The evidence is extensive."

She cleaned the cut. Lucian's attention stayed on her face rather than the wound. It was unnerving, being studied by a man who could put a round through a moving target and yet seemed more interested in whether she pressed too hard with the gauze.

Sloane secured the dressing and withdrew her hand.

For one second, neither of them moved.

"You should have told me," she said.

"About the files?"

"About whatever you knew before we walked into that vault."

"I knew Rook wanted you to find the fragment."

"That is not the same as knowing why."

"No."

"Do you enjoy answering in single syllables?"

"No."

The corner of her mouth almost moved. It was an irritating response to the situation.

She turned toward the recorder. "Let us see what he left us."

The device had no brand, no buttons except a recessed switch, and no visible port. Sloane rotated it under the fluorescent light. The casing was polished in one narrow place near the seam, as if a thumb had repeatedly pressed there.

"Clean," Lucian said.

"Too clean."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning whoever handled it cared about fingerprints, but not enough to remove the wear pattern." She tapped the polished strip. "Someone wanted me to open it. The question is whether they wanted me to hear it."

She pressed the switch.

Static filled the basement.

Then Rook spoke.

"Target file three-seven-one. Witness status: eliminated. No surviving testimony."

His voice was smooth, almost bored. Sloane had heard it in briefings, in interviews, in the calm pauses before he made someone understand that refusal had become expensive.

A second voice murmured something too low to distinguish.

Rook continued. "File four-eight-nine was corrected after the transfer. File five-one-two never reached review. File six-six-zero was closed on external confirmation."

Sloane leaned closer.

The recorder clicked. Paper moved on the other end.

"Seven files," Rook said. "Seven witnesses removed. The operative will not question the pattern until the seventh is already behind her."

Lucian's expression hardened.

Sloane stopped the playback.

"That is the recording."

"There is more."

"How do you know?"

"Rook does not leave a conclusion on the first track."

She stared at him. "You know him well."

"I worked for him."

"That is not what I asked."

"It is the answer."

Sloane restarted the device.

Rook's voice returned, still relaxed. "The seventh file remains useful. The eighth is the test. If she reaches it, the sequence holds. If she breaks before then, proceed with the rooftop arrangement."

Sloane's thumb stopped against her right wrist.

The recording hissed.

"The sister must be visible. The sister must be mourned. The sister must be believed."

Sloane hit stop so hard the recorder jumped across the table.

For several seconds, the basement held only the hum of the electrical panel.

Lucian said nothing.

That was worse than an explanation. Silence left room for every answer she did not want.

"The rooftop arrangement," Sloane said.

Lucian's eyes remained on the recorder. "It could refer to anything."

"That sentence used the word sister."

"Rook knew Mara was your pressure point."

"He knew her identity. That does not explain the body."

"You saw a body."

"I saw Mara from a distance."

"Under poor light."

"Do not do that."

His gaze lifted. "Do what?"

"Make uncertainty sound like mercy."

The words came out sharper than intended. Good. Sharp was easier to manage than grief.

She replayed the final passage. The phrase came through exactly as before.

The sister must be visible. The sister must be mourned. The sister must be believed.

Sloane looked down at her own hands.

Mara's death had contained too many deliberate details. The placement on the rooftop. The contract in Sloane's handwriting. The timing at sunrise. The way the scene had forced Sloane to look before she could think.

A murder designed to be witnessed was not always a murder designed to be completed.

"Say it," Lucian said.

Her eyes narrowed. "You sound almost eager."

"I sound like a man waiting for you to stop circling the answer."

Her left thumb resumed rubbing her right wrist. The motion was small, automatic, and furious.

Sloane looked at the recorder. "Mara's death may have been staged."

The statement sat between them.

Lucian did not correct her. He did not confirm it either.

That restraint told her more than reassurance would have.

She stood so quickly the chair scraped backward. "You knew."

"I suspected."

"Since when?"

"Since the rooftop."

"And you let me believe she was dead."

"You believed what the scene required you to believe."

"That is a very elegant way to describe standing beside me while I fell apart."

His jaw tightened. "I did not let you do anything."

"No? Then what were you doing?"

"Keeping you alive."

"By withholding information?"

"By withholding information Rook expected you to chase."

Sloane stepped around the table. "You keep saying his name as if it excuses your choices."

"It explains them."

"Explanation is not absolution."

"I know."

There it was again. The quiet certainty. The infuriating lack of defense.

She hated him for making anger feel like a conversation they had already had.

The wound on his arm began bleeding through the edge of the dressing. Sloane noticed because she noticed everything when she was trying not to notice one particular man.

"Sit down," she ordered.

"I am sitting."

"You are standing."

"That is a technical distinction."

"Your favorite kind."

She took the gauze back and pressed it to the dressing. Lucian lowered himself into the chair. When she leaned in, his hand rose and caught her forearm.

Not restraining. Asking.

Sloane looked at his fingers, then at his face. He released her immediately.

"Do not," she said.

"Do not what?"

"Ask permission after the fact."

Something shifted in his eyes. Regret, perhaps. Or recognition.

She finished securing the dressing. Her hand lingered near his shoulder for one dangerous second before she pulled away.

Lucian stood.

The distance between them disappeared.

His hand came to her face, palm against her jaw, gentle enough that she could have turned away. Sloane did not. His thumb rested beneath her cheekbone, nowhere near her mouth, yet the touch drew a memory from somewhere deeper than thought.

A dark room. His voice saying her name as if it belonged somewhere safe.

"Sloane."

He said it now with the same tenderness.

The sound broke through the silence between them.

Her breath caught. "You remember saying it like that?"

"I remember saying it."

"Do you remember why?"

"Yes."

She should have stepped back. Instead, she stayed beneath his hand, furious at the warmth in her chest and the way his eyes made her feel seen by a stranger who knew too much.

"Tell me," she whispered.

"Not here."

"That is becoming your slogan."

"Not here," he repeated, softer. "Not with Rook listening."

The recorder sat on the table between them, its green light blinking patiently.

Sloane turned her face just enough that his palm slipped away. The loss of contact was immediate and unwelcome.

"Then we find out what he left inside it."

She picked up the recorder and examined the seam. The device was too light. Too hollow. The audio mechanism could fit inside, but the casing had been built around something else.

Lucian reached for a compact blade.

"I can open it."

"Of course you can."

"That sounded almost like approval."

"It was closer to professional despair."

He placed the blade on the table instead of handing it to her. "Use the edge."

Sloane slid the tip into the seam. The metal resisted, then gave with a faint snap.

Inside, the recorder contained almost nothing.

No battery compartment. No storage chip. Only a narrow cavity beneath the polished casing.

Her left thumb rubbed over her right wrist again as she studied the interior. The motion repeated twice, paused, then pressed.

The polished strip shifted.

Sloane's breath went still.

She pried open the recorder's casing and found a stripped-down SIM card taped beneath the metal, carrying a single number.

She knew it before she finished reading.

It was Mara's childhood code to the shared safety-deposit box.
