Rook’s voice echoed through the terminal speakers before they cleared the first checkpoint: “I’ve been watching you follow a script you wrote for yourself, Mercer. Let’s see how well you improvise.”

Sloane didn’t break stride. The words hit her like a slap of cold water, but she kept moving. The maintenance corridor stretched ahead, lined with humming server racks and the sharp smell of ozone. Lucian moved at her flank, rifle low, eyes tracking the shadows between the cooling units.

“He’s in the building network,” she said. “Local feed. He’s watching every camera angle.”

“He’s been watching the whole night,” Lucian replied. His voice was quiet, but it carried the weight of a man who had accepted the trap before springing it. “He let us find the fragments. He let us get this far.”

Sloane’s stomach tightened. She knew it was true. Every scrap of evidence, every dead drop, every narrow escape—Rook had measured each step and let her take it. Her rebellion was step seven of his design. She had built the trap, but he had laid the floorboards beneath it.

Doesn’t matter, she told herself. The ledger exists. The truth exists. I just have to reach it before he burns it.

She traced the circle against her left wrist. The motion was automatic now, a reflex she no longer tried to suppress. It was the first beat of a firing sequence her body remembered even if her mind could not.

The holding cell was in the sublevel, past two security doors that Sloane cracked using the rhythm fragment she had decoded from the dead-drop. The lock clicked open like a practiced handshake.

Mara was slumped against the far wall, wrists tethered to a monitoring station by thin wires that trailed to sensors on her temples and chest. Her face was pale, her eyes hollow, but when she saw Sloane, something sharp flickered behind the exhaustion.

“Took you long enough,” Mara said. Her voice was dry, cracked, but the humor was intact.

Sloane crossed the room in four strides and dropped to her knees beside her sister. She wanted to pull her into an embrace, to apologize for every second of the nightmare, but there was no time for sentiment. “I had a late start. Someone shot you.”

“I asked you to.”

The words landed like a stone in still water. Sloane had known. She had assembled the evidence. She had seen the hand rhythm through the scope. But hearing Mara say it out loud made the truth feel heavier than concrete.

“I know,” Sloane said. She pulled the dead-drop recording from her jacket. C010. The final piece. “I found the fragments. I know what I did. I know what I asked of you.”

Mara’s gaze dropped to the recording device. A faint smile touched her lips. “You really don’t remember how it works, do you?”

“I remember enough to get here. The rest has to come from you.”

Mara raised her hand. The motion was slow, deliberate, the fingers curling into the familiar pattern Sloane had seen through the rifle scope. The rhythm that mirrored her own tic. The rhythm that had been drilled into Mara’s reflex because Sloane’s own memory could not be trusted to hold it.

Sloane’s wrist burned.

She looked down. Her left hand was moving. Tracing the same circle. The same sequence. Her body was speaking a language her mind had forgotten.

The terminal in the corner of the cell flickered to life.

Lines of code scrolled across the screen. Raw. Unformatted. The preliminary ledger fragment. MR005.

Sloane rose and moved to the terminal. The data was ugly. Names she recognized. Routes she had mapped. Account numbers that fed a weapons network built on the graves of seven witnesses Rook had needed silenced.

And there, at the bottom of the fragment, a single line that made her chest go cold.

Condition: CLIENT TERMINATION AT SUNRISE.

Her own handwriting. Her own contract. Her own death order.

“It’s there,” Mara said from the floor. “The skeleton of the whole thing. He can’t hide it anymore.”

Sloane stared at the screen. The evidence was real. The guilt was real. She had ordered the deaths of those seven people. She had turned Lucian into the instrument of her design. She had staged her sister’s shooting. She had erased her own memory and written a contract that would execute herself if Rook ever restored the obedient version of her that he had trained.

She was not the victim of this story. She was the architect.

The lights in the corridor flickered. Alarms began to pulse.

“He knows we’re here,” Lucian said. He had taken position at the door, rifle aimed at the gap.

“He knew before we entered the building,” Sloane replied. She pulled the recording device from the terminal slot. The fragment was loaded. The mirror key was turning. But the lock was not fully open.

They moved toward the root server room. The alarms grew louder. The corridors filled with the sound of boots on metal grates.

Rook’s cleaners hit them at the junction.

The first burst of automatic fire chewed through the wall inches from Sloane’s head. Lucian shoved her behind a server rack and returned fire. The sound was deafening in the enclosed space. She smelled cordite and blood.

Lucian grunted.

Sloane saw the blood bloom on his sleeve. A graze. High on the shoulder. He didn’t stop firing, but she saw the way his jaw tightened.

When the corridor went quiet, she grabbed his arm and pulled him into the alcove of a cooling unit. “Let me see it.”

“It’s a graze.”

“I don’t care what it is. Let me see it.”

He let her push the torn fabric aside. The wound was shallow, bleeding freely but clean. She pressed her palm flat against it, applying pressure. His hand came up and closed over hers.

Their eyes met.

The alarms were still screaming. Rook was still watching. The clock was still running. But in that moment, there was only the weight of his hand over hers, the warmth of his blood between her fingers, and the unspoken acknowledgment that they had chosen to be here. Together.

“You’re doing exactly what you designed yourself to do,” Lucian said.

“I designed myself to watch you bleed?”

He didn’t smile, but something shifted in his eyes. “You designed yourself to end this. The bleeding is my problem.”

She held his gaze. “Don’t make me design a widow’s pension into the next draft.”

The part of his mouth that almost moved was enough.

The root server room was at the end of the corridor. The door was reinforced steel, but the lock was the same model Sloane had cracked twice before. She worked the rhythm into the keypad, and the door opened with a hydraulic hiss.

They stepped inside.

The room was vast. Server towers lined the walls, their cooling fans humming in a chorus of mechanical breath. At the center of the room, a single terminal dominated the space. Its screen was dark.

Then it flickered to life.

Red text.

NETWORK PURGE INITIATED.

5:00.

The door behind them slammed shut. The seal engaged with a vacuum thud.

Rook’s voice filled the room. Calm. Almost gentle. “I told you, Sloane. I planned for your discovery. I added a delete sequence. You can watch the ledger compile, or you can watch it burn. Which version of you wins tonight?”

Sloane slammed the dead-drop recording into the server slot.

The terminal screen changed. The Mirror Key engaged. Lines of data began to compile—the full ledger, rising from the fragments she had spent the night collecting.

But the wipe countdown did not stop.

4:45.

4:44.

Mara stepped up beside her. “It’s not accepting the key.”

“I can see that.”

“The rhythm was the key, Lo. It should be stopping.”

Sloane stared at the terminal. The keyboard was old, the letters worn off most keys. But two were completely smooth.

‘G’ and ‘W’.

Worn down as if someone had spent hours typing a single sequence over and over. A code she could not yet read.

Her own hands. Her own forgotten plan.

She looked from the keys to the countdown. 4:30.

The Mirror Key had opened the vault. But she didn’t have the password for the lock.

“I need to remember the rest,” Sloane whispered.

The wall of her memory stood between her and the truth. And the clock was running out.
