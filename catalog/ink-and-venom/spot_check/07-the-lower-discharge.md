"Do not let them call you cured," Tobias hissed, and dragged Elara behind the linen press as the stretcher wheels started down.

The press was a narrow iron tower, six feet high and crammed with sheets that smelled of bleach and cold metal. He slammed her against it hard enough that a rail dug into her spine. His hand clamped over her mouth before she could speak.

"Quiet," he breathed against her ear. "They'll take you instead."

She bit down on the urge to shove him off. Through the gap between the press and the wall, she watched the stretcher roll past. Two orderlies in white. Nurse Cora at the front, her stride a matched pair of sharp clicks.

The man on the stretcher was the same one from the treatment room door. The young man with the grey robe.

His eyes were open.

They did not blink.

"Look at his arm," Tobias whispered.

The robe had ridden up to his elbow. The skin beneath was not skin anymore. It was a grey membrane, and beneath it the black threads had multiplied into a dense, branching root system that crawled up past his wrist and disappeared under the sleeve. The threads pulsed. Not with blood. With something slower, thicker, like ink settling into the fibers of paper.

"That's what happens when they call you cured," Tobias said. "They take you to the lower rooms. The salt rooms. And you don't come back up."

The stretcher turned toward the east stairwell. The orderlies lifted the front wheels over the threshold. The door swung shut with a seal that sounded less like a door and more like a vault closing on something that would never see daylight again.

Elara tried to count the tiles on the floor. She lost track at four.

"I saw the salt in the kitchens," she said. "It burns the threads."

"It does." Tobias released her mouth but kept her pinned against the press. "But they don't give it to you straight. They grind it into tea. They dilute it. Too weak to stop anything. Just enough to keep you docile."

"How do you know this?"

"Because I've drawn it." He reached inside his robe and pulled out a folded page. The paper was soft, worn at the edges, covered in dense charcoal lines. "Finch lets me have charcoal. He thinks it's therapeutic. It is. But not for him."

He unfolded the page.

Black branching vessels filled the paper, thick as roots, crowding every inch of the white surface. Cutting through them were jagged shapes. Crystals. Salt. The crystals interrupted the vessels, breaking them into disconnected segments. The gaps were precise, almost surgical.

"This is a map," he whispered. "You have to use the salt yourself. Whole crystal. Pressed into the pattern."

He pointed to a clean space in the web. A place where the vessels stopped.

"There's a gap in yours. I saw it on your wrist just now. You have time, but not much."

Elara looked down at her own arm. The black lines had angled toward the east stairwell, toward where the stretcher had gone. They were curved, reaching, like plant stems bending toward a window.

"They pull," she said. "Toward the lower rooms."

"They want to connect," Tobias said. "They want to finish what they started. The salt is the only thing that interrupts the signal."

He pressed the drawing into her hand.

"I can't—"

The door to the east stairwell swung open.

Cora stepped out. Her gait was silent on the stone, but her shadow crossed the floor before she did. Her gaze swept the alcove and landed squarely on the gap behind the linen press.

"Mr. Rehn," she said.

She walked straight to the press and pulled it aside.

Her eyes went to the paper in Elara's hand. Her face did not change, but her hand moved fast. Her fingers closed around the edge of the drawing.

"Give it to me."

Elara held on.

Cora's grip tightened. The paper tore. A jagged strip came away in the nurse's hand. The rest—the half with the crystals and the clean gap—stayed in Elara's fist.

Cora stared at the ripped edge. Her breath quickened. She was not looking at the drawing anymore. She was looking at Elara's wrist, where the black threads had curled away from the torn paper still pressed against her palm.

"What is that supposed to mean?" Elara asked.

Cora did not answer. Her jaw tightened. Her fingers crumpled her strip of the paper into her pocket.

"Miss Sennewald."

Finch stood at the end of the corridor. He had not made a sound. He was simply there, hands clasped, a cup of tea cooling on the sill beside him.

"I see you've met Mr. Rehn. He has a tendency to frighten new patients with his art. It's a symptom of his own anxiety." He smiled. "The patient on the stretcher is being transferred to a quieter ward. His nervous system requires absolute stillness before full discharge. That is all."

"His arms—"

"Are responding beautifully to treatment. As are yours."

Elara's wrist burned. The threads were pulling toward the stairwell again. Toward the vault door.

The drawing in her hand shifted. The jagged salt shapes brushed the black lines curving over her knuckles.

The threads spasmed.

They jerked backward, curling away from the paper like a worm from a hot surface.

Finch's eyes tracked the movement.

"Fascinating," he murmured. "Sensory rejection of the unfamiliar. We will need to titrate your next dose carefully. Nurse Cora, please prepare the low-frequency solution. And escort Mr. Rehn to the observation wing. He needs rest."

Cora's hand closed around Tobias's elbow.

Tobias did not resist. He did not struggle. He looked at Elara over his shoulder. The look in his eyes was not comfort. It was not hope. It was a command.

"Use it. Use the salt."

Cora took him down the opposite corridor. His grey robe disappeared around the corner.

The orderlies were gone. The stretcher was gone.

Elara stood alone in the alcove.

She looked down the east stairwell. The vault door was closed. Locked. The light on the safety bar was red.

She looked the other way, toward the main hall, toward the dining room, toward the art studio at the far end of the long corridor.

No one was coming.

Beatrice's shimmer flickered at the far end of the hall, a curtain of iridescent dust that did nothing, offered nothing, could not hold her hand or step between her and the locked door. It was just light. Just memory. Just proof that someone else had been here and failed.

Elara folded the torn drawing and slid it under her sleeve.

The paper sat against her skin, directly over the black vessels.

It was hot.

It burned.

She walked to the locked lower stair. The red light bathed her face. She pulled the page from her sleeve and held it flat against the wood of the door.

The salt crystals on the paper glowed white against the black branching lines of the drawing.

She lifted her sleeve.

The veins climbing her arm matched the branching lines of the drawing exactly.

Not approximately. "Exactly."

And the gap—the clean space Tobias had pointed to—was the precise shape of her own wrist, where the threads had not yet reached.

It was a map of her body.

And it was a map of how to stop it.

The red light flickered.

The black threads under her skin pulsed once, hard, as if they had felt the door recognizing what she held.

Elara pressed her palm flat against the drawing.

And the paper grew warmer.
