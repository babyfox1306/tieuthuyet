Something knocked under the floor, and the black thread in Elara's wrist knocked back.

The response was not muscle. It was not nerve. It was a pull, a turning, as if the ink line beneath her skin had lifted its head to listen. She pressed her palm flat against the studio floorboards. The wood was cold. The vibration, when it came again, traveled up through her radius and ulna straight into her molars. There was no pain there anymore. Only the pressure of sound without the ache to anchor it.

She held her breath and waited. The knock did not repeat.

The metallic shimmer was there. It danced at the edge of her vision without source and without shadow, a shiver of iridescence that threw no warmth and cast no reflection. She had stopped trying to look at it directly. Looking made it slip. But tonight it moved with intent, gathering near the far wall where the plaster met the wooden wainscoting.

Elara pushed herself up. Her knees ached with the absence of pain. The climb from floor to standing was wrong—her body moved, but the expected protest had been hollowed out.

The shimmer pooled over a section of wall that looked wrong. The paint was matte where the rest was eggshell, the seam of the old patch visible in the low lamplight. A scratch mark the length of her index finger pointed at the floor. An arrow. Drawn into the wood by a thumbnail or a knife tip.

The palette knife was still in the drawer. Elara slid the blade under the edge of the repainted patch. The plaster gave way too easily, crumbling into fine grey dust. She caught the fall with her palm. It was warm. Paint ought to be cold. The dust gathered in the lines of her lifeline, and there, threaded through the ash-colored residue, were filaments. Black. Hair-fine. Identical to the ones moving under her own skin.

Her hand began to shake. Not from fear. The threads in her wrist were reaching. Not spreading across her skin but leaning toward the pile of ash in her palm. The black lines under her dermis rose, pressing in tiny waves, like roots scenting water. Or soil.

She told herself it was coincidence. Tremor from the vibration. Overactive circulation from the injection. She told herself the filaments in the wall were old paint fibers, dust from construction, anything. But her teeth ached from the floor's low thrum, and the ache had no pain attached. It was pure, dislocated pressure, like being screamed at inside a soundproof room.

The door swung open.

Tobias Rehn stood in the frame. His grey patient robe hung loose on his shoulders, and his hair was dark with sweat. His eyes went to her hand, to the palette knife, to the scraped wall.

"You found it," he said. His voice was a scrape. "You found one."

He crossed the room in three strides and shoved a roll of paper into her free hand. The charcoal was dense, black, anatomical. An arm. A spine. A ribcage opened like a cabinet. Overlaid on the bone were systems. Branching. Rootlike. Vascular. The lines mirrored the threads on her wrist. They mapped a body that was one quarter muscle and three quarters ink.

"What is this?"

Tobias's face was bloodless. "What they're making."

He looked at the scraped wall. "She tried to stop it. Beatrice. She found the old records. Dr. Kreutler's notes."

"Kreutler?"

"The man before Finch. He drew these. Before he died." Tobias grabbed her wrist, turned it over. His fingers were cold. "Look. This is you. This is what you're becoming."

The drawings showed the arm first. The humerus wrapped in black lines that spiraled like a barber pole. The elbow joint caged in ink. The spine was worse. The vertebrae were linked not by discs but by a solid column of black, branching off to the ribs like roots through soil. And the chest. The heart was gone. The space where it should be was filled with a mass of dense, rootlike structures that spread into the lungs and throat.

Elara traced her own arm. She felt the lines under her skin. They were following the pattern in the drawing.

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because tomorrow I am cured." Tobias's voice cracked. "Tomorrow I walk out of here. And I don't know if I will remember my own name by the time I reach the gate."

Footsteps in the corridor. His head snapped up.

"Don't let them take these," he whispered. He reached down, pried a loose floorboard near the scraped wall, and shoved the drawings into the dark space beneath. "Tell them you found nothing. Tell them the treatment is working."

"The salt," Elara said. "What about the salt?"

Tobias's eyes widened. "If they offer you salt, refuse it. Say your stomach is weak. Say anything. But do not take it."

He was already moving toward the window. His body slipped into the dark before she could ask more.

The corridor footsteps grew louder.

Elara wiped her palm on her skirt. The ash dissolved into the fabric, but the filaments clung to her skin. She forced herself to breathe. The floorboard was back in place. The scraped wall was visible, but nothing about the room screamed conspiracy.

Cora Vale stopped in the doorway. Behind her stood Oskar Lenz, the groundskeeper, holding a small burlap sack. His hands were rough, the nails cracked from alpine work.

"Kitchen stock," Oskar said.

Cora took the sack from him. She hefted it, ran her palm over the coarse fabric. A few crystals escaped through the weave onto the floor. Pinkish. Coarse.

"This is not kitchen stock, Lenz. This is therapy adjunct. It goes to the locked pharmacy."

"It was on the list," Oskar said flatly.

Cora's jaw tightened. She saw Elara standing in the studio. "Miss Sennewald. You are meant to be resting."

"What is that?" Elara asked. She kept her voice light. "Salt? My mother used to put coarse salt in my bath for my joints. The old Austrian remedy."

Cora smiled. It did not reach her eyes. "This is a different kind of salt. Not for baths. Not for curiosity."

She turned and walked away. Oskar followed, his boots heavy on the floorboards.

Elara stood in the doorway and watched them go. The burlap sack swung between them. Cora held it like something precious. Like something that could burn her if it broke open.

She was still standing there when Dr. Finch appeared.

He came from the opposite end of the corridor, hands clasped behind his back, footsteps silent. He had changed his coat since the evening rounds. This one was lighter. Cleaner. The collar was turned up against the draft.

"Miss Sennewald. You look agitated."

He stepped past her into the studio. His eyes swept the room—the scraped wall, the palette knife on the floor, the loose floorboard. He did not stop at any of them. He found her.

"The transition is demanding," he said. "Heightened sensory reading, nervous preoccupation, fixation on textures and hidden meanings in surfaces. It is entirely expected."

He picked up a stray piece of charcoal from the table and turned it over.

"The mind seeks patterns. You will see warnings in every scratch. Faces in every grain. But I assure you, the old walls of this sanatorium have been scraped and patched a hundred times. The marks are from pipes. From miners' tools. From nervous patients before you."

Elara said nothing.

Finch set the charcoal down. "You have been in the studio too long. The light is poor. The textures are uneven. It creates a sensory loop."

He crossed to her and took her wrist. His fingers were cool, professional. They pressed against the threads.

She felt them settle.

The pull toward the scraped residue ceased. The vibration in the floor seemed to recede. Under his touch, the ink in her body went quiet. Like a snake under a stone.

"You are making excellent progress," he said. "But I am going to adjust your dose. The morning injection will be stronger. Your system needs to establish the pathways more efficiently."

He released her wrist.

"Nurse Cora will bring you warm milk with a sedative. Drink it. The studio will be locked for the night. You need uninterrupted rest."

"And my letters?" Elara said. "I need to write to my mother."

Finch smiled. "We will send word in the morning. Your condition is delicate, Miss Sennewald. Correspondence can wait until the treatment stabilizes."

He left.

The lock on the studio door clicked shut.

Elara stood alone in the room. The shimmer had gone still. It pooled near the window, a faint glint on the glass, saying nothing. The floorboard held the drawings. The wall held the scraped ash.

She lowered herself to the floor beside the loose board. She did not retrieve the drawings. Not yet. She simply sat. And in the silence, she brought her hand to her own wrist.

Her fingers touched the skin where Finch's had been. The threads were still there. Waiting.

She brushed her own fingers along the inside of her arm where another's hand might rest. The touch was only her own. The cold air offered nothing more. No warmth. No grip. No witness who was not already compromised.

Her fingers fell away.

She stood. The palette knife was still on the floor. She picked it up. The scraped residue clung to the blade—ash and filaments, dark against the steel.

She tilted the blade. The ash shifted. She shook it once.

The black threads under her wrist rose toward the skin like tiny roots scenting soil.
