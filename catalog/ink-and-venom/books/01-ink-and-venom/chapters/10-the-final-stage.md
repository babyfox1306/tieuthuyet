"Prepare her for completion," Finch said, and the thing beneath Elara's ribs answered him.

The pulse was not her heartbeat. It was wetter, older, a rhythm that pressed against the inside of her sternum like something testing a cage door. Elara kept her eyes open, kept her breathing shallow, kept her teeth locked together so the sound could not escape.

The lower mineral room was a grotto carved from salt-veined rock. The light came from nowhere and everywhere, a sulphuric glow that painted long shadows across the apparatus Finch called a treatment frame. Gears. Brackets. A trough that led into the floor.

Cora moved past her with the precision of a woman who had done this before.

Elara did not look at Cora. She looked at her own wrist, where the clinic dressing had gone dark at the edges. The salt was still there. Hidden beneath the gauze, pressed against the wound Tobias had helped her bandage before he vanished. The salt was sharp, crystalline, and it wanted to be found.

She flexed her fingers. The black threads beneath her skin flinched.

Not a withdrawal—a full-body shudder that rippled from her wrist up to her shoulder and down into her ribs. Her arm jerked against the leather strap, and the old pain slammed back into her hand like a door shut on broken fingers. She bit the inside of her cheek. Copper filled her mouth.

The salt burned. The threads burned. But beneath the burning, there was a clean line of sensation she had not felt in weeks—the texture of the metal table beneath her palm, the cold of the room against her cheek, the correct weight of her own body pressing down.

Her body recognized the salt as interruption.

Across the chamber, the air thickened.

Elara knew the quality of that shimmer now. She had followed it from the studio through the sealed stairwell into the archives where Tobias's salt warnings had been hidden. She had watched it trace the walls of the lower corridor on the night Cora listened to the sound beneath the floors.

Beatrice did not speak. Beatrice did not walk. But Beatrice had learned to refuse.

The iridescent light moved along the mineral wall. It did not dance. It traced. A seam. A salt-white crack that ran from floor to ceiling. Then another. A cluster of scratches in the rock that were too deliberate to be geological. The shimmer lingered over them, a silent instruction, then drifted to the next seam.

It was showing her a route.

The route did not lead deeper into the apparatus. It led away.

"You feel the pulse," Finch said. He had not moved from his station beside the gauges, but his voice came from everywhere, layered over the mineral hum. "That is the organism recognizing you. Accepting you. When the final stage is complete, you will not remember pain. You will not remember loneliness. You will be whole."

Whole. The word curdled in Elara's throat.

She wanted to scream at him. She wanted to tear the straps off and drive her fist into his grey chest. But the thing beneath her ribs replied for her—a low, territorial thrum that vibrated through the table and into the apparatus. A needle on a gauge flickered. A pipe sighed.

"The final stage," Finch repeated. "You will not feel yourself giving way. You will simply stop being the part that hurts."

Elara looked at her wrist. At the salt burning under the bandage. At the black threads, pale and thrashing just beneath her skin.

"No," she said.

It was not defiant. It was flat. A fact.

"I am not going to stop being the part that hurts."

Finch's stillness cracked. Not into anger—into pity.

"Nurse Vale," he said. "The restraint frame."

Cora moved. Her efficiency was a horror of its own—she did not look at Elara's face. She simply wheeled the second apparatus from the shadow. A metal cage on casters, lined with straps and brackets that did not look designed for comfort.

Elara's wrist burned. The salt was eating her. The pain was eating her. But the pulse beneath her ribs stuttered. Thrum. Pause. Thrum. Pause. The organism was confused. It could feel the interruption.

She shifted her weight and let her fingers brush the waistband of her clinic trousers. Tobias's sketchbook pages were folded flat against her hip. The salt diagram. The nerve maps. The charcoal warnings that had taken her this far. She pressed her palm flat against the metal table. The cold there was a friend.

Cora locked the restraint frame into position. Her hands were steady, her face unreadable. Elara watched her adjust the heel brackets and saw the faintest tremor in her fingers. Not fear. Muscle memory. The body remembering something the mind did not speak.

"The frame is prepared," Cora said. Her voice was exactly level. "Heel straps, wrist brackets, spinal brace."

Finch nodded. "Begin the transfer protocol."

Elara looked at Cora's hand. A human hand. Warm beneath the latex gloves. Her own fingers twitched, reaching for it, reaching for the human contact the organism had promised to replace with perfect unity. She wanted to grip Cora's wrist. She wanted to say "I am still here. Do not let him put me in the frame".

But Cora was not the one who could help. Cora was the one who tightened the straps.

Elara's fingers closed on air. The metal table. The bandage. The hidden salt.

No hand reached for hers. There was no lover in the corner, no friend at the door, no voice telling her it would be all right. There was only the cold, and the salt, and the shimmer showing her how to leave.

"The salt," Finch said. He stepped closer. His chest, solid grey beneath the white coat, did not rise and fall with breath. "You have been using the salt."

Elara did not confirm. Did not deny. She just looked past him, at the hatch the shimmer had revealed.

"You do not understand what you are interrupting," Finch said. "The salt cannot reverse what has already been replaced. It can only delay. And the delay is painful."

"I know," Elara said. Her voice was hoarse. "I know it is painful. I choose the pain."

Finch's head tilted. A gesture of genuine curiosity. "Why?"

Because pain meant she was still separate. Still herself. Still fighting.

Because Tobias had drawn the salt route with his own hand before they took him away.

Because Beatrice had shown her the door.

Elara did not say any of this. She held the salt against her wrist and let the old pain fill her veins like a drowning woman finding air.

Cora reached for Elara's arm.

The pulse beneath Elara's ribs screamed.

She rolled her sleeve down. She pressed the salt-stained bandage flat against her wrist. And she looked at the service hatch that Beatrice's shimmer had unlocked.

"If I refuse the final stage," Elara said, "what happens?"

Finch did not answer.

Cora's hands hesitated on the brace.

The shimmer flared. Once. A burst of light that showed every salt seam in the wall, every scratch in the stone, every handhold that would carry a body out of this room.

And then the light settled on the hatch.

Elara felt the thing beneath her ribs quiet.

It was waiting to see what she would do.

"Hold still," Cora said. But her voice was thinner now, less sure. "The frame is calibrated to your measurements."

Calibrated. As if she were a dress.

Elara watched the hatch. She had no plan. She had only the salt, the sketches, and the shimmer that had never lied to her.

Cora's fingers closed around Elara's wrist.

The organism thrummed. The machine hummed.

And Beatrice's shimmer did not fade.

It pooled against the salt-white crack like ink against wax, refusing to let the seam disappear. Elara saw the scratches clearly now—Tobias's marks, or someone else's, telling her to pull.

Cora tightened the brace.

The pulse in Elara's ribs stuttered.

The shimmer struck.

It was not fire. It was not reflection. It was a visual detonation that painted a crack of salt-white brilliance across the stone, and the hatch swung inward.

Elara's breath stopped.

Cora did not turn. She had not seen it. The light had no source she could perceive.

"The transfer protocol," Cora said, but her voice was distant now, muffled by the sound of blood in Elara's ears.

Elara stared at the salt-white darkness beyond the hatch. At the hidden passage that had opened in the wall.

At escape.

The restraint frame loomed beside her, metal and leather and calibrated brackets.

Cora reached for Elara's other wrist.

And the shimmer curved, once, through the open hatch, inviting her into the dark.
