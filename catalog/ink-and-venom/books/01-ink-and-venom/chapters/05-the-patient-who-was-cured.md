The black threads on the gurney patient's hand reached his fingernails, and Elara understood — this was a cured man leaving the clinic for the lower floor.

The orderly pushed the stretcher without looking at her, his rubber soles marking a wet rhythm against the linoleum. Nurse Cora walked beside it, her face sealed, her hands clasped at her waist like a lock that had no key.

"Cured," Elara said.

Cora stopped. She turned. The look she gave Elara was not unkind, but it was flat. The flatness of a surface that had been wiped clean too many times.

"Discharge protocol," Cora said. "The lower corridor connects to the convalescence wing for altitude adjustment."

Elara stepped closer. The patient's hand hung over the edge of the stretcher, fingers curled inward like dead leaves. The threads reached his cuticles. They were thick now, visible as a dark architecture beneath the skin, a network that had replaced something vital.

"Is he awake?"

"He is resting," Cora said.

"He looks grey."

Cora's jaw tightened by a fraction of a millimeter. "The therapy taxes the body's resources. He will recover his color once the pathways stabilize."

"Cured," Elara repeated, testing the weight of the word. It tasted flat in her mouth, like chalk.

Cora did not answer. She gestured to the orderly, and the gurney rolled forward toward the door at the end of the corridor. The one marked with a brass plate Elara had not read closely enough. "Service Access."

The door swung open. The gurney passed through.

The door closed.

The lock engaged.

Elara stood alone in the sudden quiet. The corridor lights hummed. The air was cold and motionless.

Her hand lifted from her side without her deciding it to. It floated in the space where the gurney had been, the fingers curling around nothing. A gesture meant for another person. A hand to hold. A shoulder to touch. Someone who could say, "That is strange, and you are right to be afraid."

But there was no one.

Her arm dropped. The weight of being alone pressed against her ribs like a second set of bones.

---

She found Finch in his office with the door half-open. He was reading a file, his cold tea untouched on the desk beside him. He looked up when she entered, and his expression adjusted into something practiced and soft.

"Miss Sennewald. You look troubled."

"I saw a cured patient being wheeled to the lower floor."

He set the file down. "Yes. The discharge wing is on the lower level."

"The exit is on the ground floor."

Finch smiled. It was a patient smile. The kind given to a child who has asked the same question three times. "The sudden change in altitude can destabilize the new neural pathways. We acclimatize the body gradually. It is a standard precaution."

"No one talks about being cured," Elara said. "Tobias drew a man with roots instead of veins. He said being cured is something to fear."

Finch's smile did not waver. "Tobias is a troubled young man with a rich imagination. His drawings are expressions of his own anxiety, not medical records."

"Then tell me what the lower floor looks like."

"It looks like a hospital," Finch said evenly. "Wards. Beds. Windows that open onto the mountain face. Nothing remarkable."

"And the patients who are cured there. Do they write to the others?"

Finch picked up his cold tea. He sipped it. The gesture was slow, deliberate, giving him time to choose his words. "Correspondence is often delayed during the final phase of treatment. Nervous rest. The mind needs quiet to integrate the changes."

"The letters are intercepted."

"The letters are managed," he corrected gently. "For the patient's benefit."

Elara held his gaze. He did not look away. His stillness was a wall she could not climb. Smooth, clinical, and utterly without handholds.

"Your art therapy is showing real progress," he said. "I read Nurse Cora's notes. You spent hours in the studio yesterday. That is excellent. The palette knife work, the textures — these are exactly the kind of sensory anchors your system needs."

"My system."

"Your new pathways," Finch said. "They respond to touch, to texture, to visual rhythm. The studio is not a distraction, Miss Sennewald. It is part of the treatment. I would hate for you to abandon it over gossip and groundless suspicion."

He stood. The interview was over.

"I recommend you return to the studio," he said. "Draw what you saw. It will help you process the anxiety."

He said nothing else. His hand rested on the file, covering the name written on the tab.

She left.

---

The studio was cold. The afternoon light came through the high windows in grey bands, carrying dust and the smell of dry plaster.

The shimmer was waiting.

It played along the edge of the unused easel, a ribbon of metallic light that shifted between silver and copper and the colour of petrol on water. It was dancing without wind, existing without source.

Elara closed the door.

"Beatrice," she said.

The shimmer did not respond. It never did. But she kept trying. She could not stop trying.

"Please. If you know what this place is doing, if you know what the lower floor is, show me. Give me something I can use."

The shimmer moved across the canvas. It pooled. It spread. It reflected the light from the window and offered nothing else.

Elara stepped closer. She raised her hand toward it. Her fingers passed through the place where the light lay.

There was nothing. No warmth. No resistance. No message.

She tried again. Slower. Her palm hovered an inch from the surface of the light.

The shimmer did not reach for her. It did not flinch. It simply remained, untouchable, silent, a door that would not open.

Elara lowered her hand.

She sat on the floor beside the loose board where Tobias's drawings were hidden. She did not take them out. She did not need to. She had memorized every line. The black roots replacing the veins. The blank face of the cured man. The warning that no one in this building would speak aloud.

"He was wheeled to the lower floor," she told the empty room. "They called him cured. He looked dead."

The shimmer did not answer.

She pulled her knees to her chest and watched it dance. It was the only witness she had, and it was useless.

---

At dusk, the corridor outside her room was empty. The lights had dimmed to a yellow haze that made the walls look older than they were. Elara walked toward the staircase, intending to test the lock on the lower door.

She stopped.

Nurse Cora was standing halfway down the corridor. Her back was to Elara. Her palm was pressed flat against the plaster, fingers spread wide as if she were reading braille.

Cora was completely still.

Not the stillness of someone thinking. The stillness of someone listening. Her head was tilted at an angle that suggested deep, patient attention. Every muscle in her spine was locked in the posture of reception.

Elara held her breath.

She watched Cora's hand against the wall. Watched the slight tension in her jaw. Watched the way her eyelids were half-closed, not in fatigue but in concentration.

A full minute passed.

Then Cora's hand lowered. She turned. She saw Elara standing in the dim light at the end of the corridor.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

"You should be in your room," Cora said. Her voice was careful. Controlled. It did not carry the usual flat authority.

"I was looking for the bathroom."

"It is the other direction."

Cora walked past her without another word. Her footsteps were even. Practiced. She did not look back.

Elara waited until the sound of those footsteps faded into silence.

Then she walked to the spot where Cora had been standing.

She pressed her palm against the cold wall.

At first, there was nothing. Lime and horsehair. Old plaster. The texture of a building that had settled into its foundation decades ago.

She closed her eyes.

She waited.

And then she felt it.

A vibration so low it was almost a pressure change against her skin. A pulse. Deep in the stone, deep beneath the floor, deep enough that it should not have been audible through a corridor wall.

Thump.

Thump.

Thump.

Her wrist responded. The black threads tightened. They loosened. They tightened again.

They were beating in time.

Elara pressed her ear to the plaster. The sound was clearer now. Not a heartbeat. Not a machine. Something organic, rhythmic, patient. It was not the mountain settling. It was not the building shifting. It was a presence. Regular. Aware.

Below her feet, something was waiting.

---

Alone in the corridor, Elara pressed her ear to the cold wall where Cora stood, caught a pulse that matched her wrist threads, and realized the whole clinic's silence was listening for something waiting beneath.
