# Chapter 3: Testimony Distorted

"This isn’t what I said," Sofia Bellini whispers, staring at the transcript. The words glare back, blue ink clinical against crisp government paper, yet hostile as anything shouted across a barricade.

The tribunal chamber is almost empty now—the scent of machine oil and disinfectant clings to the air, and the echo of polished shoes still haunts the corridor outside. But here, at the horseshoe table, it is only Sofia, Elias van Doren, and—just for a moment—the transcript that has decided to speak with a mouth not her own.

Elias is beside her, hunched over a second copy, his finger tracing the margin, jaw tense. "Y-you didn’t?" His stutter, quieter than usual, is laced with something heavier.

Sofia doesn’t look at him. She doesn’t need to. "No," she says, cool and deliberate. "I’m careful, Mr. van Doren. I never make statements I could regret."

She flips the page, her own handwriting visible in the margin: ‘Clarified route to exit; thirty-two survivors; recommended calm—’ All factual, all curated. But three lines down, there it is again, her name in parentheses, followed by the mechanical baring of her soul: "No one will ever know the truth if we keep cleaning up the record for them."

She resists the urge to smile. If someone is forging her voice, they have ambition. But not, perhaps, her skill.

Elias’s hand trembles on the paper. "It changed mine, too. Listen—see here?" He angles it toward her, the page wobbling. There’s a correction, written in official script: "Elias van Doren: I was afraid we’d all be lost. I wanted Sofia to speak for me."

The last line is a knife. Even now, his gaze flickers to her, desperate for some confirmation. He wants to know she isn’t the villain this page paints her as.

She leans in, lowering her voice to a hush meant just for him. "Did you say that?"

He swallows. "No. I—I never would. You know I—I don’t let anyone—"

"It’s rewriting you," Sofia murmurs, and watches a flush crawl up his neck as the words sink in.

He exhales carefully. "So it’s not… just error. Or panic."

"No." Her lips curve, thin and precise. "It’s order."

They sit with the word for a moment, the space between their chairs thick with questions they cannot utter. The system corrects them—clips passion, edits fear, invents trust where only calculation existed.

A page rustles behind them—Pierre Leclerc’s shadow falling over both of them. He’s not a large man, but the way his jacket shoulders fill the doorway, he might as well be a tidal wave of suspicion about to break.

"So," Pierre says, voice dry as the sand between tribunal tiles. "Already reviewing the record, Miss Bellini? You do like to get ahead of the story."

Sofia meets his gaze with a smile built for a funeral—serene, gentle, betraying nothing. "Only ensuring accuracy, Monsieur Leclerc. In times like these, clarity is essential."

He steps closer, eyes narrowed. His notebook is tucked beneath his arm, a single pen clipped with compulsive neatness. "And what do you make of these… improvements? Did you really say the panel was cleaning up the truth?"

She straightens her spine, hands folded atop the transcript. "That wouldn’t be very diplomatic, would it? You were there. Did I sound reckless to you?"

Pierre’s eyes dart to Elias, then back. "You sounded polished. Almost rehearsed."

She feigns disappointment. "Disappointing, isn’t it? People always expect drama from survivors."

Elias’s shoe taps—two beats, then a nervous third. Pierre leans in, lowering his voice. "I don’t like ghosts in the record, Miss Bellini. You should be careful. Words have a way of haunting the wrong people."

She holds his gaze. "Then let’s be precise, monsieur. After all, the record doesn’t belong to me alone."

Pierre doesn’t flinch, but he doesn’t press, either. He turns, pen poised, as if hoping to catch her off guard in the margins. Instead, Sofia sits perfectly still, not a hair out of place. Pierre leaves, footsteps echoing accusation, but she lets him go. He would tire himself out long before breaking her composure.

Elias lets out a shaky breath. He wipes his palm on his trouser leg. "He’s right. I remember your phrasing. And the transcript—look—" He slides his copy toward her, his hand brushing against hers.

The contact is fleeting, barely more than a spark, but it startles them both. Elias freezes, then retracts, but not before his thumb lingers at the edge of the page, almost—almost closing over her fingers.

Sofia does not move her hand away. She glances at him, expression neutral, and waits for him to speak.

"Sorry," he stammers, color rising again. "I just—"

"It’s all right," she says, softer than the tribunal deserves. "We should trust each other. No one else will protect our words."

He nods, eager, and in that moment the alliance is real—a boundary drawn not in ink, but in skin and breath.

They huddle over the transcripts, page by page, word by word. Sofia runs her finger down the margin, hunting for the tremor, the wrong note, the evidence of a ghost writer. It’s subtle. Sometimes her cautions become warnings, sometimes her reassurances gain a sharper edge. Elias’s fears are stated with a clarity that’s not his; doubt and vulnerability smoothed away into something almost noble.

"They’re making us braver," Elias says, almost in wonder. "But not honest."

Sofia glances at him, and for a moment, there’s an ache beneath her ribs she refuses to acknowledge. "Bravery is easier to market," she says, dry. "Truth is complicated."

From the far end of the room, a cough breaks the rhythm—Magda Lorenz, silent until now, folds her hands over her bag. Her gaze flicks to Sofia, unreadable, then to the door. "You two should leave," she murmurs. "Quietly. The system will be purged soon. There’s nothing left to clarify here tonight."

Sofia closes her transcript. But before she can gather her things, a sharp cry splits the corridor.

She’s on her feet instantly, Elias a step behind. Out in the anteroom, Tomasz Havel is kneeling beside a collapsed woman—one of the junior witnesses who testified earlier. Her breath rattles, eyes glassy with exhaustion. Tribunal officers rush in; one peels a page from a freshly printed transcript, the ink smudged where her name has just been ‘corrected.’

Sofia’s hands are gentle as she checks the woman’s pulse. Too fast, then fluttering, as though her body can’t remember which rhythm belongs to her.

"Stress," someone mutters. "Overwrought."

Sofia glances at Elias, who is staring at the page, horror widening his eyes. He mouths the words—"Corrected statement"—and the phrase "witness expressed relief at having the record amended" rings hollow between them.

Magda moves to intervene, but the officers wave her back. "Please, let the system do its work," says one, with the measured courtesy only the complicit can master.

Elias clenches his fists. "We can’t let this happen again—"

Sofia’s voice cuts through, sweet as a lullaby. "She needs rest, not interrogation. Please, give her air."

Her authority calms the room; she’s practiced at this, at mercy painted over violence. But inside, she is cold, counting. This is no coincidence—every ‘clarification’ draws them closer to collapse.

As the medics arrive, Sofia returns to the chamber. Magda pulls her aside, words fierce but low. "They’ll start looking for someone to blame, child. Don’t make yourself a target."

Sofia’s smile is a razor sheathed in velvet. "I’m only helping. No one could fault kindness, Magda."

Elias joins them, haunted. "We should leave."

She doesn’t argue, not with him. The three slip into the corridor, the hush of after-hours settling over the marble and glass.

Outside, Pierre Leclerc waits near the barricade, arms folded. He appraises Sofia, then holds out a page—her own transcript, this copy missing the tribunal’s official wax seal.

"Strange," he says, tapping the margin. "Your intervention here is unsanctioned. Yet it made it into the master record. Do you see?"

Sofia’s smile is fixed, patient. "Someone must have forgotten to stamp it. Or perhaps the system is faster than we are."

Pierre’s voice is gentle, but his scrutiny is scalpel-sharp. "Tell me, Miss Bellini. Do you always remember exactly what you say—or only what you wish you had said?"

Elias bristles, moving half a step closer to Sofia—a silent defense, a subtle claim. "If you have doubts, raise them formally. Sofia’s record is cleaner than anyone’s here."

Pierre’s gaze flicks between them, calculating. "Is it? Then why do her words change when she leaves the room?"

Sofia tilts her head. "Because survivors edit themselves, monsieur. It’s the only way we live with memory."

He snorts, unconvinced, and drifts toward the archive room, journal clutched like a shield.

Elias exhales shakily. "He’s not wrong," he says, voice quiet. "But it’s not you, is it?"

She glances at him. Her impulse is to reassure, to smooth his nerves, but something—pride, or perhaps the strange thrill of being misread—holds her back. "Of course not."

They descend the stairs side by side, transcript clutched between them, the night air sharp after the processed stillness of the chamber. Sofia wants to laugh at the absurdity: two nearly grown children, each accused by their own testimony, united by faith and what the record refuses to say.

At the foot of the steps, an officer in tribunal blue blocks their path. Clipboard in hand, voice flat and echoing, he reads from a prepared line.

"Sofia Bellini, please return to the chamber. There is a discrepancy between your spoken testimony and the official record."

A silence falls. Magda’s hand tightens on her bag. Elias’s eyes widen, searching her face for any sign of fear.

The officer continues, each word nailed into the air. "You will explain—to the tribunal, and to the archive—how your statement came to be altered. On record. In front of everyone."

Sofia stands very still, transcript pressed against her side. She meets Elias’s gaze for one heartbeat—a promise passing between them, silent as the missing seal on the page.

And then she squares her shoulders and walks past the officer, back into the tribunal’s fluorescent glare.
