# Chapter 6: The Clarification

"Let me clarify that for the record."

The official's voice on the tape was calm, reasonable. When the translation system replayed that sentence at dawn, Nadia Petrescu's words had been erased.

Sofia's hand trembled against the cassette player. She shouldn't be playing this. The silence window had locked them all into soundless terror hours ago, but she had found the player in the archives with a set of foam headphones, and the compulsion to know had outweighed any fear of consequences.

Yuki sat beside her, flashlight aimed at the floor, the beam catching dust motes that swirled in the stale air. They had been searching for nearly an hour when Yuki's fingers found the loose panel behind the shelf. A trick of the light—a shadow that didn't match. She had pried it open with her fingernails, and there it was.

A cassette tape.

Labeled by hand in blue ink: "Petrescu – original testimony."

Sofia ran her thumb over the label. The ink caught the light differently than it should have. It was glossy, still wet to the touch. Fresh.

Someone had been here. Someone had listened to this tape recently. In the past days, possibly even since the summit began.

Sofia pressed the foam headphones against her ears and pressed play.

"I said her statement requires clarification." The official's voice was smooth, practiced. A rustle of paper followed. "Miss Petrescu's account of the evening of October 12th is... emotionally compromised. We need to ensure the record reflects what actually occurred."

A younger voice. Female. Strained. "I told you what happened. I wrote it down. Every word."

"That is your version, Miss Petrescu. The board requires an official version."

"They are the same thing."

"They are not."

Sofia's breath caught. The voices were so vivid, so human. Decades old, but alive in her ears.

"Let me clarify," the official said again, and Sofia heard the smile in his voice. The practiced patience of someone who held all the power. "You said the system malfunctioned. You said it translated your words into something you did not recognize. But what you meant to say was that you misunderstood the instructions. That you failed to comply with the protocol. Is that not correct?"

Silence on the tape. Then Nadia, smaller now: "That is not what I said."

"But this is what you meant."

"I know what I meant!"

"And the board will record what you meant, Miss Petrescu. For your own protection. So there is no confusion."

Sofia's fingers dug into the plastic casing.

Clarification. He called it clarification.

She thought of Elias. Every time she had jumped in to finish his sentence. Every time she had rephrased his struggled words into something smoother, more articulate. Every time she had told herself she was helping.

She was the official.

She was the one clarifying.

The tape clicked and shifted. A new voice, younger, wearier. "Correction count for witness Petrescu, November session. Original statement: four pages. Official transcript: eleven pages. Words altered: three hundred forty-two. Words inserted: two hundred seventeen. Words removed: eighty-nine."

Yuki was already holding the transcript page. She had found it folded inside the cassette case. Yellowed paper, typewritten, columns of numbers that tracked every session.

Sofia took it from her, hands shaking.

Session after session. Hundreds of alterations. Each one a small death.

"Clarifications count for final session," the voice on the tape continued. "Words spoken by subject: one hundred eighteen. Words recorded as official testimony: four hundred three. Consistency rating: twelve percent."

Twelve percent.

Only twelve percent of Nadia's testimony had survived. Everything else had been rewritten.

Sofia thought of Elias. Of his halting speech, the words that fought their way out of him. She thought of every time she had translated him, reworded him, made him sound more articulate than he was.

She had increased his word count. She had reduced his consistency rating.

She had been feeding the system.

Yuki's hand found hers. Squeezed. Sofia looked up. Yuki's eyes were wet.

They both understood.

---

The dawn came without warning. Light bled through the high windows of the archives, thin and gray. The chime had not yet sounded. The silence window still held them.

Sofia carried the cassette player up the stairs. Yuki followed, clutching the transcript page. The building seemed different in the morning light—less sinister, more ordinary. But the hum remained. That constant mechanical breathing beneath everything.

The group was gathered in the conference room. Pierre sat at the head of the table, his journalist's notebook open but blank. Dmitri stood by the window, arms crossed. The others were scattered around the edges of the room, faces drawn with the exhaustion of a sleepless night.

Elias was near the door.

His eyes found hers the moment she entered.

She placed the cassette player on the table. Her hands were steady now. She had stopped shaking somewhere between the archives and the stairs.

"You need to hear this," she mouthed. No sound. The silence window held.

She pressed play.

The official's voice filled the room.

"Let me clarify that for the record."

They listened. All of them. No one interrupted. No one breathed. The tape played through to the end, to the final click, to the silence that followed.

Dmitri was the first to break the stillness. He wrote on a scrap of paper and slid it across the table.

"Clarification. The system punishes clarification."

Sofia shook her head. She wrote beneath his words.

"It punishes being clarified. The official rewrote Nadia's words. He clarified them. And the system tracked every rewrite. Every time someone's words are rewritten by someone else, it counts. And the person with the highest count..."

She couldn't finish the sentence.

Pierre picked up the pen. His handwriting was shaky. "The person who is translated the most. The person who is corrected the most. The person who is spoken for."

Sofia nodded.

She looked at Elias.

He was reading the transcript page. His eyes moved across the columns of numbers. Three hundred forty-two words altered. Two hundred seventeen inserted. Eighty-nine removed.

He looked up.

She couldn't read his expression. There was no fear there. No anger. Only something quiet and accepting.

The chime sounded.

The silence window ended.

Voices returned like water breaking through a dam. Everyone spoke at once, fragmented sentences, overlapping questions.

Sofia didn't hear any of it.

She was still looking at Elias.

"I have been clarifying you," she said. "Every day. Every sentence. Every time I thought I was helping."

"I know."

The words were quiet. Certain.

Her throat tightened. "The system has a count for you. For every time I rephrased what you were trying to say. For every time I made you sound clearer than you were."

"I know."

"Elias, I might have—"

"I know."

He crossed the room. Each step measured, deliberate. He stopped in front of her, close enough that she could see the darkness in his irises, the way his throat worked as he tried to find his voice.

"I know what you did," he said. "I know why you did it. And I would let you do it again."

"Don't say that."

" Why not? It's the truth."

"Because I can't be the reason you—"

"You are not the reason." He took her hand. "The system is the reason. The people who built it are the reason. You are the reason I am still speaking at all."

She pulled her hand back. She reached for the paper, the warning she had drafted in the archives by flashlight.

"Stop clarifying. Stop finishing sentences."

The words stared up at her.

But it was too late.

She had already done it. Every day. Every hour. Every conversation she had mediated, every sentence she had smoothed, every struggle she had erased.

The system had its count.

Elias's count.

She pressed the paper into his hand. "Read this. Follow it. Don't let me speak for you anymore."

He looked at the paper. Then at her.

"Too late," he said softly. "I don't want you to stop."

"Elias—"

"That is what the system wants." He held up the paper. "It wants us to stop communicating. It wants us to stop trusting each other. It wants us to be afraid to speak for each other."

"It wants to kill you."

"It wants to isolate us. Killing is just the method."

She stared at him. He was not afraid. He was not backing down.

He caught her hand.

She hadn't realized she had dropped the paper. It floated to the floor, landing face-down. The warning vanished from sight.

Elias's fingers wrapped around hers. Warm. Steady. He did not try to speak. He did not write a note.

He lifted her hand to his lips and pressed them against her palm.

It was not a kiss of passion.

It was a seal. A mark. A declaration.

I choose this. I choose you.

Tears slipped down Sofia's cheeks. She did not wipe them away. She let them fall, let him see every drop of terror and love that was splitting her open.

The building hummed.

The translation system was processing. It was counting. It was reading every word they had ever spoken, every clarification, every revision, every well-intentioned rewrite.

Somewhere in the basement, the tapes kept turning. The records kept growing.

And the countdown to the next sunset had already begun.

Deep in the walls, in the space between wires and concrete, the translation system hummed to life, processing their newest words.
