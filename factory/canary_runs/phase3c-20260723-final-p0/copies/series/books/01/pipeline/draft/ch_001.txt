# Chapter 1: The Sound of Dawn

"You speak for him?"

The summit coordinator's voice carried the practiced patience of someone who had asked the same question a hundred times before. Her eyes moved from Elias van Doren's clenched jaw to Sofia Bellini's ready lips.

"Yes," Sofia said, before Elias could force his voice out.

She felt the weight of twelve stares pressing against her skin. Twelve students from twelve countries, gathered in a conference room that smelled like old wood and newer anxiety. The walls were paneled in dark oak, the windows sealed with electronic shutters that had been locked before any of them arrived. A summit, they had been told. A cultural exchange. A chance to prove themselves.

Sofia had expected translators. She had expected challenges. She had not expected to feel like a hostage before lunch.

Elias sat beside her, his hands flat on the table, fingers tapping a rhythm only he could hear. She had noticed the tapping during the welcome video, a steady pulse against his thigh. Now it had moved to the tabletop, a Morse code she could not decipher.

"It's better this way," Elias said. The words came slowly, each one pulled from somewhere deep. "I take time. She's faster."

The coordinator nodded, making a note on her tablet. "Elias van Doren, Netherlands. Assigned translator: Sofia Bellini, Italy. Noted."

Sofia's throat tightened. She had been assigned. The word felt like a brand.

She had arrived the night before, shuttled from the airport in a van with blacked-out windows, deposited at the summit's entrance like a package. The building was a converted academy perched on a cliff overlooking nothing but fog. No city lights in the distance. No roads visible beyond the gate. Just stone and glass and the constant hum of something mechanical beneath the floor.

She had slept in a room with a single bed, a desk, and a mirror that made her reflection look wrong. Too pale. Too tired. Too young.

Now she sat at a table designed for twenty, with twelve names on the seating chart and an earpiece in her hand that felt heavier than it should.

"Put them on," the coordinator said. "They will calibrate to your voice patterns. The summit operates in twelve languages. These earpieces will ensure everyone is understood."

Sofia fit the device into her ear. The plastic was warm, molded to a shape that matched her canal perfectly. But the weight was wrong. It pulled her ear down, dragging at the cartilage, as if filled with lead.

She adjusted it, then adjusted it again. Nothing fixed the sensation.

"Comfortable?" The question came from her left. Yuki Tanaka, a girl with sharp eyes and a sharper smile, already wearing her earpiece like she had been born with it.

"No," Sofia said.

"Good. It means you're paying attention."

Yuki's English was precise, each syllable cut clean. She was from Japan, her file had said, a prodigy in simultaneous interpretation. Sofia had read the dossiers during the van ride, memorizing names and faces, trying to prepare for an event she had not been told the purpose of.

The coordinator stood at the front of the room, her tablet glowing with the seating chart. "For the next seven days, you will speak only through these devices. No other communication devices are permitted. Your phones have been collected. Your laptops will remain in your rooms. This building operates on a closed system."

A hand went up near the window. Pierre Leclerc, France, his hair falling across his forehead in a way that seemed deliberate. "What happens if we don't use the earpieces?"

"Then you will not be understood."

"That seems simple enough."

The coordinator did not smile. "The summit is designed to test your abilities. Each day, you will be given a series of translation tasks. The system will track your accuracy. At the end of seven days, the most accurate translator will receive the fellowship."

Dmitri Ivanov leaned back in his chair, his arms crossed. He was large, broad-shouldered, with the look of someone who had spent more time in a gym than a library. "And the least accurate?"

"Will not receive the fellowship."

Sofia studied the coordinator's face. There was something flat in her tone, a careful neutrality that did not match the sealed windows and the locked doors and the earpieces that pulled at her ear like a weight.

She touched her throat, a habit she had never been able to break. Her locket was warm against her collarbone, the only thing she had been allowed to keep.

Elias was still tapping. She watched his fingers move, a rhythm she was beginning to recognize. He had not looked at the coordinator once. His eyes were fixed on the table, on the grain of the wood, on anything that was not another person.

"Elias," she said softly. "Do you want me to ask a question for you?"

His fingers stopped. Then started again, faster.

"No," he said, the word taking two seconds to emerge. "I can. Just. Takes time."

"Take as much time as you need."

She meant it. His voice was slow, but it was careful. Each word was measured, chosen like a stone placed in a wall. She could see the effort it cost him, the way his jaw tightened between sentences, the way his shoulders rose and fell with each breath.

The coordinator began handing out schedules. Twelve sheets of paper, one for each student. Sofia's listed her tasks for the next three days: orientation, group translation exercises, individual assessments, a cultural presentation.

"The dawn replay will begin each morning at six," the coordinator said. "The system will replay all spoken words from the previous day. This is to ensure accuracy and to reinforce learning. You are required to be present in the main hall for the replay."

Dawn replay. Sofia had not heard that term in any of the briefing documents. She made a mental note of it, filing it away with everything else that felt wrong.

The first day passed in a blur of introductions and exercises. Sofia translated for Elias during a group discussion on semantics, filling the gaps when his words came too slowly for the conversation. She did not mean to do it. She saw him struggling, saw the frustration building behind his eyes, and her mouth opened before her brain caught up.

"The point of lexical borrowing is not to fill a vacuum," she said, repeating what Elias had been trying to articulate. "It is to acknowledge that some concepts exist only in one language."

Elias looked at her. There was gratitude in his eyes, and something else. Shame.

She had seen that look before. She had seen it on the faces of everyone she had ever translated for, the quiet humiliation of needing someone else's voice.

After the exercise, he said, "You do not have to. Speak for me."

"I know."

"I can do it. Slowly."

"I know."

He pressed his lips together, a thin line of anxiety. "But thank you."

She nodded. Under the table, her hand found his. She squeezed once, brief, a touch that said I see you. His fingers curled around hers for a moment, and she felt the tension in his palm, the tremor he was trying to hide.

It was supposed to be a competition. Twelve translators, one fellowship. But sitting next to Elias van Doren, feeling the rhythm of his hand against hers, she did not want to compete against him.

She wanted to protect him.

Dinner was served in a hall that looked like a converted chapel. High ceilings, stained glass windows that had been painted over, a long table that could have seated thirty. They ate in silence, the earpieces humming with the occasional fragment of conversation.

Amara Okafor sat across from Sofia, her plate untouched. She was from Ghana, her profile said, a specialist in tonal languages. She had not spoken much during the exercises, but her eyes were sharp, cataloging everything.

"The food is good," Sofia said.

Amara looked up. "Is it?"

"Try the bread."

Amara picked up a piece, examined it, took a small bite. "It is bread."

"Better than nothing."

"Marginally."

Sofia almost smiled. There was something dry in Amara's tone, a humor that did not reach her face.

The conversation fragmented into smaller groups. Dmitri was arguing with Pierre about something, their voices rising and falling in a rhythm that did not need translation. Yuki was writing notes in a small notebook, her handwriting precise and small.

Sofia looked at Elias. He had not eaten much. His fingers were tapping against the table again, a rhythm she was starting to associate with his internal processing.

"What are you thinking about?" she asked.

He took a breath. Held it. Released. "The windows."

"Sealed?"

"No. The glass. It is. Thicker than it should be."

She looked at the stained glass windows, their colors muted by layers of paint. He was right. The glass was thick, paned in a way that suggested more than decoration.

"They don't want us to see out," she said.

"Or they don't. Want anyone. To see in."

The thought settled in her chest like a cold stone.

Before bed, the coordinator gathered them in the main hall. "The dawn replay begins at six. Be here. Speak only what you mean, because it will be repeated. The system records everything."

Sofia's earpiece hummed. She touched it, feeling the weight pull at her ear.

In the hallway, she passed Amara, who was standing by a window, looking out at the fog.

"Goodnight," Amara said. The word was simple, casual.

"Goodnight," Sofia replied.

The word followed her into her room, into the narrow bed, into the dark of a building that hummed with machinery beneath the floor.

Something is wrong, she thought. But she did not know what.

Sleep came slowly, in fragments. She dreamed of her locket opening, of a voice she did not recognize speaking words she did not understand. She dreamed of Elias tapping his fingers against a wall, his rhythm matching the hum of the building.

She dreamed of Amara, standing in the fog, repeating one word over and over.

Goodnight.

Goodnight.

Goodnight.

Sofia woke to the sound of her earpiece crackling.

The room was dark. The window was still sealed. The hum beneath the floor had changed pitch, rising to a frequency that vibrated in her teeth.

She sat up, her heart hammering.

The earpiece was warm. Hot. She pulled it out, but the sound continued, coming from somewhere else, from the walls, from the ceiling, from everywhere.

The dawn replay.

She dressed in seconds, her hands shaking, her locket swinging against her chest. She ran to the main hall, where the other students were gathering, their faces pale in the dim light.

The coordinator stood at the front of the room, her tablet in her hand. "The replay will begin in one minute. Please remain silent during the process."

Sofia found Elias. He was standing near the back, his arms crossed, his fingers still. He looked at her, and she saw the fear in his eyes, the same fear she felt.

She moved to stand beside him.

The speakers crackled.

A voice filled the room. It was a collection of sounds, fragments of conversation from the day before, stitched together in a tapestry of language. Sofia heard her own voice, translating one of Elias's sentences. She heard Yuki's precise English. She heard Dmitri's Russian, Pierre's French.

Then the word.

Goodnight.

Amara's voice, clear and simple.

But what echoed back was not goodnight.

The speakers screamed. A strangled plea, wet and desperate, in a language that did not exist in any of Sofia's training. It was a sound that should not have come from a human throat, twisted and broken and wrong.

Sofia turned to look at Amara.

Amara was standing in the center of the room, her eyes wide, her mouth open. The sound was still playing, still clawing through the speakers, and Amara's lips were moving, trying to form the word she had said, the simple word that had been corrupted.

She fell.

Her body hit the floor with a sound that was too loud, too final.

Blood trickled from her ear, a thin line of red against her skin.

The speakers fell silent.

Someone screamed. Sofia did not know who. She could not hear anything over the pounding of her own heart.

She looked at Amara, at the blood pooling beneath her head, at the earpiece that had fallen from her ear and was still crackling, still whispering fragments of that strangled plea.

The coordinator stepped forward, her face expressionless. "The dawn replay has concluded."

Sofia's hand found Elias's. He was shaking. They were both shaking.

She squeezed his fingers, but she did not know if she was comforting him or begging him to hold her together.

Amara was not moving.

No one was going to help her.

The building hummed beneath them, the sound rising, falling, waiting.

Sofia looked at the sealed windows, at the thick glass, at the fog that refused to lift.

And she realized, with a certainty that settled into her bones like lead:

They were not here to be translators.

They were here to be heard.

And they were not going to leave.
