# Chapter 2: Testimony in the Ashes

Every testimony points to her. What am I missing — or is that the point? Pierre tapped his pen, rapid staccato, against the battered tape recorder balanced on his knee. His eyes swept the dull surface of the interview table, its veneer cracked and scorched at one corner, a relic of some past fire. Across from him, Magda Lorenz adjusted her cuffs, the overhead light slicing her face into planes of wary resolve and evasiveness.

Magda was the tribunal’s most tenacious survivor, her file thick with redactions and faded court stamps. Her gaze flicked restlessly to the door, avoiding Pierre’s steady scrutiny as if the room itself might betray her secrets.

“You’re on record as having worked closely with Sofia Bellini during the first phase of the tribunal system trials.” Pierre’s tone was almost gentle, but there was a razor behind it. “Can you clarify her relationship to Nadia Petrescu in the days leading up to the summit collapse?”

Magda smiled, thin-lipped. “Clarify. That’s one thing this building teaches you there’s never enough of.” She toyed with a plastic badge at her lapel, thumb brushing over the tribunal’s faded insignia. “Sofia… Sofia kept to herself mostly. She was methodical. I don’t think anyone truly knew what she felt about Nadia or anyone, if that’s what you’re after.”

“You gave sworn testimony on May twenty-third,” Pierre pressed, flipping a page in his folder so a corner caught the light. “You said Sofia and Nadia were ‘inseparable, co-conspiratorial.’ Has your assessment changed?”

A pause. Magda’s fingers found the edge of the table, nails clicking softly. “Memory is slippery, Monsieur Leclerc. When everyone around you is certain, you question your own recollections.” She exhaled, glancing at the cracked ceiling. “Maybe I overstated their bond. The record is what it is.”

Pierre’s pen stilled. “The record is not always what happened.”

Magda looked at him flatly. “Then you should talk to the person who built the record.” She gathered her bag. “Sofia Bellini built the system. That’s what matters now.”

Her words hung, smoke-thick, as she rose and left with a click of disappointed heels. Pierre started the tape again, reviewing her responses, searching for hesitation, for any syllable out of place. All he found were the echoes of her evasions.

He shut the recorder off, jaw tight. If Sofia’s guilt was painted in broad strokes across every testimonial canvas, why did each witness seem to retreat behind the same fortress of certainty?

He thumbed open the confidential folder still ink-scented from the archive’s dark. On the first sheet, a new statement had been appended since his last survey. The header was fresh — Sofia Bellini: Addendum. It had been entered less than twelve hours prior, signed off by Chief Adjudicator Rasmus himself.

The confession was concise, almost chilling in its self-awareness. Sofia’s voice, transcribed with mechanical neutrality, seemed to bleed through the typeface:

“I, Sofia Bellini, accept responsibility for the design, deployment, and operational oversight of the tribunal system. Every parameter, every safeguard, every potential for unintended consequence was mine to consider. I did this knowingly, and I do not contest the results. I accept responsibility.”

Pierre rubbed at the ink smudge on his thumb. Chillingly consistent. He had cross-referenced her original statements countless times, and each version, each confession — whether in written affidavit, video transcript, verbal acknowledgment before the council — matched to the word. No revision, no regret, not even a stray hesitation. Even her tics, the clipped pauses before she spoke, were noted in the margins like musical notations.

He read the testimony again. On the second page, an anomaly snagged his eye. A line of marginalia, written in angular red ink, cut beneath the text:

“Was there any moment of doubt?”

No attribution. No initials. He flipped through his notes, searching for any prior reference to this particular addendum, but found nothing. No one had initialed the comment, and none of the official sign-off stamps were in red. He pressed his thumb against the margin. The ink did not smudge. It felt recent, but he could not say by whom.

A soft knock at the door jolted him. Pierre glanced up to find Elena Brask, her notepad clutched tight, stepping into the corridor’s haze.

“Still digging up the architect’s bones?” Her tone was teasing, but the edge of rivalry glittered beneath. Her articles always shadowed his, but sided with the official record, never the inconsistencies he clung to.

“Just observing the evidence,” Pierre replied, voice guarded. He shut the file before she could peer inside.

Elena’s gaze lingered on the closed folder, as if she could see through the cardboard to the confession beneath. “I’ll save you the trouble, you know. Every version of the case comes back to Bellini. Even Rasmus says so, and when has he ever wavered?”

Pierre shrugged, feigning indifference. “Sometimes the truth is not in the main story, but the footnotes.”

“Sometimes,” Elena agreed, lips quirking. “But sometimes people get so obsessed with footnotes, they forget how the main story ends.”

She slipped out before he could answer. Pierre remained in the hush, the silence thickening until only the faint buzz of the overhead light and the far-off clatter of a cleaning cart reminded him the world still spun.

He returned to the testimony, noting the curious absence of any mention of Bellini’s emotional state. Each record, each interview, every cross-examination — all were bereft of speculation, as if Sofia herself had personally redacted any stray note of doubt or vulnerability. As if only her certainty could exist in the archives, filling the spaces where questions might grow roots.

He scrolled through the digital backup, eyes snagging on a cross-referenced note within Rasmus’s official summary. A single line, bland but damning:

“Chief Adjudicator Rasmus: I had, and retain, total trust in the record as it stands. Sofia Bellini alone authored the system’s core logic. There is, in my judgment, no margin for error or reinterpretation.”

His pen tapped the recorder again, faster now, the beat matching the thrum of unease in his chest. Why was there no margin for error? What bureaucrat, what judge, would ever claim such perfect certainty in a system’s history — unless uncertainty was forbidden by more than just policy?

He flicked through the yellowed transcripts from the early tribunal hearings, searching for a thread to tug. A dated page — May 7, three days after the summit tragedy — spilled old voices onto the table.

Pierre’s gaze snagged on a line attributed to Elias van Doren, one of the system’s original programmers:

“She was impossible in her steadiness. Bellini, I mean. No matter what we threw at her, it never rattled her. Sometimes I wondered if she felt anything at all — or if something in her just… refused to be moved.”

Pierre frowned, tracing the words with a fingertip. Impossible steadiness. The phrase stuck, unsettling. Was that awe? Affection? Fear? There was no follow-up, just the next witness, the next box ticked. He jotted a note on the back of his hand, not trusting himself to forget.

He replayed the scene in his mind, remembering Elias at the back of the tribunal chamber, gaze never meeting Sofia’s. Was it professional jealousy, or something deeper? Had something happened between them, some unacknowledged bond, now ground away beneath the record’s certainty?

The tape recorder clicked. He rewound, listening to Magda’s interview again — to her evasions, her careful refusal to name what she might remember. He heard the silence where her words should have been: not a denial of Sofia’s guilt, but a reluctance to color outside the lines.

He scrawled a question on his pad: Who benefits from the echo of certainty?

The clock above the door ticked past midnight. Pierre’s eyelids ached, but he pressed on, reviewing the overlapping statements, hunting for contradicting dates, stray margins, any sign the past might be less firm than the tribunal would have him believe.

He returned to the marginalia in red ink. Who wrote it? No one at the tribunal was so careless with color — red was reserved for rejections, for reversal orders, never for simple annotation. The handwriting was sharp, almost impatient, unlike the looping signatures of the administrators.

A chill ran down his neck. He recalled Magda’s veiled look, her insistence that the “record is what it is.” Was she warning him? Or was this something else — a sign left by someone who doubted the story, but could not say so outright?

His phone vibrated on the tabletop, breaking the tension. Pierre unlocked the screen, expecting another terse briefing from the chief archivist. Instead, a new message flickered onto the display, number withheld. The body held just one line: 

Don’t trust every record. Not all evidence is born in the light. 

No signature. No sender. Only a blurred attachment — the header from the Bellini Addendum, the same as the one in his hand, but bearing a date from two days before Sofia was even arrested. Impossible.

Pierre’s breath caught, heart pounding. He stared at the screen, then at the folder, then back, hands trembling just enough for the tape recorder to rattle on the tabletop.

Someone else was watching the same margins. Someone who knew the dates did not add up.

Outside, the silence pressed in, thicker than ever. Pierre scrolled through all the contacts he trusted, but none fit the sender. Was it Magda? Rasmus? Someone deeper in the system? Or someone like Sofia herself, refusing — even now — to let the story close cleanly?

He didn’t know. But he would find out.

And as he reached for the phone again, another message appeared — just as anonymous, just as terse:

If you want the truth, start with what’s missing. Before they replace it.

Pierre’s world narrowed to the glow of the screen and the taste of panic rising in his mouth.

He had always thought the record was the end of the story.

But tonight, it felt more like the beginning.
