# Chapter 1: The Survivor’s Record

"Your testimony is all that remains. Why do you think you survived, Sofia Bellini?"

The voice was mechanized through the tribunal’s static, yet the question hung between them with unsettling intimacy, as if it had pressed itself into the hush between breaths. Sofia’s eyes drifted to the polished table where she sat—alone under the white blaze of the chamber lights—before she allowed the gracious ghost of a smile.

She had been expecting the question. The tribunal always began this way: with an invitation to confess, to explain the impossible calculus of survival, as if martyrdom or guilt must be hiding inside the word “witness.” The faint, persistent hum from the recording device amplified the weight behind every silence; each word she spoke would be etched somewhere permanent, the official truth.

She folded her hands, fingertips aligning with mathematical care, and met the sea of watching faces. Three adjudicators, faceless in their dignity. Tomasz Havel, hunched and note-taking. Magda Lorenz behind her sensible spectacles, already bracing with silent encouragement. And on the far right bench, Pierre Leclerc—the journalist with his ink-stained fingers and wary, telescoping gaze.

She exhaled in a soft, measured rhythm that only she noticed.

"I’m not sure anyone can truly answer that, Chief Adjudicator," Sofia replied, her tone honey-smooth, unhurried. "But if my presence here helps the tribunal find peace for those we lost, then I accept it. I only remember doing what anyone would have—I found the fire exit and tried to help others." Pause. "I wish I could have done more."

A pen tapped quietly. The chief adjudicator’s transcript assistant—gray-haired, severe—clicked keys, but Sofia knew not everything typed was exactly what was spoken. That was the system’s secret: the record shaped itself to the needs of procedure. She tilted her voice warmer, smoothing the edges of possible accusation.

"I see," the adjudicator said, voice echoing from the panel. "And your memory of the events remains unchanged? You continue to state that you saw no cause for alarm before the incident?"

She met their question with readiness. "No cause for alarm that I could see. The summit was meant to be a chance for reconciliation. We were unguarded, even hopeful."

From the gallery, Pierre Leclerc shifted in his seat. His gaze flicked to the digital clock set above the chamber door, then back to Sofia with feline patience. He had that particular brand of skepticism journalists wore, the kind that wrote questions across their faces before their notepads. His thumb traced the corner of a folder, but he made no sound.

Sofia let her eyes rest for a heartbeat on Magda Lorenz, who nodded so minutely it could have been a tremor. Her mentor’s trust was a thread—thin but unbroken. Sofia’s own trust in the tribunal was a performance, but that performance was all that stood between her and the weight of suspicion pressing from every direction.

The adjudicator consulted his file. Light shone off the pages, making the black text glimmer. "The system logs indicate you attempted to lead survivors to safety. Is this accurate, Miss Bellini?"

Sofia nodded, the humility almost too polished. "I tried to keep everyone calm. Panic is the greatest danger in chaos. It’s easy to forget the power words have when people are frightened." A small gesture, palms open. "Sometimes, reframing the moment can bring relief. Even hope."

There: a seed planted. She watched as the words were swallowed by the machine, the transcript assistant typing, the persistent hum of the recorder intensifying. Every statement was a brick in the version of events that would matter—one she shaped with patience and a surgeon’s precision.

Magda’s eyes darted to the red binder beside her elbow, as if she wished she could pass it to Sofia. But this was not a time for intervention.

"Let the record show," intoned the assistant, "that Miss Bellini claims to have acted in a spirit of reassurance, with no prior suspicion of threat."

The echo of that judgment—the subtle finality of the system’s version—made Sofia’s pulse flicker once, no more. She wondered who had programmed such specific scripts of mercy, but that was not a thought for this room.

"Thank you, Miss Bellini," said Chief Adjudicator Rasmus, voice gentler now. "We understand how much this burdens you. You were the youngest among the delegates, and yet you alone survived." A weighted silence. "Do you attribute your survival to chance, or do you believe you were meant to shoulder this responsibility?"

A carefully constructed trap, but Sofia smiled with practiced vulnerability. "I don’t know if anyone is meant for something so heavy. I can only promise to be honest, and to remember those we lost as best I can."

Pierre Leclerc scribbled something—his movements precise, deliberate. Tomasz Havel glanced at him, brows knitting. For an instant, the tension arched between them, a silent crackle of challenge.

Sofia’s gaze returned to the adjudicators. The gentle hum of the device seemed almost hungry, consuming her words into permanent record. If the transcript twisted her language, no one here would notice until it was too late; after all, the system was perfect.

The chief adjudicator’s gavel clicked softly. "We will proceed to the incident timeline, Miss Bellini. Please recount, in your own words, what happened when the alarms sounded."

Here was the choreography. Sofia began, slow and vivid, sketching the moments before reality unraveled: the taste of over-steeped tea, the laughter of diplomats who imagined safety. She described the alarms as distant, muffled, almost polite—a warning that did not feel urgent until it was too late.

"I remember thinking it was a drill," she said. "People hesitated. It’s difficult to move groups when not everyone is convinced of danger. I tried to reassure them, to frame the alarms as an opportunity to practice safety, not as a threat."

She heard herself shaping the narrative. Her memory was precise, but her retelling was careful—each word a sedative. No panic, no accusation. Only benevolent guidance.

Pierre’s pen halted. His eyes narrowed just enough for Sofia to feel the question forming: if she had seen so little, why was she the only one to survive?

Magda cleared her throat. "Miss Bellini has always been attentive to the well-being of others," she interjected, soft but firm. "She was recognized for her composure during prior emergencies."

The chief adjudicator acknowledged, "Yes, that is on record. Still, these deaths are without precedent. The system registered multiple transcript irregularities during the summit. Did you notice any unusual changes to the language or behavior of those around you?"

A subtle shift. Sofia caught scent of doubt, masked as procedure.

"I recall some confusion," she said. "People repeating themselves, or pausing as if searching for words that wouldn’t come. But I thought it was fear. Fear makes us forget how to speak."

"Do you believe the system contributed to the confusion?" Tomasz Havel’s voice was gentler than his question. He was not asking whether the system was flawed—just whether fear, reframed, could explain the deaths. The system could not be suspect, not openly.

Sofia shook her head, serene. "No machine can erase the human heart. I think those final moments were shaped by emotion, not error."

It was an answer impossible to challenge—too tender to provoke, too vague to incriminate.

"Let the record show: Miss Bellini identifies fear as the dominant factor in survivor behavior," the transcript assistant pronounced, as if this had been a formal diagnosis.

Pierre tapped his folder, eyes hooded. He did not look away from Sofia. She wondered whether he’d found something—a thread, a fracture in the official story. But she only returned his gaze with open calm, the performance flawless.

"In accordance with Tribunal Protocol F," Chief Adjudicator Rasmus said, "we must note: Sofia Bellini is the last surviving witness of the summit." His words were sonorous, as if spoken through cathedral stone. "Her cooperation is crucial to our understanding of the tragedy."

A ripple of discomfort ran through the chamber. The designation was both shield and blade. Survivor, yes—protected. But also the last—alone, singular, suspect.

"One final question for today," Rasmus continued, glancing at his assistant: "Did you, at any time prior to the summit, communicate with Nadia Petrescu?"

Sofia’s heart did not stutter. Her answer was delivered with a gentle melancholy, devoid of secrets. "No. I only know of Nadia Petrescu by reputation and through the tribunal’s files, like everyone else. Her legacy is tragic, and I am sorry for how she is remembered."

Magda’s grip tightened on her pen, but she said nothing. The transcript assistant typed, keys clicking—a sound more judgmental than the spoken words.

Pierre’s attention sharpened. He wrote two words on the corner of his pad and tore it slowly, folding the slip once, twice, as though considering a move on a chessboard.

The chief adjudicator stood. "The tribunal will adjourn until tomorrow. Thank you, Miss Bellini. Your courage is noted."

Sofia rose, hands composed in her lap. She looked every inch the survivor: wounded but whole, keen to heal a world she did not trust to remember truly.

The hum from the recording device grew slightly louder as the panel filed out. Magda rose to join her, Tomasz gathering his documents with a harried efficiency. The chamber emptied, leaving Sofia under the single white light—spotlight, interrogation, or benediction, she could not say.

As she stooped to gather her bag, Pierre Leclerc lingered at the edge of her vision. He did not approach, but as he passed, his hand darted below the table’s surface and released something—an origami crease of paper, pale against the lacquered wood.

No one else noticed. Magda was already shepherding Sofia toward the side exit, voicing some gentle platitude about rest and breathing space. But Sofia’s eyes snagged on the slip beneath her fingertips.

Meet me—about what really happened.

The hum seemed to sharpen, the light growing colder. She pressed the note flat, the message as delicate and loaded as a detonator, and slipped it into her sleeve before anyone else could see.

Sofia moved for the door. Pierre Leclerc was already gone, trailed by suspicion and the scent of ink and new paper. But Sofia’s calm remained.

She opened the door to the corridor. Somewhere down the marble hall, the future waited—hungry for her words, for all her silences.

And now, someone else wanted the truth.
