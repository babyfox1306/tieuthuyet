# Chapter 8: The Secretarial Trail

The file number was in the public index. Iris Kane gave it to the clerk. The clerk typed, frowned, and said, "This record has been moved to the inquiry's sealed protocol. You'll need to submit a request to the Commissioner."

Iris did not move. "The contract authorization for the Kessler Line load-test schedule was entered into the commission archive three weeks ago. How is it sealed already?"

The clerk's badge had a small scratch that looked like the number '63' if you tilted your head—the same as the death toll. She adjusted her glasses and did not look at the screen. "The protocol was established by administrative directive last Tuesday. All documents related to the procurement phase are reclassified under Section 4.3 of the inquiry terms."

"Section 4.3 excludes administrative discretion from review," Iris said. "It doesn't remove records from the public index."

"The directive expands the scope." The clerk's voice was careful, practiced. "Any document that originated in a pre-inquiry administrative context is considered internal to the commission's deliberative process. You can still access it. You just need the Commissioner's signature."

Iris let her thumbnail press into the edge of the counter. The laminate was smooth, no cracks. "How long does that take?"

"Typically three to five business days for submission, then the Commissioner's office reviews the request. If approved, the document is released to your secure terminal. You may be required to view it under supervision."

"And if it's not approved?"

The clerk blinked. "Then the document remains sealed."

Irs felt the shape of the floor under her feet. Someone had moved the records before she knew to look for them. That meant someone knew she would look. "When was the directive effective?"

"Last Tuesday."

"Who initiated it?"

"Commissioner Renn's office," the clerk said. "It's standard procedure for documents that could compromise the impartiality of the ongoing investigation."

Iris smiled without warmth. "By preventing the investigator from seeing them."

The clerk said nothing.

She left the secretariat without signing the request form. The lobby was half-empty, a security guard leaning against the far wall, a pair of administrative assistants crossing to the elevators. Outside, the street was damp from a morning rain she had not noticed. She stood on the steps and pulled her phone.

Theo Vance answered on the second ring.

"I need to talk to you about the contract authorization file for the load-test schedule," she said. "It's been sealed under a directive from the Commissioner's office."

A pause. "Where are you?"

"Outside the secretariat building."

"Don't talk about this on the phone. Come to my office."

She hung up and walked the five blocks to the commission annex. The black sedan appeared two blocks behind her, the same unmarked model she had seen outside the lab yesterday. It kept a steady distance, matching her speed. She did not look back, but she counted the seconds before its engine note changed at the intersection. The driver was careful. Trained.

Vance's office was on the fourth floor, a corner room with a view of the river. He was standing when she entered, phone in hand, tie loosened. He set the phone face-down on the desk and closed the door behind her.

"You're being followed," he said.

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because the fast-track approval file was sealed before I could request it. And you're the only person inside the commission who's been telling me to follow the evidence."

Vance ran his hand through his hair. It was an habitual gesture, but today it looked strained. "Iris, I wasn't aware of the directive until this morning. Renn's office moves fast when they want something buried."

"The file number was public three weeks ago. Now it's gone. Who knew I would look for it?"

Everyone, she thought. The lab technician. The clerk. The man in the sedan.

Vance's jaw tightened. "I don't know. But you need to be careful. The more you push at this, the more resistance you'll meet."

"Resistance isn't evidence."

"It's a sign that you're close to something."

She took a step closer. "Then tell me what it is. You know more than you've said. You fed me the Marek signature discrepancy. You knew where to look. But when I ask about the trail that leads past Marek, you deflect."

Vance's eyes held hers. For a moment she saw the calculation behind them. "I've given you what I can. If I give you more, I'm exposed too."

"You're already exposed. You're the one who told me about the contract authorization. That came from your office."

He looked away. "Yes."

"Who told you to tell me?"

He didn't answer.

Iris studied the desk. A stack of folders, a pen in a ceramic cup, the phone face-down. "The man from your office called the lab to ask about my appointment. That was you, wasn't it?"

"I didn't call," he said. "But I knew you were taking the sample."

"So you had someone watching me."

"Protecting you."

"From what?"

Vance sat down heavily. "From the people who don't want you to find the rest."

She stood in the silence of the room, the river gray through the window. The black sedan was still out there. The seal was tightening.

She turned and left without another word.

The corridor was empty, the elevator doors open. She stepped inside and pressed the ground floor, her mind moving through the grid of evidence. The contract authorization was the key to the next tier. Someone had signed off on the load-test schedule. Whoever signed was the person who would have known if the test was tampered with. The seal was a wall, but walls had gaps.

She thought about the banks. Marek had kept a safe-deposit box. The bank had cameras.

The elevator stopped and she walked out into the lobby, past the security desk. The black sedan was parked across the street. She ignored it and turned toward the Metro entrance.

The bank was three stops north, a low concrete structure wedged between a pharmacy and a parking garage. The branch manager recognized her name and refused her request before she finished the sentence.

"Bank records are not subject to the inquiry's request," he said. "We require a court order."

"I'm not asking for records. I'm asking for CCTV footage from the day Marek's box was accessed."

"The same policy applies."

"The records have already been viewed by someone else. I'm trying to identify who."

The manager's face was still. "I cannot disclose that information."

Iris leaned on the counter, her thumbnail dragging along the edge. "Someone visited his box before I could. The vault log shows a time stamp. The cameras confirm identity."

"Then you have what you need."

"I don't have the footage. You do."

The manager stepped back. "I'm sorry. I can't help you."

She left the bank and crossed the street, feeling the weight of the dead end. The black sedan was not visible here, but she assumed it was out there somewhere, logging her route. She looked up at the bank's facade, the small camera above the entrance, the tinted windows.

She turned to walk back toward the station, but the teller who had processed Marek's box earlier was standing at the bus stop, a cloth bag over one shoulder.

"Ms. Kane," he said quietly.

She stopped.

He did not look at her. He pulled a folded piece of paper from his jacket pocket and pressed it into her hand, then stepped onto the bus without another word.

She did not open the note on the street. She entered the first building she found—an insurance office with a narrow stairwell—and climbed halfway to the first landing. The walls were white, the air still.

She unfolded the paper.

Call 555-0199 at 1800. Ask for A. Watters.

She looked at her watch. 5:47 PM. Thirteen minutes.

The stairs were silent. Her phone screen showed no bars underground, but she would not use it anyway. The inquiry had her office line tapped. Her mobile was registered with the commission. The sedan outside would log her location.

She looked at the pay phone on the corner, two blocks down, at the edge of the sight line.

Thirteen minutes to decide how far she was willing to step outside the rules.

She held the note flat against her palm, the paper warm from the teller's hand. The question was not whether she would call. The question was whether the sedan would follow her to the corner, or whether the man named Watters would answer and give her the name she needed to break the seal.

She looked at the stairwell door. Sunlight cut a long yellow rectangle across the concrete.

5:48. Twelve minutes.

The rules were a wall. But walls had doors. And someone had just handed her a key.
