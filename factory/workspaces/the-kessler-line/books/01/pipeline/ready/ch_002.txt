# Chapter 2: The Supporting Cast

Theo Vance met her in the lobby of the St. Katherine Building. "Commissioner Renn is ready for you. He likes to meet the experts before the first session."

Iris Kane looked past him at the brass directory, then at the security desk, where three clerks were pretending not to listen.

"Experts," she said. "Plural?"

"That is usually how commissions justify their expense."

His tone was mild. His suit was dark, his tie correctly knotted, and his left hand kept drifting toward his hair without quite touching it. An interrupted gesture. Anxiety stopped halfway to becoming visible.

Iris closed the folder tucked beneath her arm. "You are Theo Vance."

"Technical advisor."

"That was not the question."

"It was the answer they gave me."

He offered a hand. Iris took it because refusing would have created paperwork before nine in the morning. His grip was firm, brief, and professionally warm. The kind of handshake designed to reassure a witness and leave no evidence.

"Commissioner Renn has given us forty minutes before his next appointment," Theo said. "He prefers to begin with the scope."

"People who prefer scope before evidence usually intend to control both."

A small pause touched his expression. It vanished quickly.

"Then you will fit in well."

The St. Katherine Building had been renovated recently, but its old bones remained visible in the lobby: pale stone columns, iron ventilation grilles, and a long reception counter polished so often that its edges had softened. Iris ran her thumbnail along the counter as she followed Theo toward the security gates. The surface was smooth. Too smooth near the corner. A shallow hairline ran beneath the lacquer.

New finish over an old fracture.

The thought was uninvited and therefore useful.

Theo scanned them through the first gate. At the second, he checked his phone.

"Expecting an emergency?" Iris asked.

"Expecting instructions."

"From the commission?"

"From several people."

He put the phone away before she could see the screen. That was not suspicious by itself. Everyone in a government building had at least three people who believed their message deserved immediate attention. But Theo checked the device again before the elevator doors opened, and this time he did not pretend it was accidental.

The elevator carried them to the upper administrative floor. No windows. No floor indicator beyond a thin white line moving upward.

"How long have you advised the commission?" Iris asked.

"Since the appointment was announced."

"That is not a duration."

"Three days."

"Better."

"You always interrogate the person escorting you?"

"Only when he volunteers the wrong answers."

Theo looked at her then. His eyes were gray, attentive, and more tired than his face permitted. "You were appointed because you do not accept convenient answers."

"That is what the appointment letter says."

"And what do you think it means?"

"Someone needed a name that could be placed beside the phrase independent review."

The elevator opened before he replied.

Commissioner Aldous Renn occupied an office with no personal objects except a framed architectural rendering of the Kessler Line before the collapse. The rendering showed the tunnel as a clean black curve beneath a district of white blocks. No cracks. No dust. No bodies. Architecture had an admirable talent for keeping the consequences out of frame.

Renn stood behind his desk. He was in his late fifties, silver-haired, spare, and composed without appearing stiff. He wore no visible jewelry, and the only unusual detail was a small stack of sharpened pencils arranged parallel to the edge of his blotter. Each one had been cut to exactly the same length.

"Ms. Kane," he said. "Thank you for coming."

"Commissioner."

"Theo tells me you have already challenged the wording of our mandate."

"The wording challenged itself."

Renn smiled with practiced patience. "Good. We need skepticism. We do not need theatrics."

"I have no use for theatrics."

"That is one of the reasons your appointment was supported."

He indicated the chair opposite his desk. Iris sat. Theo remained near the door, holding a thin tablet against his side. Renn did not ask him to join them.

"The Kessler Line collapse has created a serious public wound," Renn said. "The commission's responsibility is to establish the facts and provide a conclusion that can be acted upon."

"Which conclusion?"

"The accurate one."

"That narrows it less than you think."

His mouth tightened by a fraction. "The official cause currently recorded is seismic microfracture compounded by dispatch error."

Iris watched his face when he said it. Nothing moved except one finger, which tapped once against the desk and stopped.

"Currently recorded," she repeated.

"Until the commission has reviewed the evidence."

"Then the record is provisional."

"All records are provisional before an inquiry."

"Some are simply premature."

Theo shifted near the door. Renn's gaze moved to him briefly, then returned to Iris.

"The families need clarity," Renn said. "The transport authority needs a safe basis for continued operations. The market needs reassurance. And the public needs to see that accountability is possible without turning the inquiry into a permanent political event."

"How swift would you like the conclusion to be?"

"Swift enough to be useful. Contained enough to remain credible."

There it was. Not pressure exactly. Pressure with its jacket buttoned.

Iris leaned back. "Contained is a strange requirement for a factual process."

"It is a practical one. A commission that expands without discipline becomes a second disaster. We will not examine every procurement decision made in the last decade because a tunnel failed."

"Only the decisions connected to the tunnel."

"Naturally."

"Who decides what is connected?"

"I appointed you to help decide that."

He said it smoothly, almost generously. Iris knew generosity when she saw it. This was not generosity. It was a responsibility wrapped in flattering language, with a signature line waiting at the bottom.

Renn opened a folder and slid a schedule toward her. The first session was set for the following morning. Preliminary interviews followed. Site access appeared on a later page, listed as pending authorization.

"You have not arranged a site visit," Iris said.

"The tunnel is currently restricted."

"Restricted by whom?"

"By safety officials."

"Names?"

"We will provide them."

"Before or after the first session?"

Renn's smile disappeared. "Ms. Kane, I understand your standards. I am asking you to understand the conditions under which we are working. The Kessler Line must not become a theater for suspicion. We will proceed in order, and we will reach a conclusion."

"A swift, contained conclusion."

"An accurate conclusion," he corrected.

The correction was too quick. That made it more valuable.

When Iris stood, Renn did not offer his hand. "Mr. Vance will coordinate your technical access. Ms. Sol has agreed to present Vantor's internal timeline this afternoon."

"Vantor is presenting its own account."

"Vantor is a principal party."

"That is not the same as a reliable party."

"No principal party is reliable by default. That is why you are here."

The commissioner returned the schedule to its precise alignment with the blotter. "Try to keep the first week orderly, Ms. Kane."

"Orderly is not the same as clean."

"I am aware."

She left with Theo, feeling Renn's attention between her shoulder blades until the door closed.

The corridor outside was quieter than the lobby. Theo exhaled.

"He likes to establish the perimeter," he said.

"He established an outcome."

"That is a strong interpretation."

"Then give me a stronger one."

Theo checked his phone again. A message lit the screen. He read it, locked the device, and placed it face down in his palm.

"The commission wants to avoid speculation before the evidence is assembled."

"That is reasonable."

"You sound disappointed."

"I am disappointed when reasonable statements are used to carry unreasonable weight."

They walked toward a smaller conference room. Theo opened the door and switched on the lights. Inside were two chairs, a wall display, and a long table with a glass surface. A paper cup had been left upside down beside the waste bin. Someone had flattened it carefully before throwing it away.

"The technical records are incomplete," Theo said. "Some material is still being transferred from the transport authority."

"Transferred from where?"

"Multiple offices."

"That answer is beginning to develop a personality."

"I do not control the records."

"Do you control access to them?"

"Some access."

"How much?"

His hand went through his hair this time. He caught himself and lowered it. "Enough to keep you from wasting time on duplicate schedules and irrelevant maintenance logs."

"Who decides what is irrelevant?"

"The technical team."

"Which includes you."

"Among others."

Iris sat at the table but did not open her folder. "What do you know about the liner certification?"

"The collapse occurred after the liner was subjected to abnormal strain."

"That is not what I asked."

Theo met her eyes. His expression remained calm, but his thumb pressed against the edge of his phone.

"The certification passed internal review," he said.

"Internal review is not a physical property."

"No."

"Did the load test pass?"

"I am waiting for the original load-test data."

"Waiting from whom?"

"Records management."

"Records management is a room, not a person."

His jaw tightened. "I said I would locate it."

There were answers people gave when they did not know. There were answers people gave when they knew too much. Theo's belonged to the second category, though Iris could not yet tell whether the excess knowledge was technical, political, or merely professional self-preservation.

He pulled a chair out for her, then seemed to realize the gesture was unnecessary. It stopped before becoming intimate, which suited them both.

"Ms. Sol will have the company timeline," he said. "Start there."

"Because it is reliable?"

"Because it is organized."

"Organization is not reliability."

"No," he said. "But it makes discrepancies easier to find."

That was the first useful thing he had said.

Adrienne Sol arrived seven minutes late and made the delay look administrative rather than personal. She entered carrying a pristine corporate binder with black cloth covers and a silver Vantor seal. Her charcoal suit was immaculate. A small ink mark stained the side of her right index finger, as if she had pressed it against a wet signature and forgotten to wipe it clean.

"Ms. Kane," Sol said. "I hope Commissioner Renn made the commission's expectations clear."

"He made them concise."

"That is one of his strengths."

Theo stood. Sol glanced at him, then at the phone in his hand. The exchange lasted less than a second. It contained more information than most meetings.

Sol placed the binder on the table. "This is Vantor's formal timeline of the Kessler project, from procurement through the collapse."

"Formal according to whom?"

"According to Vantor's records."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer available today."

Iris opened the binder. The pages were clean, numbered, and cross-referenced. The timeline moved in straight lines: contract issued, materials approved, construction phases completed, inspections logged, dispatch protocols confirmed. Every date had a supporting reference code. Every reference code appeared to lead to another page.

The first page of the fast-track procurement approval letter had been stapled through a typo in the header. The word approval was missing a letter, and the replacement page had been aligned slightly higher than the pages beneath it. Whoever had changed it had been in a hurry, or had wanted the change to look like haste.

Iris touched the staple with one fingertip.

Sol noticed. "The document was corrected during compilation."

"After it was signed?"

"I would need to confirm the sequence."

"Then do not call it formal."

Sol's smile was thin. "You have a reputation for precision."

"Reputations are usually another word for prior observation."

Theo looked down at the table.

Iris turned another page. "This approval predates the finalized collapse timeline."

"The procurement process did."

"The approval letter predates it."

"Yes."

"Why was the procurement approval handled before the timeline was formalized?"

Sol folded her hands. "The fast-track procurement approval was handled above my pay grade before the collapse timeline was formalized."

The room went still.

Iris lifted her eyes. "Above your pay grade is not a chain of authority."

"It is a description of my access."

"Who had the authority?"

"I was not the approving officer."

"Who was?"

"I do not have that name in the material provided."

"Convenient."

"Accurate."

Sol said it without defensiveness. That was more interesting than an argument would have been.

"Did Vantor request the fast-track?" Iris asked.

"Vantor submitted the required materials."

"That is not what I asked."

Sol's ink-marked finger pressed once against the binder's cover. "Vantor sought approval for the contract to proceed on schedule."

"And the approval arrived before the collapse timeline was formalized."

"Yes."

"Who instructed your department to treat it as valid?"

"I would need to review internal routing."

"You brought a formal timeline without the routing."

"We brought the information relevant to the collapse."

"Relevant according to Vantor."

"Relevant according to the project record."

Iris looked at Theo. "Is the project record complete?"

His gaze flicked to Sol and back. "I have not finished checking it."

Sol collected the question before Theo could say more. "The collapse was caused by seismic microfracture compounded by dispatch error. Vantor's timeline reflects that the liner passed all scheduled checks before the event."

"Passed," Iris said. "Or was recorded as passed?"

"Those are equivalent in an operational record."

"No. One describes an event. The other describes an entry."

Sol's eyes cooled. "The commission requested documents. It did not request philosophy."

"Good. Philosophy is difficult to staple."

Theo's mouth almost moved. Almost. Sol did not react.

Iris continued through the pages. Maintenance had been logged at regular intervals. Dispatch communications were summarized rather than reproduced. A boxed note stated that no deviation from approved procedure had been identified before the collapse.

"Where are the original dispatch logs?" Iris asked.

"With the transport authority."

"Where is the load-test data?"

"The technical team is locating it."

"Who is the technical team?"

Theo answered this time. "I am coordinating the retrieval."

"Then retrieve it."

"I intend to."

Sol closed the binder with careful pressure. "You will find that Vantor has cooperated fully."

"Cooperation is not the same as completeness."

"Neither is suspicion the same as analysis."

"No," Iris said. "Analysis starts when the documents stop agreeing with their labels."

For the first time, Sol's composure showed a seam. Not fear. Irritation, quickly sealed.

The meeting ended with no agreement except that Iris would receive additional files by the next morning. Sol left first. Theo remained behind, staring at the closed binder.

"You knew about the approval," Iris said.

"I knew it existed."

"You knew it had been handled above Sol."

"I knew that was her position."

"Is it true?"

He checked his phone. The screen was blank.

"I do not know."

"That answer sounded rehearsed."

"Most accurate answers do, after someone has asked the same question repeatedly."

"Who has been asking?"

Theo slid the binder toward her. "You should read the Marek file before the first session."

The name appeared on the cover of a thinner folder that had been left beneath the binder. Dov Marek. Former site inspector. Certification oversight.

"Why?"

"Because he signed the liner certifications."

"That makes him relevant."

"It makes him the cleanest starting point."

"Cleanest?"

"His record is complete."

Iris studied him. "Complete records are usually either curated or boring."

"Then you can determine which."

He moved toward the door.

"Theo."

He stopped.

"If you are advising me, advise me. If you are managing my access, say so."

His shoulders held still, but his fingers tightened around the phone.

"I am trying to make sure you have enough material to work with."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the one I can give."

He left before she could decide whether to call him back.

Her temporary office was small, windowless, and furnished with a desk whose laminate had begun to peel along the front edge. A paper tray sat empty except for a single staple, bent into a narrow triangle. Iris set the folders down and ran her thumbnail along the desk's damaged edge.

The laminate lifted by less than a millimeter.

She opened the Marek file. Twenty-seven clean certifications. Four years. No failed load tests. "Perfect records are a lie disguised as discipline," she murmured.
