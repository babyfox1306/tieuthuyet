# Chapter 10: The Lily and the Letter

"I passed it on," Elena read aloud, the tintype trembling in her fingers. The words were light cursive on aged paper, but they carried the full weight of a curse breaking open in the morning stillness.

Julian stood in the kitchen doorway, coffee mug suspended halfway to his mouth. He didn't ask what it meant. He already knew. He had seen the fracture in her from the first moment she stepped onto the rotting porch.

She turned the photograph over. Her mother's face stared back—younger, softer, before the hard lines set in. Diane March at twenty-three, holding an infant in a christening gown. The baby's face was blurred, a smear of white in the black-and-white image.

*That's me.*

Diane had written on the back: *Elena. June. I passed it on.*

"She knew what the house was," Elena said. Her voice came out flat, scraped clean of emotion. "Cora told her. And she gave it to me anyway. She packaged it up with a deed and a letter of condolence and let me walk into it blind."

Julian set the mug down. It made a hollow sound against the wood. "You're not blind anymore."

"No. Now I'm just standing in the middle of it, holding the receipt." She set the tintype face down, the inscription against the table. "My mother split herself when she was fifteen. Cora wrote about it in the diary we found. Diane broke a mirror in the bathroom and the pieces never came back together. She walked out of that room missing something."

"And she gave it to you."

"She passed it on. Like it was a brooch. Like it was an inheritance she had no right to keep."

---

Julian cleared the attic after breakfast. Elena didn't help. She stood in the doorway and watched, arms crossed, the morning light turning the dust in the air into spinning constellations. He worked methodically, lifting collapsed boxes, brushing away cobwebs, testing the floorboards before putting his full weight on them.

"Cora was organized," he said, pulling a plain pine box from behind a stack of brittle curtains. "This is nailed shut, but there's a note taped to the lid."

"What does it say?"

He read it silently, then looked up. His face was unreadable, but his hand tightened on the box. "It says *For the one who comes after*."

He brought it down. They opened it together on the kitchen table, using a butter knife to pry the nails out of the soft pine. Inside were three journals, each bound in gray cloth with leather ties. The scent of lavender and old paper rose out like a breath held for decades.

Elena opened the first one. Cora's handwriting was delicate, careful, the lines straight and evenly spaced. A woman trying to impose order on chaos.

*The mirrors in this house are not for seeing. They are for keeping.*

She flipped to an entry dated forty years ago.

*Diane broke the third mirror today. I heard the crash from the kitchen, and when I got upstairs, she was standing in the bathroom with her hand bleeding, staring at a hundred versions of herself in the shattered pieces. She said she felt better. Lighter. Like she had finally let the right part of her out. I didn't understand then. I understand now. We don't heal. We split. And then we hand the cracks to our daughters.*

Elena closed the journal. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. She had expected the shaking, the cold, the familiar spiral. But something had settled in her after the lake. The girl in blue was no longer standing behind her in the glass. She was inside her, a quiet voice that remembered everything and refused to let her forget.

"Three generations," she said. "My grandmother. My mother. Me."

Julian was reading over her shoulder, his breath warm against her hair. He reached past her and turned to the last page of the third journal.

*If you are reading this, you are already whole. The lake takes its due, but it gives back what belongs to you. Do not be afraid of what you saw in the water. It was never a ghost. It was you, waiting to come home.*

*But warn the one who comes after. The mirror doesn't just hold the past. It holds the future. And Diane didn't just split herself. She let something out. Something that is still looking for a body to wear.*

---

Elena went to the bedroom alone. The trunk was still at the foot of the bed, the brass latch cool under her fingers. She lifted the lid.

Pip was there, lying on top of the folded quilts, his single button eye catching the light from the window. She picked him up. The fur was matted, the stitching loose. She had carried this bear through every move, every apartment, every failed attempt at starting over. He was the only thing she had kept from childhood.

The memory of Tomas rose in her chest. A boy's laughter. The smell of mud. The weight of water in her lungs.

She didn't push it away. She let it sit, heavy and cold, until it passed.

"I remember you," she whispered to the bear. "I remember him. And I'm not sorry I locked it away. I was too small to carry it alone."

The bear didn't answer. It was just cloth and stuffing and a button eye sewn on by Cora's hand.

She placed Pip in the trunk. His one eye caught the light, a glint of amber in the gloom. The memory it held was no longer poison. It was just a scar. Something she could live with.

She closed the lid.

---

When she came back to the kitchen, Julian was reading one of the journals at the table. His sleeves were rolled up, forearms streaked with dust from the attic. He looked up when she entered, and the expression in his eyes made her stop.

"Your grandmother," he said. "She knew what was coming. She tried to stop it."

Elena sat down across from him. "How?"

"She wrote a letter. To Diane. But she never sent it." He turned the journal around so she could see. "She says the third mirror is still walking. That Diane didn't just split herself—she made a door. And something came through."

"That's not possible."

"You said the same thing about the girl in the lake."

She looked down at the journal. The words were neat, deliberate, the ink slightly blurred as if Cora had been crying when she wrote them.

*I do not know if the woman I see in town is my daughter or her reflection. She wears Diane's face. She speaks in Diane's voice. But the eyes are wrong. They are too old. Too empty. I think Diane let a version of herself out that does not know how to feel. And I am afraid of what happens when it finds a mirror to crawl back through.*

Elena pushed the journal away. Her hands were shaking now.

"Find her," Cora had written. "Find her before she finds you."

---

Julian reached across the table. His hand covered hers, warm and solid, the calluses rough against the thin skin of her knuckles. She looked up. The morning light caught the silver in his hair, the deep lines at the corners of his eyes.

"Elena." His voice was low. "I'm not leaving."

She wanted to argue. Had the words ready—*This isn't your mess, I don't need a keeper, you have your own life.* But the memory of the lake was still too close, the water pressure in her ears, the cold hands of the child she used to be wrapping around her wrists. She didn't want to be alone.

He stood, the chair scraping against the floorboards. His hand slid from her knuckles to her jaw, tilting her face up to meet his gaze. His thumb traced the line of her cheekbone, a touch so gentle it almost undid her.

"Promise me," he said.

She swallowed. "You promise me first. That you won't run when you read what's in that journal."

Julian didn't waver. "I've seen ghosts and curses and women who disappear into their own heads. I'm not afraid of a book."

He leaned in, closing the space between them. His lips met hers with a soft pressure, warm and steady. It tasted like coffee and dust and the beginning of trust. A seal on a vow neither of them fully understood.

When he pulled back, his forehead rested against hers.

"Whatever's coming," he said, "we face it together."

---

She found the letter in Cora's writing desk, tucked into a secret drawer she had overlooked on the first day. The drawer was shallow, barely deep enough for a single sheet of paper. But inside was a letter folded into the shape of a water lily.

The petals were dry and fragile, the creases stained with old perfume. Lavender. The same scent that lingered in the lake house's empty closets, as if Cora herself had breathed the word onto the page.

Elena unfolded it carefully, the paper crackling under her fingers.

The letter was dated the month Cora died.

*My dear Elena,*

*If you are reading this, you have already done what I could not. You have faced the lake. You have brought the girl in blue back into the light. I am proud of you, even if I never told you while I was alive.*

*But I must tell you the truth about your mother, and it is not a gentle truth.*

*Diane did not simply inherit the March women's curse. She broke the third mirror when she was fifteen. She did not just split herself—she let a version of herself out into the world. A reflection that learned to walk on its own. I do not know what it wants. I do not know if it is still Diane, or if it has become something else entirely.*

*But I know it is hunting. It is looking for a new vessel. It is looking for you.*

*Your mother is not who she pretends to be. Find her before she finds you. The reflection can walk out on its own.*

Underneath the script, Cora had drawn a map to an address Elena had never seen. The streets were narrow, winding, laid out in ink so fine it seemed to have been drawn with a single hair. In the center of the map, marked with an X, was a name.

*The Sanctuary of Lost Mirrors.*

The letter trembled in her hands.

"Julian."

He was at her side in an instant, reading over her shoulder. His hand pressed against the small of her back, steadying her.

"It's not over," she whispered.

"I know."

She folded the lily back into its petals, tucking the map into the center. The lavender scent clung to her fingers, and it was no longer the smell of memory or comfort. It was the smell of a warning.

Outside the kitchen window, the lake was flat and gray under the morning sky. The surface was still. But beneath it, there were currents she had only begun to understand.

And somewhere out there—in a city she had never seen, in a room lined with broken mirrors—her mother was waiting.

Or something wearing her mother's face.
