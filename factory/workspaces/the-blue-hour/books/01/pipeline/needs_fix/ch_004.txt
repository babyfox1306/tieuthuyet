# Chapter 4: The Brass Inheritance

"You're still here," Julian said, his voice rough with sleep or worry, framed by the gray light of dawn flooding the ruined hallway.

Elena looked up from the armchair where she had spent the night clutching Pip to her chest. The bear's single glass eye caught the pale light, glinting like a swallowed star. She hadn't closed her eyes once. Every creak, every sigh of the old house had kept her wired, alert, waiting for the girl to step out of the shadows and finish whatever she had started.

"I'm still here," Elena said. Her voice came out cracked, dry. "Barely."

Julian crossed the distance in three long strides. He was still wearing yesterday's clothes, a dark flannel shirt wrinkled at the elbows, his boots unlaced. He crouched in front of her, his knee pressing into the worn floorboard, and his hands went straight to her shoulders. His fingers pressed into the fabric of her shirt, firm and searching, as if he expected to find a wound hidden beneath the cotton.

"Did you sleep?" he asked.

"No."

"Did you see anything?"

Elena hesitated. The wet handle. The humming floor. The weight of Pip in her hands after she had pried open the seam at the bear's neck and found the brass key nestled in the stuffing, cold and impossible. She had not told him about that. She had not told anyone.

"I heard things," she said. "The house talks at night. I think I'm getting used to it."

Julian's gaze dropped to the object she was clutching. His eyes narrowed. "What's that?"

She opened her palm. The brass key lay across her lifeline, tarnished and heavy. "I found it inside Pip. My aunt's bear. It was sewn into the stuffing."

Julian's jaw tightened. He didn't take the key, but his thumb brushed the edge of her palm, a fleeting touch that sent a current up her arm. "Do you know what it opens?"

"No. But I have a guess."

She had spent the dark hours examining the key, turning it over and over, rubbing the grain of the metal against her thumb until the ridges imprinted on her skin. The teeth were intricate, old-fashioned, the kind that belonged to a lock that hadn't been turned in decades. And the house had a thousand doors, but only one that felt heavy enough to deserve such a key—the iron-bound trunk in the attic.

Elena stood. Her legs were stiff, her back aching from the shallow armchair. Julian straightened beside her, his hand dropping from her shoulder, but his presence remained close. She could smell the dawn cold on his skin, the faint scent of coffee on his breath.

"Show me," he said.

She led him through the house, past the parlor where the mirrors still reflected only her, past the hallway where the handle was now dry and ordinary, past the stairs that groaned under their combined weight. The attic door was at the end of the second-floor corridor, a low wooden rectangle with a rusted latch. Elena pulled it open. The ladder descended automatically, its hinges screeching.

"After you," Julian said.

She climbed. The attic smelled of cedar and mouse droppings, of time compressed into dust. A single dormer window let in the gray morning light, illuminating the shape of the trunk in the corner. It was iron-bound, the metal bands studded with rusted rivets, the wood dark and warped with age. A brass plate on the front bore the initials C.M.—Cora March.

Elena knelt before it. Her hands trembled as she inserted the key into the lock. The metal was cold against her fingertips, but as she turned it, a warmth bloomed through the brass, a vibration like a struck tuning fork that hummed up her arm and into her chest. The lock clicked open.

She lifted the lid. The smell of mothballs and old paper rushed out.

Inside, the trunk held a collection of childhood artifacts: a worn stuffed rabbit with one ear missing, a handful of smooth river stones, a dried flower pressed between sheets of wax paper, and a leather-bound diary with a blue ribbon bookmark that had faded to the color of a stormy sky.

Elena reached for the diary. Her fingers brushed the cover, the leather soft and cracked.

Julian knelt beside her. "That's your aunt's?"

"It must be." But the handwriting on the cover's label—E. March—said otherwise. Her own name, written in a child's careful print.

She didn't open it yet. Instead, she lifted it out, and a cloud of dust rose from the trunk's depths. Elena inhaled sharply and coughed, the particles scratching her throat.

Before she could catch her breath, Julian moved. His chest pressed against her back, one arm curving around her waist to steady her, the other hand bracing against the trunk's edge. He was warm. Solid. The contact was brief but deliberate, his breath stirring the hair at her temple.

"Easy," he murmured.

Elena's cough subsided, but she didn't pull away. For a moment, she let herself lean into the heat of him, let the trembling in her hands still against his steadiness. His thumb traced a small circle at her hip, a gesture that could have been accidental or could have been a question.

Then he stepped back, clearing his throat. "Sorry. Didn't mean to crowd you."

"It's fine." Her voice was thinner than she wanted.

She opened the diary.

The first page was filled with a child's handwriting, looping and uneven, the letters pressed so hard into the paper that they had left grooves on the page beneath. The same sentence, repeated again and again, each line slightly more frantic than the last.

I made the water take him.

Elena's blood turned to ice. The words blurred, then sharpened, and she could feel the room tilt around her. Julian was saying something, his hand on her arm, but his voice came from far away.

I made the water take him. I made the water take him. I made the water take him.

The handwriting spiraled down the page, crowding into the margins, climbing over itself until the final line was nothing but a jagged scratch, a wound in the paper.

Elena's grip tightened on the diary. The brass key, still warm, pressed against her palm like a second heartbeat.
