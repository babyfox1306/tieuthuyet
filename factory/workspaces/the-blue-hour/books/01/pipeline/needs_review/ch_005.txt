# Chapter 5: Entries in Ash

"It wasn't a ghost," Elena read aloud, her voice trembling in the silent room; "it was a punishment we agreed to."

The words seemed to leave the diary and hang above the opened trunk like smoke. Julian stopped moving behind her. Even the floorboards went still, as if the house had leaned in to hear what Cora March had written in that rust-brown ink.

Elena shut her mouth hard enough to click her teeth.

"Read it again," Julian said.

"No."

His throat cleared softly. "Elena."

"What part of that sentence made you think repetition would improve it?"

He did not answer immediately, which was one of Julian Vane's more irritating talents. He had a way of making silence feel like an argument he was winning. Across the attic room, morning light pressed through the dusty dormer window in a pale rectangle, catching motes in the air and making them look almost intentional.

Elena kept one hand on the diary and the other against the lid of the old trunk. Her thumb rubbed over the splintered grain automatically. The wood was dry, rough, honest. Wood did not ask why a grown woman wanted to throw a dead aunt's diary across the attic and then set the house on fire for good measure.

Julian stepped closer, but not too close. He had learned that much already. Or he was simply the kind of man who gave a cornered animal room to choose the direction of its bite.

"That line matters," he said.

"Congratulations to the line."

"Who is 'we'?"

Elena looked down at the page. The diary's paper had gone soft at the edges, swollen from years of damp. Cora's handwriting slanted sharply, impatient and elegant, the strokes too dark to be ordinary ink. Elena rubbed the side of one letter with the pad of her finger before she could stop herself.

A rusty smell lifted from the page.

Iron.

Her stomach clenched.

Julian noticed. Of course he noticed. His gaze dropped to her hand, then the diary, then her face. He was too careful to ask the obvious question. That made it worse.

"It smells like blood," Elena said before he could be noble about it.

His jaw tightened. "Old iron gall ink can smell metallic."

"Naturally. Let's all be soothed by stationery trivia."

"Cora kept records. She labeled cupboards, jars, fuse boxes, storm shutters. She would have chosen what lasted."

"She also apparently wrote cryptic punishments in a diary and hid it in an attic trunk that required a brass key sewn into a bear's ear." Elena glanced at the torn toy on the table beside the trunk. Pip sat slumped against a stack of folded linens, one ear split open like a wound. "So perhaps Cora's filing system had range."

Julian's mouth almost moved. Not a smile. Something too restrained to qualify. "Keep reading."

"No."

"Then give it to me."

Her fingers tightened on the page. "Absolutely not."

"You can't refuse to read and refuse to let anyone else read."

"Watch me explore new levels of inconsistency."

His pocket watch came out of his waistcoat with a small silver flash. He thumbed it open, checked it, snapped it shut. The sound was neat and final in the stale attic air.

Elena eyed him. "Does it say run screaming yet?"

"It says we have hours before dusk."

"That was not an answer."

"It was the relevant fact."

He tucked the watch away, but his fingers stayed near the chain. The morning had made him look less severe than the night had. Less like a warning carved into a man and more like a tired human being with a stubborn mouth and eyes that kept finding too much. His dark hair was damp at the temples from the climb to the attic, his shirt sleeves rolled to the forearm, dust clinging to the fine hairs along his skin.

Elena noticed all of that. Unfortunately. Her brain had poor timing and no dignity.

She dragged her attention back to the diary.

The next line waited.

Punishments only work if everyone remembers what they are for. But Elena does not remember. Diane says that is mercy. Cora says mercy is just another door locked from the outside.

Elena's scalp prickled.

"Diane," she said.

Julian went very still. "Your mother?"

"Sadly, yes. Unless there is a second Diane March wandering around ruining childhoods as a hobby."

"You said you and your mother are estranged."

"I said nothing of substance. That is my preferred family tradition."

Julian accepted the correction with a small nod. "Do you want to stop?"

Elena laughed once. It sounded wrong in the attic, too sharp and too high. "Do people stop halfway through removing a knife?"

"Some do if it is keeping pressure on the wound."

"Very practical. Horrifying, but practical."

She turned the page before courage could evaporate. The paper rasped under her fingers. Something blackened the bottom corner, not burned clean through, but smudged as if the diary had once been too near ash. Her thumb came away gray.

Cora had written the next entry with more pressure. The brown letters dug into the page.

July 18.

The children returned to the reeds today though Diane forbade it. She forbids softly, which is the same as permitting. Tomas led the way with his chin up and his pockets full of stolen sugar cubes for bravery. Elena carried the blue ribbon she refuses to wear and dragged the bear by one paw until its fur was green with weeds.

Elena stopped breathing for three seconds.

A blue ribbon.

Not a memory. Not exactly. More like a splinter under the skin, too deep to pull, too shallow to ignore.

She saw, for less than a blink, a flash of blue fabric caught on a nail. Small fingers. Mud under nails. The sour smell of lake plants crushed beneath bare feet.

Then it vanished.

Her hand found the edge of the trunk. Thumb to grain. Back and forth. Back and forth.

Julian's voice lowered. "What is it?"

"Nothing."

"That was not nothing."

"Fine. It was a terrible interior decorating choice from my childhood."

"Elena."

She hated the way he said her name, like it deserved careful handling. People said her name sharply or with pity or not at all. Julian made it sound like a door he would knock on but never kick open.

"I saw a ribbon," she said. "For a second. Blue. That's all."

"Did you remember where?"

"The lake, probably. The moon, possibly. My brain has become avant-garde."

His hand shifted, as if he wanted to reach for her and stopped himself. Good. Sensible. If he touched her right now, Elena had no idea whether she would flinch or lean in, and both options were unacceptable.

She read on.

They have made a game of holding breath under the dock. Diane says all children play foolish games. I told her foolish games do not make the glass frost over in July.

Julian drew in a breath through his nose.

Elena stared at the words until the lines doubled. Holding breath. Under the dock.

No.

That was the word that rose inside her, huge and immediate. No, not as denial. Denial had finesse. This was something older, blunt and animal.

No.

She could feel cold wood beneath her palms. Could hear water slapping against pilings. Could taste lake silt at the back of her throat.

Then the attic was simply the attic again, dust and trunk and diary and Julian standing too near the slope of the roof.

Elena swallowed. "It says they played a game."

"Do you remember it?"

"No."

"Elena."

"No," she snapped, and the word cracked louder than she meant it to. Pip tipped slightly against the linens, as if the sound had nudged him.

Julian did not retreat. He did not push, either. He only nodded, once, as if marking a boundary on a map.

"All right."

She hated that, too. The respect. It left her with nowhere to aim the anger.

The attic door creaked downstairs.

Both of them froze.

Not in the attic. Below them.

A slow creak. Then another.

Elena looked toward the narrow stairwell. "Tell me that was the house settling."

"The house does not usually settle by opening doors."

"Your devotion to accuracy is becoming a personal attack."

Julian moved to the stairs first. He descended three steps and listened. Elena stayed by the trunk because staying by the cursed diary and mutilated bear apparently counted as the less terrifying option.

From below came a faint knock.

One. Two. Three.

Not loud. Not urgent. Polite, almost.

Julian's shoulders tightened. "The parlor."

"How do you know?"

"Sound carries through the chimney."

Another knock.

Elena closed the diary.

The attic exhaled.

Every sheet draped over the stored furniture fluttered at once, though no window was open. White cloth lifted and settled over chair backs, over stacked frames, over a tall wardrobe with one cracked mirror facing the wall.

Elena's gaze snagged on that mirror.

The glass was turned away. She could see only its dull edge, a thin silver wound between the wardrobe and the cloth. Still, every nerve in her body knew better than to look.

If she did not look, it was not there.

Old logic. Useless logic. The kind of logic children invented and adults kept like rotten teeth.

Julian climbed back up the three steps. "We take the diary downstairs. More light. Easier exit."

"Has anyone ever mentioned that your idea of reassurance needs renovation?"

"Often."

"By women fleeing rooms with you?"

"Mostly by contractors."

Despite herself, Elena almost smiled. It died quickly, but he saw it. His face softened in a way that made her chest feel uncomfortably full.

She gathered the diary, Pip, and the brass key. The key was smaller than she remembered from the night before, cold and innocent in her palm. It had been hidden inside a toy she apparently dragged through weeds as a child. Very normal. Entirely healthy. Nothing to discuss with a professional and a large glass of whiskey.

Downstairs, the house seemed brighter and worse.

Morning light filled the hall, but the brightness did not clean it. It only revealed more. Warped floorboards. Water stains blooming at the baseboards. A row of old family photographs along the wall, their glass clouded from the inside.

Elena kept her eyes away from the glass.

In the parlor, the fireplace stood cold. Above it hung the wide rectangular mirror she had carefully avoided since arriving. Its tarnished frame was carved with laurel leaves and tiny birds with open beaks. Someone, maybe Cora, had tucked folded paper behind one corner of the frame to keep it level.

The knocking had stopped.

On the low table sat the glass of water Julian had poured earlier, untouched since dawn. Beside it were a chipped saucer, an empty matchbox, and a ceramic thimble painted with tiny red houses. The thimble was the signature detail of the room, absurdly cheerful in a place determined to rot from the soul outward.

Elena set the diary down as far from the water as possible.

Julian noticed. "Wise."

"Basic survival. Never put cursed literature near liquids."

"You think the diary is cursed?"

"I think the diary is rude."

He moved to the window and tested the latch. Then the next one. His motions were precise, contained. A man assembling facts into a wall. Every few minutes his hand went to his pocket watch. Open. Look. Shut. The small click began to thread through the room like a nervous pulse.

Elena sank onto the edge of the settee but did not settle back. Dust puffed up around her hips. She placed Pip beside her, then changed her mind and moved him to the table. Then changed her mind again and kept him in her lap.

"Make a decision," she muttered to herself.

Julian turned. "What?"

"Nothing. Speaking to the bear. As one does before noon in a haunted inheritance."

He crossed to the mantel and stood with his back deliberately to the mirror. "Continue from the game."

Elena opened the diary again. The metallic smell rose at once. She rubbed the page between thumb and forefinger, and the dried brown letters gave off that faint iron tang, as if Cora had written with something more intimate than ink.

The next entry began in the middle of a thought.

The blue girl watches them play from the reeds.

Elena's voice stuck.

Julian looked up.

She forced the rest out. "She stands where the water weeds grow tallest, though no one admits to seeing her at the same time. Tomas said she cannot be a real girl because real girls leave footprints. Elena said real girls do not always want to be found."

A log shifted in the cold fireplace though there was no fire.

Elena stared at the diary.

Julian crossed the room in two strides and crouched before the hearth. He picked up the iron poker and prodded the ashes. Gray dust collapsed inward. Beneath it, several half-burned scraps of paper curled black at the edges.

"More diary pages?" Elena asked.

"Maybe." He lifted one carefully with the poker. It disintegrated.

"Wonderful. Even the evidence has stage fright."

Julian checked his watch again. The chain flashed. Open. Shut. "Shadows are lengthening."

Elena glanced at the windows. The light had altered, but only slightly. "It is barely afternoon."

"In this house, the hour changes before the clocks do."

"That sounds dangerously close to speculation."

His eyes met hers. "Observation."

The air cooled.

Not dramatically. Not like a door opening to winter. Just a thin drop in temperature that slid over Elena's skin and settled at the back of her neck. The glass of water on the table trembled.

Elena saw it before Julian did.

The surface tightened, smooth as a held breath. Then one ripple widened from the center. Another followed. Rings touching rings, circling outward though nothing had moved.

"Julian."

He turned.

The water rippled harder.

Elena stood too quickly, Pip clutched against her ribs. The diary lay open beside the glass, Cora's cramped handwriting exposed and waiting. The water rose against the inner wall of the glass, bulging upward as if something below the table had struck it.

Then it spilled.

Not tipped. Not knocked. Spilled.

Water leapt over the rim in a clean, impossible sheet and slapped across the table, soaking into the diary pages.

"No!" Elena dropped Pip and lunged.

Julian caught the diary first, lifting it by the spine as water streamed from the edges. Brown ink bled instantly. Lines blurred. Words softened into stains.

Elena grabbed the saucer and shoved it under the dripping pages, as if a chipped piece of porcelain could save a dead woman's confession. "Get cloth. Towel. Anything."

Julian moved to the sideboard, yanked open drawers, found only napkins yellowed with age. He brought them anyway. Together they blotted the pages, shoulder to shoulder, close enough that Elena could feel the heat of him through the cold air.

The diary shuddered in her hands.

She almost dropped it.

Something moved beneath the wet paper. Not a thing. A pressure. A pushing from within the page, as if the words were trying to rise through the pulp.

A new line darkened where water spread.

Elena watched it appear.

Not written. Revealed.

Let the little one keep what the others cannot bear.

Her breath stopped.

"Did Cora write that?" Julian asked.

"It wasn't there."

His hand covered the page before more water could smear it. His fingers were broad, nicked at the knuckles, steady in a way Elena found offensive under the circumstances.

The room gave a low knock.

Not at the door this time.

Inside the walls.

One. Two. Three.

The photographs along the hall began to tick softly in their frames, glass tapping against wood. The sound spread, small and precise, until it filled the parlor like teeth chattering behind plaster.

Elena took one step back. Her heel hit Pip on the rug. She stumbled.

Julian's hand shot out.

She caught it without thinking.

That was the terrifying part. Not the water. Not the diary. Not the house politely counting down from inside its bones. Her own hand moved before pride, before strategy, before all the cold little barricades she had built since childhood.

Julian's fingers closed around hers.

He did not yank her closer. He did not make a speech. He interlaced his fingers with hers and squeezed tight, his thumb rubbing over her knuckles in a slow, silent promise of protection.

It should not have helped.

It helped.

Elena hated that almost as much as she needed it.

His palm was warm and calloused. Hers was damp from the water and shaking, though she would have denied that under oath. He held on as if her fear was not embarrassing. As if it was only a fact, and facts could be stood beside.

"Look at me," he said.

"Hard pass."

"Elena."

"If you tell me to breathe, the house can have you."

A brief flicker crossed his mouth. "I was going to tell you not to look at the mirror."

Every thought in her body turned toward the mantel.

Above the fireplace, the mirror waited.

Elena had not looked directly at it since entering the room. She had tracked it in edges, corners, glints. Now the silvered surface seemed brighter than the rest of the room, holding a patch of light that did not belong to any window.

Her reflection stood there with Julian's beside it.

Only their joined hands were wrong.

In the mirror, Elena's hand was empty.

A girl in blue stood between them.

Not in the room. Only in the glass.

Small. Still. Dark hair plastered to her cheeks as if wet. A blue dress clinging at the hem. Her face turned toward the diary, not toward Elena, and somehow that was worse. She did not speak. She did not move her mouth. She only lifted one dripping hand and pointed.

Elena jerked her gaze away so hard her neck hurt.

"What did you see?" Julian asked.

His voice had changed. Lower. Controlled, but not calm.

"Reflection."

"Of what?"

"Do not make me inventory the nightmare."

"Elena."

"The girl." Her throat tightened around the words. "Blue dress. In the mirror. Pointing at the diary."

Julian's grip on her hand strengthened, once. Then he released her only long enough to reach for the diary. The loss of his fingers left her palm cold.

He turned the wet pages carefully. Several clung together. Ink ran into the margins. He found a page near the back that had not been completely soaked, perhaps protected by the curve of the binding.

His pocket watch came out again. He looked at it and went pale.

"What?" Elena demanded.

"The hands have stopped."

"Mechanical watches stop."

"At exactly the blue hour."

The room had dimmed while she was not watching.

That was impossible. It had been afternoon minutes ago. The windows now held that washed-out, bruised light between day and dark, the hour when colors thinned and every reflection looked a little more alive than the thing reflected.

Julian backed away from the mantel. He did not turn toward the mirror. "We need to leave the house."

Elena stared at him. "You will not enter after dusk, but apparently evacuating with wet haunted paperwork is fine?"

"Now."

The parlor door slammed shut.

Not hard enough to break. Hard enough to make the latch bite.

Elena flinched, then immediately resented every muscle involved.

Julian crossed to the door and seized the handle. It did not turn. He threw his shoulder against it once, measured, controlled. The wood did not give.

"Facts?" Elena asked, voice thin.

"Door is stuck."

"That is not a fact. That is a hostage negotiation."

Behind them, the diary pages began to turn.

One by one.

No wind. No hand. Wet paper peeling from wet paper with a soft, obscene whisper.

Julian came back slowly. His eyes stayed on the diary, not the mirror.

The pages stopped.

A line sat in the center of the open spread, darker than the rest, as if the water had dragged it up from underneath every entry Cora had ever written.

Julian leaned in.

Elena should have stepped back. She did not. He stood close enough that his shoulder nearly brushed hers, close enough that she could smell dust, cold air, and the faint soap on his skin. The house knocked once inside the wall.

He read under his breath first. Then his voice sharpened.

"Elena, this says, 'The child in blue is not the one who drowned.'"

The mirror above the fireplace cracked violently down the center, and the sound of rushing water filled the room.
