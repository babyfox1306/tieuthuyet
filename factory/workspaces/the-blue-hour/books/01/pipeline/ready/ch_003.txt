# Chapter 3: The Weight of Water

The sky outside turned the color of a bruised plum, and the silence in the house suddenly dropped, heavy and suffocating.

Elena stood in the center of the hallway, the echo of the slamming door still vibrating in her teeth. She stared at the wood grain, expecting it to stop shaking, but the trembling seemed to transfer from the house to her own hands. She clenched her fists, digging her nails into her palms until the sharp sting grounded her. Julian was gone. His taillights were nothing but a memory dissolved into the tree line.

She was alone.

The air pressure in the vestibule shifted. It wasn't just the quiet; it was a physical presence, pressing against her eardrums like she had dived deep underwater and forgotten to equalize. Her pulse hammered a frantic rhythm against her ribs, a trapped bird looking for an exit.

"Stop it," she muttered to the empty room. Her voice sounded thin, swallowed instantly by the high ceiling. "It's just an old house settling. It's wood and nails, not a monster."

She turned back to the front door. She didn't know why she felt the sudden, desperate need to open it again. She was safe inside. The door was locked. But the walls felt like they were inching closer, the shadows stretching down from the crown molding like elongated fingers.

She reached for the brass handle, intending to check the lock, to reassure herself that the barrier between her and the encroaching woods was solid.

Her fingers touched the metal.

Elena snatched her hand back with a sharp gasp, stumbling until her spine hit the wall.

The handle was dripping wet.

She stared at it, her breath hitching in her throat. There was no leak here. The ceiling above was solid, the roof intact. There hadn't been a drop of rain from the bruised purple sky. Yet, the brass handle glistened in the dying light, slick with a viscous, clear fluid.

Slowly, fighting the instinct to run back to the car she no longer possessed, she leaned forward. She didn't touch it this time. She just smelled.

The scent hit her like a physical blow to the stomach. It wasn't the metallic tang of tap water or the rain-clean smell of a storm. It was the thick, cloying stench of rot. It smelled of slime-covered rocks, of stagnant weeds, and of the dark, muck-covered bottom of the lake.

Algae. Decay. Drowning.

"What the hell..." she whispered, the bile rising in her throat.

The humidity in the hall spiked. The air grew thick, hot, and wet, filling her lungs with the taste of stale lake water. She coughed, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand, but the taste was persistent, coating her tongue in something ancient and wrong. If she didn't look at the door, maybe it would just be a hallucination. If she didn't look, the handle would be dry brass again.

She squeezed her eyes shut. If I don't look, it isn't there.

"Drafts," she said, her voice trembling. "Old plumbing. Condensation."

But the silence of the house laughed at her. It was a low, humming sound, resonating in the floorboards beneath her shoes. She pushed off the wall and moved away from the door, her steps quick and uneven. She needed to be away from the vestibule, away from the water that shouldn't be there.

She fled deeper into the house, moving toward the parlor. The windows here were large, panoramic views of the lake that usually commanded high real estate prices. Now, they looked like black maws waiting to be fed.

The sun had finally dipped below the horizon, leaving the world in that strange, violet limbo known as the blue hour. The lake outside was no longer water; it was an abyss, a sheet of black glass that reflected nothing but the dying light.

Elena stopped in the center of the parlor, her chest heaving. She needed to calm down. She needed to think. She reached into her pocket, her fingers brushing the cold glass of her phone. It was a lifeline, a tether to the modern world where physics made sense and door handles didn't ooze pond scum.

She looked up at the window to her right.

The glass was dark, reflecting the interior of the room more than the exterior landscape. She could see the faint outline of the furniture, the dusty silhouette of the standing lamp, and her own ghostly pale figure standing in the center of the room.

Then, her reflection moved.

Elena froze. She was holding her breath. She was absolutely, positively holding her breath.

But the Elena in the glass wasn't.

The reflection exhaled, a small puff of fog appearing on the surface of the window pane. Then, slowly, the reflected figure raised a hand.

Elena's own hands hung limp at her sides. She tried to scream, but her throat clamped shut. The reflection raised its right hand, palm flat, and pressed it against the glass.

Not Elena's hand. A smaller hand. A child's hand.

Behind the reflection of Elena's adult form, superimposed over the dark backdrop of the night sky, another shape emerged. It was a girl, no older than eight, wearing a dress the color of deep, drowning blue. She stood right behind the reflection of Elena, so close that if they were in the same room, their shoulders would have touched.

The Girl in Blue.

She wasn't looking at Elena's reflection. She was looking through the glass, her eyes wide, dark pits of sorrow. She pressed her small hand flat against the window, as if she were trapped on the other side, trying to step out into the room.

The pressure in the room skyrocketed. Elena's ears popped. The temperature plummeted, but the air remained wet, clinging to her skin like a second, suffocating layer of clothing.

"Get out," Elena choked out, the sound barely a whisper. "Get out of my house."

The girl in the window didn't move. She just stared, her hand leaving a faint, foggy print on the glass.

Elena scrambled backward, her heel catching on the edge of the rug. She pitched forward, catching herself on the arm of a stiff velvet chair. She didn't look back at the window. She couldn't. If she looked, the girl would be closer. She knew it with the terrifying certainty of a nightmare.

She scrambled for her phone, nearly dropping it as she fumbled with the screen. Her vision swam, dark spots dancing at the edges. She needed to hear a voice. She needed proof that the world hadn't dissolved into madness.

She dialed the only number she had memorized besides her mother's.

The line rang once. Twice.

"Julian," she said the moment the call connected, before he could even speak. She hated that her voice cracked, hated the desperation that leaked out.

"Elena?" His voice came through instantly, low and steady. The background noise was faint—the rumble of an engine. He hadn't made it far. "What happened? Is the door secured?"

"The door is wet," she blurted out, pressing the phone hard against her ear, as if his voice could physically push back the spirits in the room. "The handle. It's... it's covered in slime. It smells like the lake. And the window—Julian, there's a girl in the glass."

Silence on the line. For a second, she thought she'd lost him. Then she heard the sharp intake of breath, a familiar tic before he delivered bad news.

"The blue hour," he said, his voice tight but controlled. "Listen to me, Elena. Do not engage with the reflections. Do not look at the windows."

"I'm not engaging! She's right there!" Elena pressed her back against the wall, sliding down until she hit the floor. She hugged her knees to her chest, gripping the phone like a weapon. "Why is the handle wet? Julian, tell me that's normal. Tell me it's a pipe."

"It's not a pipe," he said softly. The engine noise in the background faded; he was pulling over. "The moisture... the house absorbs the lake. At dusk, the barrier thins. The water comes in. It's not just water, Elena. It's memory."

She let out a harsh, jagged laugh. "Memory doesn't drip from brass handles."

"No," he agreed. "But trauma does. Listen to me. I'm turning around. I'm coming back."

"No!" The word was out of her mouth before she could think. The idea of him driving back into this suffocating darkness, of him seeing the impossible water on the door... it was too much. She didn't want him to see her losing her mind. She didn't want to be the weak client who needed a rescue an hour after signing the papers. "Don't. Just... stay on the phone. Please."

"Elena, the rules—"

"Screw the rules!" She took a shuddering breath, forcing her heart rate down. She focused on the sound of his breathing. It was rhythmic, human. It didn't smell like algae. "Just talk to me. Tell me something boring. Tell me about the watch you keep checking."

There was a pause on the line. When he spoke again, his voice dropped an octave, losing the professional edge, becoming something warmer, something that wrapped around her fraying nerves.

"It's a vintage Audemars Piguet," he said. "My father's. He was obsessed with time. Said it was the only thing we couldn't buy back. I check it because I hate waiting. I hate being late."

Elena closed her eyes, the image of the window fading slightly, replaced by the mental image of Julian's strong hands turning the silver knob of his watch. "Are you late often?"

"Never," he said, and she heard the faint trace of a smile. "Which is why I am currently parked on the side of the highway, arguing with a client who refuses my help."

"I'm not refusing help," she murmured, resting her forehead on her knees. "I'm just... pausing it."

"Okay," he said gently. "We're pausing it. Are you still in the hallway?"

"No. Parlor."

"Is the reflection still there?"

Elena risked a glance. The window was a black rectangle. The girl was gone. The foggy handprint was fading, evaporating into the dry air of the room.

"No," she whispered. "She's gone."

"But the water is there?"

"I'm not going to check the door."

"Good. Stay where you are. The blue hour passes. The sun sets completely, the shadows stabilize. Just breathe."

The sound of his voice was an anchor in the rising tide. She found herself matching her breathing to the cadence of his speech. In. Out. The air in the room lost that humid, suffocating weight, though the metallic taste lingered at the back of her throat. She realized she was gripping the phone so hard her fingers ached.

"You're trembling," Julian observed. It wasn't a question. He could probably hear the rattle in her breath.

"Adrenaline," she lied. "I'm fine."

"You're not fine, Elena. You're in a house that is trying to drown you." His voice roughened. "I should never have left you there alone tonight. I knew the reading was off. I felt it in the floorboards."

"Don't get poetic on me, Vane," she said, but her tone lacked bite. It sounded more like a plea. "Just... keep talking."

They stayed like that for minutes that felt like hours. He talked about the structural integrity of Victorian foundations, about the specific type of rot that affected cedar siding. He talked about anything and everything that was solid, logical, and real. Elena listened, letting the words wash over her, chasing away the silence.

Eventually, her breathing slowed to normal. The oppressive darkness in the corners of the room receded, becoming just shadows again. The house felt old, yes, but not actively malicious.

"The sun is down," Julian said quietly. "The worst of it is over. The lake will settle."

"Yeah," Elena said. She felt drained, hollowed out. "I think I'm okay now. I'm going to... I'm going to find a lamp."

"Elena." He stopped her. "If you see the water again, if it rises... you run. You don't call me. You break a window if you have to. Do not let the water touch your skin."

"Okay," she whispered, a chill running down her spine.

"I'll be there at first light. Seven AM. Sharp."

"I'll be here," she said. "Probably barricaded in the attic."

"Try the living room. It has the best egress."

"Goodnight, Julian."

"Goodnight, Elena."

The line clicked dead. The silence returned, but it was different now. It wasn't the heavy, suffocating blanket from before. It was just silence. Empty. Quiet.

Elena lowered the phone, her hand trembling slightly. She sat on the floor for a long moment, gathering the strength to stand. The romance of the conversation, the strange intimacy of his voice in the dark, faded as reality set in. She was still in the house. The handle was still wet. The girl was still somewhere.

She pushed herself up, her legs stiff. She needed to see. She needed to know.

She walked to the window. The glass was cold, dry, and clear. The girl was gone. Outside, the lake was a black void under the rising moon.

She turned away from the window and scanned the room. She needed a distraction. Something to do with her hands. Her eyes landed on the armchair she had stumbled into earlier. Sitting there, propped up against the cushion as if waiting for her, was Pip.

The bear.

It looked worse than she remembered. One of its glass eyes was missing, leaving an empty, threadbare socket. The fur was matted, smelling of dust and cedar chest. It was a pathetic thing, a relic of a childhood she couldn't remember.

Elena walked over and picked it up. The fur was coarse under her thumb. She rubbed the pad of her thumb against the grain of the fabric, a tic she hadn't realized she needed until her heart rate slowed.

"Pip," she whispered. "You look like hell."

The bear stared back with its one good eye.
