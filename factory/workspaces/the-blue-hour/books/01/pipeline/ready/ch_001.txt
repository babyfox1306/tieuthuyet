# Chapter 1: The Wood That Knows

Don't touch the railing, Julian said, his voice low and rough, cutting through the humid silence before Elena had even fully stepped onto the porch.

Elena froze, her hand hovering inches from the peeling paint of the balustrade. Her fingers twitched, a reflex aborted mid-air. She pulled her hand back, clutching the strap of her canvas bag tighter against her shoulder. The wood there looked gray and sick, but beneath the rot, something about the grain seemed to vibrate, as if the house itself were exhaling.

She hadn't even heard him approach. The lake house sat in a clearing of overgrown pines, isolated and silent, yet Julian Vane had materialized out of the shadows like he was made of them. He stood by the support column, his silhouette broad and blocking the dying light of the afternoon. He didn't look at her with the polite curiosity of a neighbor. He looked at her the way a coroner looks at a body that hasn't decided if it wants to be dead yet.

It was unnerving. Elena preferred wood to people. Wood didn't ask why her hands shook. Wood didn't have eyes that stripped away her defenses before she could speak.

I wasn't going to break it, she said, her voice sharper than she intended. She hated how defensive she sounded, how small she felt in the shadow of this sprawling, decaying structure. I was just going to open the door. It is my door, legally speaking.

Julian stepped away from the column. The movement was fluid, predatory but restrained. He wore a coat that looked too heavy for the sticky summer heat, the dark fabric absorbing the last of the sun's warmth. He reached into his pocket, his fingers brushing against the metal chain of a watch—a nervous tic, perhaps, or a countdown to something she couldn't see.

The structural integrity of the porch is compromised, he stated. Flat. Factual. No emotion, just the cold reality of rot and physics. If you put your weight there, you go through. The contractors were supposed to cordon it off.

Contractors, Elena repeated, looking past him to the front door. The paint was chipping there too, revealing the raw wood underneath, dark and wet-looking despite the lack of rain. Aunt Cora never mentioned contractors. She just... stopped answering the phone.

Julian didn't respond. He just watched her, his gaze lingering on her face for a second too long before sliding back to the house. The air between them felt thick, charged with a static that had nothing to do with the coming storm. It was the friction of strangers occupying the same space, one desperate to forget, the other seemingly desperate to remember.

I'm going inside, Elena said, lifting her chin. She needed to get away from his eyes, away from the feeling that he could see the fractures in her psyche just by looking at her posture.

She moved toward the door. Her boots crunched on the gravel of the path, the sound loud in the quiet clearing. As she reached for the brass knob, the heavy oak door swung inward with a slow, agonizing creak.

Elena stopped. There was no wind. The air was dead still, the leaves of the surrounding pines hanging limp in the humidity. She looked back at Julian.

It's unlocked, she said.

Julian frowned, a deep line forming between his brows. It shouldn't be. I locked it myself yesterday.

Yesterday? Elena asked. You've been here?

I check the perimeter, Julian said. He evaded the direct answer, stepping closer but keeping a respectful distance. The house needs maintenance, Miss March. Your aunt left it... vulnerable.

Vulnerable. The word sat heavy in the air. Elena looked into the dark throat of the hallway. It smelled of stale air, dust, and something sweet and cloying, like lilies left too long in a vase.

I can handle a little dust, she muttered.

She stepped over the threshold. The floorboards groaned under her weight, a high-pitched whine that echoed down the long, empty corridor. To her left, a parlor sat shrouded in sheets, the furniture looking like crouching beasts in the gloom. To her right, a staircase swept upward into the darkness, the banister winding like a spine.

Wait, Julian said from behind her.

He didn't follow her in immediately. He stood on the porch, one boot on the threshold, the other on the rotting wood outside. He looked hesitant, his hand gripping the doorframe so hard his knuckles turned white.

The light, he said, his eyes darting to the window where the sun was beginning to dip below the tree line. It's going to be dark soon.

So? Elena turned to face him, the dim light of the hall casting long shadows across her face. Are you afraid of the dark, Mr. Vane?

Julian cleared his throat, the sound raspy and abrupt. I don't enter the house after dusk, he said. It's a precaution. The wiring is faulty. The foundation shifts at night.

It sounded like a lie. It sounded like the kind of careful, constructed truth a man tells when he's hiding a much bigger, uglier fear. But Elena didn't push. She didn't want him here anyway. His presence was too loud, too solid. He disrupted the silence she needed to hear herself think.

Then I suggest you leave, she said. I have work to do.

Julian hesitated. He looked at the staircase, then back at her. For a moment, something like worry softened the hard lines of his face. He reached out, as if to touch her arm, then thought better of it and let his hand drop.

Be careful on the stairs, he said. The third step from the top is loose. And don't touch the walls in the attic. The plaster is...

I'll be fine, she interrupted.

He nodded slowly, backing away onto the porch. The sun was a sliver of orange fire now, painting the sky in bruises of purple and gray. He pulled his pocket watch out, snapped it open, checked it, and snapped it shut.

I'll be at the cottage down the road, he said. If you hear anything. If the house makes noises that don't sound like a house.

He turned and walked away without waiting for a reply. His footsteps crunched on the gravel, fading into the chirping of the cicadas. Elena watched him go until the trees swallowed him up.

Then she was alone.

The silence of the house rushed in to fill the void. It wasn't a peaceful silence. It was a holding breath. Elena let out a breath she didn't know she'd been holding and dropped her bag on the floor.

She walked further into the hall. The air here was cooler, almost cold. She ran her hand along the wall, her fingertips catching on the rough wallpaper. She needed to inventory this place, to clear it out, to sell it. That was the plan. Get in, get rid of the ghosts, get out.

But the house felt like it was watching her. The Girl in Blue, a fragment of a memory she couldn't quite grasp, flitted at the edge of her mind. Just a trick of the light, she told herself. Just the stress of the drive.

She started toward the stairs. She knew she should check the ground floor first, check the kitchen, check the locks, but something pulled her upward. A magnetic hum in the floorboards.

She climbed slowly. The wood complained with every step, a symphony of age and neglect. She remembered Julian's warning about the third step from the top. She counted as she climbed. One. Two. Three.

She tested the step with her toe. It didn't give, but it felt spongy, soft with rot. She skipped it, stepping onto the fourth.

Upstairs, the darkness was deeper. The hallway stretched out before her, lined with closed doors. Dust motes danced in the shafts of dying light that speared through the dusty windows.

Which room? she wondered.

Her feet moved of their own accord, guided by an instinct she couldn't name. She stopped in front of a narrow door tucked under the eaves. The attic.

The door was slightly ajar. Elena pushed it open. It squealed on its hinges, a high, thin sound like a child crying.

The attic was a cavern of shadows. Sloping ceilings pressed down, cluttered with the debris of a lifetime. Trunks, old lamps, stacks of magazines yellowed with age. The air here was thick, tasting of old paper and dry rot.

Elena stepped inside. She moved slowly, navigating around a stack of wooden crates. Her heart was beating a little faster now, a rhythmic thrum against her ribs. She didn't want to be here, but she couldn't leave.

She scanned the shelves that lined the far wall. Junk. Broken toys. A rusted typewriter. And then, something caught her eye.

Sitting on a high shelf, half-hidden behind a stack of moth-eaten quilts, was a bear.

It was small, matted fur the color of dried dirt. One of its button eyes was missing, leaving a empty thread-stitched hole. It sat slumped forward, as if it had been waiting for a very long time for someone to pick it up.

Pip.

The name dropped into her mind without effort. A sudden, sharp pang of longing hit her chest, so intense it nearly knocked the wind out of her. She didn't remember getting the bear. She didn't remember holding it. But she knew its name.

Elena reached up. Her hand trembled, not from cold, but from a sudden, inexplicable sense of dread. Her fingers brushed the coarse fur.

The moment her skin touched the bear, the world tilted.

The attic vanished.

The smell of old paper was replaced by the smell of water. Cold, dark, suffocating water. It filled her nose, her throat, her lungs. She wasn't standing in a dusty room; she was under. The pressure was immense, crushing her chest. She couldn't breathe. She couldn't scream.

Bubbles. Silver bubbles rising past her eyes. Darkness above. Darkness below.

A hand. Reaching for her. Or was she reaching for it?

The panic was absolute. A primal fear response. Her lungs burned, screaming for oxygen that wasn't there. The cold seeped into her bones, freezing her from the inside out. She was drowning. She was eight years old again, and she was drowning.

No! she gasped, jerking her hand back.

The connection broke. She stumbled back, her boot catching on the edge of a rug.

Her balance gone, she pitched backward. She flailed her arms, grabbing at nothing but air. She braced herself for the impact of the hard floor.

But the floor didn't meet her.

A hand shot out from the gloom, gripping her forearm with iron strength. She wasn't underwater anymore. She was in the attic, and Julian Vane was holding her up.

She gasped for air, her chest heaving, sucking in the dusty attic oxygen greedily. Her vision swam, the spots of the afterimage of dark water fading slowly.

Julian steadied her. He stood close, too close. She could smell the scent of rain and tobacco on his coat, a sharp contrast to the sweet rot of the house. His grip was firm, grounding her, pulling her back from the edge of the abyss.

I told you not to come up here, he said, his voice a low rumble that seemed to vibrate through her bones. Are you hurt?

Elena looked up at him. His face was in shadow, but his eyes were bright, intent. He wasn't looking at her with judgment. He was looking at her with a terrifying understanding.

I... I tripped, she stammered, her voice weak. She cursed herself for the tremor. She tried to pull her arm away, but he held on for a second longer.

His thumb pressed against the inside of her wrist, right over her racing pulse. He didn't say a word, just watched her face as if measuring the rhythm of her fear. The contact sent a jolt of electricity through her, a sudden, shocking heat that burned away the cold memory of the water.

For a heartbeat, neither of them moved. The attic was silent, save for the ragged sound of her breathing. The tension between them was a physical thing, a tight wire pulled taut. It wasn't just fear anymore. It was awareness. The solid, undeniable reality of his hand on her skin.

Then, as if realizing he had crossed a line, Julian released her.

He took a step back, the sudden loss of his warmth leaving her feeling adrift again. He checked his pocket watch, the snap loud in the quiet room.

It's time, he said abruptly. He wouldn't look at the bear on the shelf. He wouldn't look at her. I have to go.

Elena wrapped her arms around herself, rubbing the spot where he had held her. It still tingled. You were leaving anyway, she said, trying to recover her composure, trying to find the sharp, sarcastic armor she usually wore.

Julian nodded. He turned toward the door, then paused. He looked back at her, his profile silhouetted against the dim light of the hallway.

Don't touch the things in here, Elena, he said softly. Some things are better left buried.

He turned and walked down the stairs. This time, his footsteps were rapid, urgent. He was fleeing the house, fleeing the dark, fleeing whatever it was he had seen in her eyes.

Elena stood alone in the attic. She looked at the bear on the shelf. It sat there, innocent and still. But the air around it felt heavy, weighted with the ghosts of breath held too long.

She took a deep breath, forcing the sensation of water out of her mind. It was just a panic attack. Just stress. Just an old house playing tricks.

She turned and left the attic, closing the door firmly behind her.

She walked down the hallway, the floorboards creaking under her feet. The sun had finally set. The house was submerged in the blue-grey twilight of the evening. The Blue Hour.

She needed to wash her face. She needed to cool down. She needed to scrub away the feeling of Julian's fingers on her skin and the phantom pressure of the lake water in her lungs.

She found the bathroom downstairs. It was ancient, cracked porcelain and rusted faucets. She turned the tap. The water sputtered, then ran cold.

She splashed water on her face. The shock of it helped. She looked up, dripping, into the mirror above the sink.

The glass was spotted with age, distorting her reflection. She saw her own face—pale, wet, eyes wide. She saw the water dripping from her chin.

She reached for a towel to dry her eyes. As she lowered the towel and looked back into the glass, the air left her lungs in a silent scream.

Standing directly behind her shoulder, close enough to touch, was a small girl.

She wore a dress the color of a deep, bruising lake. Her hair was wet, plastered to her forehead in dark strands. She wasn't looking at Elena. She was looking through her, her expression hollow, her eyes empty pits of sorrow.

Elena spun around, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird.

The bathroom was empty.

The doorway was clear. The hallway was still.

She turned back to the mirror, her breath hitching in her throat. The girl was gone. Only Elena's own terrified reflection stared back, her face white as a sheet.

But on the mirror, right where the girl's shoulder had been, a single drop of water slid down the glass, leaving a wet trail through the dust.
