# Chapter 5: The Journal

If Julian tells, the Vale name will shatter. I must act, but I am afraid.

Mara read the line twice, the lamp casting a long shadow across the page. The ink was slightly smeared, as if Ilse’s hand had trembled violently when she wrote it. The date at the top of the entry was from over twenty years ago, late summer, the precise season where Mara’s own memory became a jagged scar instead of a continuous film. She rubbed her thumb over the rough texture of the paper, feeling a grind of anxiety in her chest that had nothing to do with the dust motes dancing in the yellow light.

She was sitting at the mahogany desk in the study, a room that smelled of old leather and dried lavender. The house was settling around her, the wooden groans sounding almost like human sighs. Outside, the wind was picking up, rattling the windowpanes, but inside, it was just her and the terrifyingly mundane thoughts of her dead aunt.

"Julian," she whispered, testing the name. It meant nothing to her. No face popped into her mind, no voice. But the way Ilse wrote it—with a pressure that nearly indented the next page—suggested he was a catalyst. A thread pull that could unravel the entire sweater of the Vale family history.

Mara looked away from the journal, her eyes darting to the darkened corner of the room. She refused to look at the glass of the window when the room was this dim; the temptation to see something that wasn't there was too strong. Her left hand throbbed faintly, the scar tissue tight. She flexed her fingers, forcing the sensation away.

Logic. She needed logic. If Ilse had hidden this journal in a hollowed-out encyclopedia, she had intended for it to be found, or perhaps, she had intended to hide it just well enough that only someone looking for the truth would find it. The entry after the warning was maddeningly vague. *The hearth knows the heat. The left side consumes the secret.*

Mara closed the journal, the leather cover creaking loudly in the silence. The radiator in the corner gave a sudden, violent clang—thump-thump-thump—mimicking a racing heartbeat. She ignored it. The house was old, pipes were noisy. That was the explanation. It had to be.

She stood up, her chair scraping against the floorboards. She needed a second pair of eyes, and more importantly, a second brain that wasn't currently haunted by the ghost of its own childhood. Asher Croft was in the living room, ostensibly guarding the perimeter from the increasingly aggressive Gerald Vance, though Mara suspected he was just trying to give her space to process.

She found him standing by the fireplace, his back to her. He was staring at the portrait of Lydia Vale that hung above the mantle, his hands clasped loosely behind his back. The fire was out, had been for years, but the grate was still filled with charred logs that looked like fossilized bones.

"Asher," she said.

He turned immediately, his movements fluid and controlled. The tension in his jaw was evident; he ran his fingers along the line of it, a habit she noticed he did when he was cataloging risks. He looked at the journal in her hands, then at her face.

"You found something," he stated. It wasn't a question.

“Ilse was terrified of a man named Julian,” Mara said, walking into the room. She kept her voice level, refusing to let the tremor in her hands reach her voice. “She wrote that if he spoke, the family would be destroyed. And she left a clue about where the rest of the information is.”

Asher raised an eyebrow. “The hearth?”

“Yes. ‘The left side consumes the secret.’” approached the fireplace, standing close enough to him that she could smell the scent of rain and cedar that clung to his coat. It was a grounding smell, starkly real against the mustiness of the house.

Asher looked at the fireplace, his eyes narrowing. He crouched down, inspecting the brickwork. “This is original to the house. 1920s construction. It’s possible there’s a void, but the bricks would have been mortared in place.”

“If anyone could hide a void in a wall, it was Ilse,” Mara said, crouching beside him. The space was tight, their shoulders almost brushing. She felt the warmth radiating from him, a stark contrast to the chill that seemed to seep from the masonry.

Asher ran his hand over the rough bricks of the left side of the firebox. He moved with precision, tapping each stone gently, listening for a change in pitch. Tap. Tap. Thud.

He paused. His hand hovered over a brick near the back corner, mostly obscured by soot and ash. “It sounds hollow.”

Mara leaned in, the dust making her nose twitch. “Can we get it out?”

“We can try.” He looked at her, a dry humor touching his lips. “Though I should warn you, my experience with forced entry is limited to unlocking doors when I’ve locked myself out of my own office.”

“Ilse didn’t put a lock on it,” Mara murmured, staring at the brick. “She put a puzzle. It has to be a lever or a pressure point.”

She reached out, her fingers hovering over the soot-stained mortar. She hesitated. The last time she had reached into a dark, enclosed space in this house, she had found the photograph that had started this whole nightmare. But she wasn’t a child anymore, and she wasn’t alone.

“Move over,” she said, nudging his knee with hers.

Asher shifted back, giving her room but staying close. “Be careful. If it’s unstable, the whole structure could—”

“It’s a brick, Asher, not a nuclear warhead,” she cut him off, though her heart was hammering against her ribs. She pressed her palm flat against the brick. It was cold, biting into her skin. She pushed. Nothing. She twisted her wrist, applying pressure to the top edge.

There was a sudden click, loud as a gunshot in the quiet room. The brick didn't fall out, but it depressed inward, sliding smoothly into the darkness behind the wall.

“Or that,” Asher said softly. “That works too.”

A section of the firebox wall shuddered. With a grinding scrape of stone on stone, a narrow panel swung inward, revealing a gap barely wide enough for a person to squeeze through. A blast of stale, freezing air hit them, smelling of sealed-off time and decay.

Mara shivered. “It’s a false wall.”

“Behind the fireplace,” Asher murmured, shining the light from his phone into the void. “Clever. The chimney stack would mask the space from the outside.”

He stood up and brushed off his hands, then looked down at her. “I’ll go in. It’s tight.”

“I’m going,” Mara said, standing up and brushing the dust from her jeans. “It’s my family’s secret, Asher. I’m not sending you in like a canary in a coal mine.”

He didn't argue, which she appreciated. He just nodded, that stoic mask slipping back into place.

The opening was cramped. Mara had to turn sideways, ducking her head to avoid the lintel. Asher followed right behind her. The darkness was absolute here, away from the lamplight of the living room, the only illumination coming from the weak beam of Asher’s flashlight.

“Careful,” Asher whispered, his voice right in her ear. The acoustics of the small space made everything sound intimate.

The space behind the wall was small, a triangular crawl space created by the angle of the chimney breast. It was claustrophobic, the ceiling pressing down on Mara’s shoulders. She forced herself to breathe steadily. *Logical possibilities,* she told herself. *I am stuck in a small space with a near-stranger looking for letters. That is all. This is not the attic. This is not the dark.*

Her heart rate didn't get the memo.

“Over there,” Asher said, directing the beam of light.

On a small, makeshift shelf of stone sat a metal box. It was rusted along the edges, the kind of box that might have held cookies once, but now held something far heavier.

Mara reached for it, but the angle was awkward. She had to stretch, her shoulder brushing against Asher’s chest. He reached out at the same time, his hand covering hers to steady her grip on the slick metal.

For a second, everything stopped.

The contact was electric. Not a static shock, but a visceral hum that seemed to vibrate up her arm and settle somewhere deep in her chest. She could feel the calluses on his fingertips, the warmth of his skin, the strength in his grip. She froze, her breath catching in her throat.

Asher didn't pull away immediately. His hand lingered, his thumb grazing the sensitive skin of her wrist. He was looking at her, she could feel it, even in the near-total darkness. The air between them felt charged, heavy with something that had absolutely nothing to do with hidden letters or family secrets.

It was terrifying. It was grounding.

“Steady,” he murmured, his voice lower than before, rougher.

Mara swallowed hard and pulled her hand away, breaking the connection. The cold rushed back in instantly. “I’ve got it,” she said, her voice sounding breathless to her own ears.

She gripped the box with both hands and pulled it free. It was heavier than it looked. She turned, awkwardly maneuvering in the tight space to face the exit.

“Let’s get out of here,” Asher said. He positioned himself behind her, a solid presence at her back that made the squeeze back out feel less like entrapment and more like an escape.

They emerged back into the living room, gasping in the comparatively fresh air. Mara set the box on the coffee table, her hands trembling slightly. She wiped her palms on her jeans, trying to erase the lingering sensation of his touch, but it was branded there.

Asher sat on the sofa opposite her, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees. He watched her, his gaze intense but unreadable. “Open it.”

Mara nodded. She didn't wait. She unlatched the rusted hook. It snapped open with a sharp *ping*.

Inside, nestled between folds of discolored velvet, was a stack of envelopes bound together. The letters were bound with a black ribbon, but the bow was a clumsy child's knot—Ilse tied it in a hurry, terrified. The sight of that messy, desperate knot sent a spike of sorrow through Mara’s heart. Aunt Ilse, always so composed, always so proper, had sat here and tied this ribbon with shaking hands.

Mara undid the knot gently, preserving the loops. She lifted the top envelope. It was addressed to *Julian*.

She flipped it over. No return address.

She picked up the next one. This one was different. The paper was thicker, cream-colored. It was sealed with red wax, the imprint cracked and faded.

“Who are they from?” Asher asked.

Mara broke the seal on the cream-colored envelope. She pulled out the letter. The handwriting was sharp, architectural, masculine. It looked nothing like Ilse’s loops.

She scanned the page. The signature at the bottom caught her eye first.

*J. Croft.*

She frowned. She looked up at Asher. “J. Croft. Do you know him?”

“My uncle,” Asher said. The words were quiet, devoid of inflection, but Mara saw the way his posture changed. He straightened up, the precision returning to his movements, his mask of calm slipping back into place like armor. “Julian Croft. My father’s younger brother. He disappeared decades ago. We were told he left the country.”

Mara felt the blood drain from her face. She looked back down at the letter. She read the first line aloud, her voice shaking.

*’Arthur, I cannot stay silent any longer. She is dangerous. I have proof of what happened in the attic. If I do not return, do not look for me in the usual places.’*

Mara stopped. She looked at Asher. His face was pale, the lines around his mouth tight. He reached out and took the letter from her. His movements were usually so fluid, but now there was a stiffness to him, a mechanical precision that scared her more than any outburst.

He read the words, his eyes scanning the page rapidly. Then he read it again.

The room was silent. The radiator knocked again, a lonely, hollow sound in the vastness of the house.

Asher’s hands trembled as he held the letter. It was a subtle vibration, barely visible, but it was there. He looked up, and for the first time since she had met him, the dry humor was gone. The calm was gone. He looked stripped bare, staring into an abyss he hadn't known existed.

“Julian Croft is my uncle,” he said, his voice hollow, echoing off the cold walls of the room. “My father never knew what happened to him.”

He looked at Mara, and the devastation in his eyes made her scarred hand ache in sympathy. We were strangers, she thought. We were supposed to be strangers.

But the secrets of this house had a way of entangling everything, of tying knots that could never be undone.

“He didn’t leave the country, Asher,” Mara whispered, the weight of the journal’s warning settling over them like a shroud. “He died here. And your family... and mine... they buried him.”
