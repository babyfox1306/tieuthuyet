# Chapter 1: The Key That Turned

The key felt heavier than a piece of brass ought to be, dragging my hand down toward the lock like an anchor sinking in deep water. I stood on the warped porch of Blackwood Manor, the wood groaning under my boots, and told myself for the tenth time that this was just a transaction. A legal transfer of property, a box on a checklist to be ticked off before I could sell the place and scrub the Vale name from this town forever.

My thumb found the jagged ridge of scar tissue on my left palm, rubbing the slick, raised skin until it burned. It was a bad habit, a nervous tic I couldn't shake, but the pain grounded me. It kept the memories at bay, or at least, it kept the *missing* pieces of them from swarming too close to the surface.

I inserted the key into the deadbolt. It didn't slide in smoothly; it caught, scraped against the tumblers with a metallic shriek that set my teeth on edge, and then turned with a resistance that felt suspiciously like reluctance.

The door swung open, and I stepped into the dark.

The air inside was stagnant, trapped in a vacuum for years, and it hit me with the force of a physical blow. It smelled of Ilse. That overpowering, cloying scent of rosewater she had doused herself in every day of her life. It clung to the wallpaper, the drapes, the very dust motes dancing in the slivers of light piercing the gloom.

But beneath the floral perfume, sharp and biting, was something else. Something metallic.

Copper. Old blood. Or rust.

My heart hammered against my ribs, a frantic, irregular rhythm I tried to suppress with logic. *It’s just old pipes,* I told myself. *It’s the smell of neglect and damp. Stop being dramatic, Mara.*

I reached out and flipped the light switch by the door. Nothing happened. Of course. The electricity had likely been cut years ago, or the wiring had simply given up the ghost. I fished my phone from my pocket, turned on the flashlight, and swept the beam across the foyer.

The beam cut through the darkness, revealing a sprawling entrance hall that looked like a tomb for a wealthy eccentric. A grand staircase curved upward into the shadows, the banister polished to a dark shine by hands that hadn't touched it in a decade. Dust coated every surface like gray snow, thick enough that I could write my name in it.

"You're being ridiculous," I whispered to the empty air. My voice sounded small, swallowed instantly by the high ceilings.

I closed the door behind me. The latches clicked into place, a sharp, final sound that echoed through the house.

I moved deeper into the hall, my boots silent on the threadbare runner rug. To my left was a parlor, the furniture shrouded in white sheets that looked like ghostly crouching figures in the dim light. To my right, a dining room dominated by a table that could seat twenty, the chairs pushed in neatly, as if expecting guests who had never arrived.

I avoided looking at the large mirror hanging above the sideboard in the hallway. I had caught my own reflection in the darkened glass of the car window earlier, a pale, haunted face with eyes too wide for the skull beneath, and I had no desire to repeat the experience. Mirrors in the dark were lies. They showed you what you feared, not what was there.

*Focus,* I chided myself. *Find the boiler. Check the structural integrity of the ground floor. Get out.*

I walked past the staircase, aiming for what looked like the door to the kitchen. The air grew colder the further I moved from the entrance, a chill that seeped through my coat and settled in my bones. It wasn't just cold; it was a sterile, clinical chill, like a morgue or a hospital corridor.

Then, the sound started.

*Clank. Clank. Clank.*

I froze, the beam of my flashlight jerking toward the source of the noise. It came from the wall behind me—a massive, cast-iron radiator tucked against the baseboard.

*Pause.*

*Clank... Clank... Clank.*

Three short. Three long. Three short.

I stared at the radiator, my breath hitching in my throat. The house was empty. The power was off. There was no boiler running, no water pressure building up in the pipes. For this thing to knock, there had to be heat, there had to be flow.

Unless someone was inside. Behind the wall.

Or unless the house was simply laughing at me.

"It's thermal expansion," I said, my voice trembling slightly, trying to force the logic to stick. "Metal contracts and expands with temperature changes. It’s basic physics."

The radiator didn't answer. It just sat there, a silent, hulking beast of iron and paint, mocking my attempt at rationalization.

I backed away slowly, my eyes locked on the pipes, waiting for another sound, another sign. When nothing came, I turned and hurried toward the kitchen, my pace faster than I intended. The smell of rosewater was stronger here, almost choking, masking that underlying scent of copper that made my stomach churn.

The kitchen was large and outdated, with linoleum floors peeling at the edges and appliances that looked like they belonged in the 1950s. A heavy wooden table sat in the center, and for a second, just a fleeting second, I saw a flash of blue in my peripheral vision.

I spun around, flashlight slashing through the gloom. Nothing. Just the corner of the room, shadows cast by a hutch stacked with china.

"You're tired," I muttered, gripping the edge of the table until my knuckles turned white. "The drive was six hours. You're hallucinating."

I moved to the sink, turned the rusty tap. Nothing but a dry, gasping cough from the pipes. I needed a plan. I needed to secure the perimeter, check the windows, and then maybe find a hotel in town. I couldn't stay here tonight. That was non-negotiable.

I left the kitchen and moved into a sitting room I had missed earlier. This one felt different. Less formal, more intimate. There was a fireplace, cold and ash-filled, and a worn armchair angled toward the window.

On the wall next to the fireplace hung a needlepoint sampler. I stepped closer, lifting the light to examine it. Most houses in this area had them; homages to God or home, usually with cheerful floral borders.

This one was different. The background was a dark, angry red, stitched with coarse thread. In the center, a house was depicted, but the perspective was wrong, twisted. The windows were stitched with black thread, empty and staring, and the door was nothing but a jagged tear in the fabric where the canvas had been cut away.

Beneath the house, the threads formed a sentence. *She sees nothing, she speaks nothing.*

I felt a cold prickle at the nape of my neck. I reached out to touch the rough fabric, my fingers hovering just inches from the tear in the canvas. It looked like a wound.

"Don't touch that," a voice said, low and calm.

I didn't scream. I reacted the way I had trained myself to react for years—instant, defensive motion. I dropped into a crouch, spinning around, my flashlight beam blindingly high as I searched for the threat.

"Who's there?" I demanded. My voice was steadier than I felt.

A man stood in the archway leading to the hall. He was tall, framed by the shadows, looking for all the world like he belonged in the dark. He wore a dark coat, unbuttoned, and his hands were buried deep in his pockets. He didn't look alarmed by my defensive posture. He looked... bored. Or perhaps, mildly curious.

He raised a hand to shield his eyes from the flashlight, a gesture that was annoyingly deliberate.

"I said, don't touch that," he repeated. His voice was deep, smooth, with a dry undercurrent that suggested he found this situation tedious. "The frame is loose. It fell on my father's cat when I was a boy."

I didn't lower the light. "Who are you? And why are you in my house?"

"I'm not in your house," he corrected me, stepping fully into the room. He moved with a casual grace, his gait measured. "I'm standing in the doorway. There's a difference."

I studied him. He was older than me, maybe mid-thirties, with sharp features and hair that looked like he had run his hands through it one too many times. There was a precision to him, though—the way his coat was buttoned, the straight line of his posture—that clashed with the messy hair. He looked like a man who spent his life organizing chaos, including his own.

"And the name?" I asked, my grip tightening on the phone.

"Asher," he said. "Asher Croft. I live next door. Or, I suppose 'next door' is a relative term when the properties are this size. I'm your closest neighbor, unless you count the coyotes."

I lowered the light slightly, keeping it aimed at his chest. "Coyotes generally don't break into houses and give warnings about needlepoint."

"True," he admitted. A faint, dry smile touched his lips, but it didn't reach his eyes. His eyes were searching my face, analyzing me, filing me away in some mental cabinet. "And I didn't break in. The back door was unlocked. It's been unlocked since Ilse died. I've been checking the place once a week. Making sure the local teenagers haven't turned it into a party spot."

I felt a flicker of unease. He had been coming here? Walking through these rooms? Touching things?

"You knew Ilse?" I asked.

"I knew of her. Everyone in Blackwood did. She kept to herself. Not the friendly type." He paused, looking past me at the sampler on the wall. "She was particular about that room. Never liked people touching her things."

I turned my head to look at the sampler again, then back at him. "Well, she's dead now. Things are changing."

"Are they?" He took a step closer, invading my personal space just enough to make the air between us feel charged. Up close, he smelled like rain and expensive tobacco, a stark contrast to the dust and decay of the manor. "You seem... attached to the past, Mara."

I stiffened. "How do you know my name?"

"It's a small town," he said evasively. "And you look like her. Your mother, I mean."

The mention of Lydia was like a bucket of ice water thrown over my head. I took a step back, my hand instinctively going to the scar on my palm. I didn't want to talk about my mother. I didn't want to talk about the resemblance.

"I didn't come here for a reunion," I said coldly. "I came to assess the property. If you've finished your neighborly vigil, you can leave."

Asher didn't move. He stood there, his gaze drifting from my face to the hand rubbing my scar. He noticed the tic. I saw him register it, file it away.

"I heard the radiator," he said, changing the subject so smoothly it gave me whiplash.

"What?"

"In the hall. Just now. Three short, three long, three short." He looked at me with an intensity that made my skin prickle. "Does it do that often?"

My heart skipped a beat. I hadn't told him about the pattern. And there was no way he could have heard it from the next door, not through these thick walls. Unless he had been closer than he claimed.

"It's just the pipes," I said, echoing my earlier lie.

Asher studied me for a long moment, his expression unreadable. Then, he reached into his coat pocket. I flinched, ready to run or fight, but he only pulled out a small, leather-bound notebook and a pen. He scribbled something down, his movements precise and controlled.

"Just the pipes," he murmured, almost to himself. He looked up, his eyes locking onto mine. "Be careful, Mara. Houses have memories. If you stir them up, sometimes they bite back."

"What is that supposed to mean?"

He closed the notebook with a snap. "It means you shouldn't be here alone at night. The floorboards in the attic are rotten. I told the real estate agent that months ago, but apparently, the memo didn't reach you."

I stared at him. How did he know about the attic? How did he know the floorboards were bad unless he had been up there? The logical part of my brain screamed that he was just a helpful, if creepy, neighbor. The instinctual part, the part that had kept me alive through years of anxiety and fractured memories, told me to run.

"I can handle rotting floorboards," I said, my voice hard.

"Can you?" He took a step back, retreating into the shadows of the hallway. "Then I suggest you start by fixing the lock on the front door. It hasn't worked since the nineties."

He turned and began to walk away. I watched him go, his silhouette disappearing into the gloom of the foyer. I waited for the sound of the front door opening and closing, but I heard nothing.

Just silence.

And then, from the wall behind me, the radiator shuddered violently.

*Clank-clank-clank.*

I spun around, my heart pounding. The radiator was silent again, just a hunk of cold iron. But on the floor, directly beneath it, the dust had been disturbed.

Footprints. Small footprints. Too small for an adult.

I shone my flashlight down. They weren't boots. They were bare feet, the toes distinct, leading away from the radiator and disappearing under the heavy velvet drapes of the window.

I backed away, my breath coming in short, sharp gasps. I was alone. I had to be alone. Asher had left, or maybe he was still hiding, playing a game.

I grabbed the edge of the heavy curtain and yanked it back.

The window was shut, locked from the inside. The glass was covered in a thick layer of grime, but through it, I could see the manicured lawn stretching toward the woods.

And in the distance, standing at the edge of the tree line, was a figure.

It was Asher. He was watching the house, his posture perfectly still. He wasn't looking at the front door. He was looking up.

My eyes followed his gaze, slowly, inexorably, traveling up the side of the house to the narrow, gabled window on the third floor.

The attic.

A light flickered in the attic window. Not a bulb. A cold, blue light, pulsing like a heartbeat.

I looked back at the tree line. Asher raised a hand, not in greeting, but in a gesture of warning. He pointed one finger directly at the attic, then turned and walked into the darkness of the woods, leaving me alone with the house, the copper smell, and the blue light glowing above me like a dead star.
