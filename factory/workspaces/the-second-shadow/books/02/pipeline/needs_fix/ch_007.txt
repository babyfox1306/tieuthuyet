# Chapter 7: The Map in the Drawer

"The garden," Asher said, holding the faded map against the lamplight. "Ilse buried something there the night Julian died."

Mara's breath caught. The paper was larger than she expected, yellowed and cracked at the edges, but the ink lines were still sharp—a bird's-eye view of the Vale property. Her aunt's handwriting crowded the margins in familiar slanted strokes.

"Let me see that." She took the map carefully, cradling it with both hands. The paper was soft, almost fragile, and it smelled of dust and old wood. "Where did you find it?"

"The secret compartment in the desk." Asher reached into the drawer and pulled out a small brass key, tarnished with age. "This was taped to the back panel. There's a hidden cavity under the false bottom."

Mara's pulse quickened. She knelt beside the desk, running her fingers along the underside until she felt a small slit barely wider than a needle. She slid the key in. It turned with a click, and a seam appeared in the wood grain.

"Help me pry it open."

Together they worked the false bottom loose. Inside was a shallow recess, no more than two inches deep, lined with dust and cobwebs. Empty except for the map.

Mara lifted it out carefully. It was folded into a tight square, secured with a piece of twine that had darkened with age. She untied the knot and spread the paper flat under the lamp's glow.

The map showed the main house, the overgrown garden, the orchard, and the stream that cut through the eastern edge of the property. Near the woods, someone had drawn a small square with a circle inside and marked it: Old Root Cellar. Beside the symbol, Ilse had written in tight script: August 12—buried what cannot remain. Depth 12 ft, but pushed into wall cavity.

"She says she hid it in a wall cavity, not buried," Mara said, her throat tight. "So the depth is misleading."

Asher leaned closer, his shoulder brushing hers. "She wanted to protect whatever's in there. If someone dug blindly, they'd find nothing."

Mara turned the map over. The back was covered in a faded floral pattern—tiny roses that had once been pink but now were pale gray, their petals barely visible against the yellowed paper. The same wallpaper that lined the guest room upstairs, peeling at the edges, stained by years of damp.

"This paper matches the guest room," she said. "Ilse tore it from the wall."

"Probably at night, when no one could see." Asher's voice was low. "She drew this in secret, under cover of darkness."

Mara traced the roses with her fingertip, imagining her aunt alone in that cold room, scratching a map by candlelight while the truth rotted in the attic above her. Ilse had spent years protecting her. Now it was Mara's turn to uncover what had been hidden.

She looked at the marking for the root cellar. "We need to find this place tonight."

Asher was already flipping through Ilse's journal, matching dates. "Here. August 12, twenty-two years ago. She writes: 'I have hidden the box inside the old root cellar. It contains my sworn testimony of what I witnessed. If anything happens to me, the truth will remain.'"

Testimony. A written account of whatever Ilse had seen the night Julian died. Mara felt her stomach tighten. That testimony could name names. It could reveal what Lydia had done—and what Mara had been forced to forget.

"Read more," she said.

Asher found the next entry. "August 14. 'I have drawn a map and concealed it in the false drawer. Only Mara can ever find it. I pray she never has to.'"

Mara's throat closed. Ilse had hidden this for her, knowing that one day she might need it. And now that day had come.

"The testimony might tell us exactly what happened," Mara said. "Why Julian died. Why my mother ran. Why I can't remember."

Asher's jaw tightened. "And why my brother never came home."

Silence settled between them. Mara could feel the weight of his grief, the chasm of years without answers. They were both searching for the same truth, from opposite sides of the same wound.

Then the lamp flickered.

Mara felt it first—a drop in temperature, so sudden it left goosebumps on her arms. The air turned cold and still. The shadows in the corner of the room seemed to thicken, and a pale blue luminescence began to seep into the dark.

Her blood went cold.

She knew that light.

"You don't see her?" Mara whispered.

Asher looked up, his eyes scanning the room. "See what?"

But Mara's eyes were fixed on the corner. The Girl in Blue stood just behind Asher's chair, barefoot on the hardwood, her faded dress hanging limp on a body that was too thin. Her hair tangled around a face that seemed too old for a child—hollow cheeks, deep shadows under her eyes.

And her eyes—pale, almost transparent—were fixed directly on Mara.

She raised her small hand and pointed at the map. At the root cellar. Her lips moved soundlessly, forming a single word over and over: "Find. Find."

Mara gripped the edge of the desk. "I see you."

The Girl's expression shifted—desperation, maybe relief. She pointed again, more urgently, her small finger jabbing toward the marking. Then she dissolved, fading like smoke into the darkness.

The room returned to its normal temperature. The lamp brightened. The shadows receded.

Mara was trembling.

Asher was watching her carefully. "She was here."

"Yes." Mara's voice came out raw. "She pointed to the root cellar. She wants us to go there now."

Asher set down the journal and stood. He came around the desk and took her hand. His palm was warm, rough with calluses, and he closed his fingers around hers with a grip that was not gentle—it was firm, grounding, a physical anchor in a world that kept shifting beneath her feet.

He squeezed once, hard.

"Look at me," he said.

She did.

"Whatever we find in that cellar, we find together. I don't know what Ilse witnessed, or what your mother buried. But I know this." He held her gaze. "You're not alone in this anymore. I'm with you. Until the end."

Mara's heart hammered. She wanted to pull away, to protect herself the way she always did. But the warmth of his hand, the steadiness in his eyes, made her want to believe.

She squeezed back. "Together."

He released her, but the imprint of his fingers lingered on her skin.

She folded the map carefully and tucked it into her jacket pocket. Asher gathered the flashlight, a crowbar, a small shovel. They didn't speak. Words would only crack the fragile covenant between them.

They moved down the stairs, footsteps soft on the worn carpet. The house creaked around them, but it no longer felt solely like a threat—it felt like a witness, holding breath, waiting.

Mara reached the front door and paused. Through the frosted glass, she could see the darkness of the night. Her hand hovered over the handle.

Then a bright glow swept across the window. Headlights.

A car was coming up the long drive.

Mara's blood chilled. She knew the sound of that engine. The low hum, the slight rattle of a loose muffler. Lydia's sedan. The same dented green car that had pulled out of this driveway twenty years ago, leaving Mara at the window, watching her mother drive away.

The headlights cut across the gate, illuminating the rusted chain and the overgrown hedges. The car idled at the entrance, engine running, lights glaring into the yard.

Mara pressed her palm against the doorframe to steady herself.

"She never gets out," she whispered. "She just sits there, waiting."

Her hand moved to her pocket, where the map lay folded. The weight of it felt heavier than paper should be.

Asher came up beside her, his voice low. "If we leave through the side door, we can circle through the orchard. She won't see us."

Mara nodded. Her legs felt numb as she turned away from the front door and followed Asher through the dark kitchen, past the pantry, to the small service door that opened onto the side lawn.

She stepped into the night.

The grass was wet with dew. The air smelled of damp earth and rotting leaves. The headlights still burned at the gate, but the car remained motionless, a sentinel in the dark.

Mara didn't look back.

She walked toward the stream, toward the woods, toward the root cellar that held Ilse's testimony.

Behind her, the headlights stayed fixed on the empty drive.

But Mara knew her mother.

Lydia had not come to watch.

She had come to stop them.
