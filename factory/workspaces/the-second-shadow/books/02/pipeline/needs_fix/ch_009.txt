# Chapter 9: The Eye of the Storm

Dawn arrived like a thief, pale and guilty, as Mara pulled on her boots and followed Asher into the garden without a word. The grass was slick with dew, soaking the hem of her jeans, and the air smelled of wet earth and something metallic—rust, or maybe blood from a cut she hadn't noticed. She didn't ask where they were going. She just walked, the map from Ilse's desk burned into her memory.

Asher stopped near the gnarled oak at the edge of the property, its roots buckling the ground like knuckles pressing through skin. He pointed to a depression in the earth, barely visible beneath a tangle of blackberry vines. "There," he said. "Root cellar. Has to be."

Mara studied the spot. The ground dipped in a perfect rectangle, as if the soil had settled over a hollow space. "You sound sure."

"I've seen enough old farmhouses to know the signs." He pulled a pair of shovels from the back of his truck and handed her one. The handle was worn smooth, warm from the truck bed. "You ready to get your hands dirty?"

She took the shovel without answering. The metal blade bit into the earth with a wet thud, and she let the rhythm of the work silence the questions hammering in her skull.

They dug in silence for the first ten minutes. The soil was heavy, dark, full of stones and twisted roots that fought every inch. Mara's shoulders burned, and her scarred palms ached inside her gloves, but she didn't stop. Each shovelful felt like prying open a locked door, one grain at a time.

Asher grunted, driving his blade deeper. "You're thinking too loud."

"You can hear thoughts now?"

"Don't have to. You've got that look—like you're trying to solve a murder while digging a hole."

She paused, leaning on the shovel. "Maybe I am."

He gave her a long look, then returned to digging. "Find anything interesting?"

Mara spotted a glint in the overturned soil near her foot. She knelt, brushing away the dirt with her gloved fingers. A small ceramic thimble emerged, still intact, the blue forget-me-not pattern crisp against the white glaze. She picked it up, turning it over. The inside was dark with age, but the flowers were unmistakable.

Forget-me-nots. Ilse's favorite.

"What is it?" Asher asked.

"My aunt's thimble." Mara held it up. "She used to sew. I remember watching her by the fire, her needle flashing in the lamplight." She paused, the memory hazy, like a photograph left too long in the sun. "I don't remember much else."

Asher set down his shovel and came closer, his boots crunching on loose gravel. "That's a good sign. You're remembering something."

"Or I'm finding souvenirs of a life I never really knew."

He didn't argue. He just looked at her, patient and steady, and she felt the weight of his attention like a hand on her shoulder. She pocketed the thimble and stood, brushing the dirt from her knees.

"Let's keep going."

They worked deeper, the hole now waist-high. The air grew cooler, the soil damper, and Mara's arms ached with a satisfying burn. She was about to suggest a break when her shovel clanged against metal.

The sound was dull, hollow, like striking a bell underwater.

Asher's eyes snapped to hers. "We're there."

They cleared the remaining dirt with careful, almost reverent movements. A rusted lockbox emerged, its surface pitted and orange, the lock crusted with corrosion. It was larger than she expected—maybe two feet long, a foot wide—and heavy when they pried it from its earthen cradle.

Asher lifted it onto the grass and examined the lock. "We'll need to break it. No key is opening that."

"Ilse wouldn't have made it easy." Mara watched him retrieve a crowbar from the truck, the metal gleaming in the pale morning light. He worked the tip under the lid and leaned into it.

The lock gave with a screech that seemed to echo through the still garden.

Mara's heart hammered as Asher lifted the lid. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a child's dress, blue as cornflowers, folded with precise, trembling care. The fabric was soft, faded, the stitches small and even—Ilse's work. And beneath it, a yellowed envelope.

Mara reached for the dress first. She lifted it out, and the cloth seemed to hold the shape of a small body, as if it had been worn and laid to rest. She pressed it to her chest without thinking, and a wave of grief rolled through her, so visceral she nearly staggered.

Asher took the envelope. He slid out a sheet of paper, covered in Ilse's spidery handwriting. He read it silently, then met Mara's eyes.

"What does it say?" she asked, her voice barely a whisper.

He handed her the note. She read the words aloud, her tongue stumbling over the confession.

*"I couldn't let them bury the truth with him."*

Mara looked up. The garden was still, the birds silent. The windows of the house stared back like empty eye sockets.

Then above them, a window screeched open.

The sound was raw, violent—a rusty hinge protesting. Mara's gaze shot to the attic window, the one she'd seen dark every night.

The Girl in Blue stood in the frame.

She was holding the same lockbox. The same rusted, mud-caked box. Her small hands gripped it, and her empty eyes fixed on Mara with a patience that stretched beyond years.

Mara's blood turned to ice. "That's not possible."

Asher followed her gaze. His breath caught. "Jesus."

The girl didn't move. She stood there, a mirror of the moment, the box in her hands a twin to the one at their feet. The air grew cold, sharp, and Mara felt the ground tilt beneath her.

She stepped back, her legs unsteady. The dress fell from her fingers, landing on the grass like a fallen petal.

Asher's arm wrapped around her, firm and warm, pulling her against his chest. "I've got you," he murmured, his voice low and steady, anchoring her. "I've got you."

Mara pressed her hand over his, her fingers trembling, her eyes locked on the girl in the window. The same dress. The same box. The same blue dress Ilse had folded with such care.

She didn't know what it meant.

But she knew, with a certainty that cracked through her ribs, that the truth was not buried in the ground.

It was waiting for her in the attic.
