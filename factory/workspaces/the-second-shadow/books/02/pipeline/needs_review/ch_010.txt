# Chapter 10: Wick

The Girl in Blue did not appear in the mirror this time. She stood at the foot of the attic stairs, her small hand pointing not to the garden, but to the ceiling—at the loose board above the rocking chair. She was solid today, more defined than she had ever been, the edges of her gingham dress sharp against the dusty gloom. Mara froze, her breath hitching in a rhythm that felt too jagged for her lungs. Usually, the girl was a blur, a suggestion of a shadow, but now she was a command.

"Mara?" Asher's voice was a low hum behind her, cautious and warm. He hadn't seen the girl. He never did, not unless the house decided to tear down the veil between them, and today, the house seemed intent on keeping its secrets solely to Mara. "You stopped. What is it?"

She swallowed hard, forcing her analytical mind to catalog the anomaly before the panic could set in. The girl was pointing up. The attic was a cavern of forgotten things, smelling of cedar dry rot and old paper. Mara stepped forward, her boots crunching softly on the floorboards. She needed to organize this chaos. She needed to make sense of the geometry.

"She's showing me something," Mara whispered, her voice sounding thin in the vast space. She touched the scarred tissue of her left palm, a grounding technique she used when the world tilted. "Up there. Above the chair."

The rocking chair sat in the corner, a relic of a time when the nursery was actually used. It was still, the air around it heavy and undisturbed. Asher moved closer, the floorboards groaning under his weight, a sound that was reassuringly solid. He didn't ask who she was talking about. He didn't dismiss it. He just watched her, his blue eyes scanning the shadows she indicated.

"The loose board," Asher observed, his tone shifting from concern to recognition. He stepped up to the chair, reaching up to run his fingers along the plank where the Girl in Blue had pointed. "It's barely visible, but the nail holes are empty. Someone pried this open recently. Or a long time ago and never nailed it back properly."

"It's not recent," Mara said, her mind racing through the architectural layout of the house. The attic ran the length of the east wing. The space above the ceiling joists would be shallow, filled with insulation and dead air, or maybe... pockets of storage. "Ilse hid things here. She was paranoid toward the end. She didn't trust banks, she didn't trust people."

"Get the flashlight," Asher said, turning to look at her. The sunlight filtering through the dormer window caught the sharp angle of his jaw, highlighting the dust motes dancing around them. He looked tired, but there was a fire in his gaze, a relentless need for the truth that mirrored her own.

Mara moved to the workbench where she had left the heavy metal flashlight. Her hands were trembling, just slightly. She hated the loss of control, the way her body reacted to the unseen forces in this house. She was an architect; she dealt in concrete and steel, measurable loads and compressive strengths. But this house... this house was fluid. It breathed with her. The anxiety curling in her stomach made the air in the attic feel ten degrees colder.

She returned to the chair, handing him the light. Asher positioned the beam, then with a grunt of effort, shoved the flat edge of a pry bar he had found earlier into the seam of the wood. The sound of wood scraping against wood was grotesque, a screaming friction that made Mara wince. She hated disorder, and this was a violation of the structure, a forced entry into a sealed domain.

With a sharp crack, the board gave way. It swung down on rusted hinges, knocking a cloud of gray dust into the air. Mara coughed, waving her hand, but her eyes were fixed on the dark rectangular cavity revealed in the ceiling.

"Reach in?" Asher asked, handing her the light.

She took it, the metal cold against her skin. She directed the beam into the hole. It was empty, save for a single object wrapped in a faded wool cloth, sitting on the cross-member of the joist. She reached up, her fingers brushing the rough wool. It felt dry and brittle. She grasped it and pulled it down.

The bundle was small, light. She set it on the seat of the rocking chair and unwrapped the cloth.

It was a bear. Or what was left of one. It was battered, its fur matted and worn to the weave in places, a dark, dusty brown. One eye was missing, leaving a frayed hole in the fabric. The other eye was a mother-of-pearl button, its iridescence catching the sliver of light from the attic window, shimmering with a ghostly luster.

"Wick," Mara breathed, the name surfacing from a place she hadn't accessed in twenty years. She had known this bear. She had loved it.

"That's the anchor," Asher said softly, coming to stand beside her. He looked at the toy with a reverence that surprised her. "The thing you lost. The thing holding the memory."

Mara reached out and picked it up. The bear was stiff, the stuffing gone hard with age. As her thumb brushed over the mother-of-pearl button, the attic didn't just get cold. It vanished.

The floor dissolved beneath her feet. The dusty smell of the attic was replaced by the sharp, green scent of a storm rolling in over the ocean. The air was humid, electric, tasting of ozone and salt. She wasn't standing. She was sitting on a high ledge, the stone of the balustrade digging into her legs.

She was eight years old again.

The memory was not a hazy dream; it was hi-definition, visceral, overwhelming. She could feel the wind whipping her hair against her face, stinging her eyes. Below her, the garden was a blur of violent color, heaving with the approaching gale.

Two figures were standing a few feet away on the wide balcony. One was a woman—tall, elegant, her blonde hair pinned up in a style that was severe even then. It was Lydia. She was younger, her face smoother, but her eyes held the same cold, calculating light that Mara had seen in the confrontation days ago.

The other figure was a man. He had his back to Mara. He was gesturing, pleading, his hands open and empty.

"You can't do this, Lydia," the man said. His voice was desperate, cracking under the weight of his emotion. "It's wrong. It destroys everything."

"Everything is already destroyed, Julian," Lydia replied. Her voice was calm, terrifyingly so. A flat line in a chaotic world. "You just refuse to see the rubble."

Mara watched, her small heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird. She wanted to look away, but she was paralyzed, a passenger in her own mind. She knew this moment. She had buried it so deep that it had become a geological layer beneath her consciousness.

"Think of Mara," Julian pleaded, turning slightly. Mara saw his profile—the strong nose, the dark hair, the resemblance to Asher that hit her with the force of a physical blow. This was Julian Croft. Asher's brother. "She's just a child. She needs to know the truth. She needs to know who she is."

"She knows what I tell her," Lydia said. She stepped closer, invading his space. "And if you tell her otherwise... if you poison her mind with your sentimental lies..."

"I won't let you hurt her," Julian said, his voice hardening. He took a step back, toward the stairs. "I'm taking her out of here. Tonight."

The air seemed to still. The wind died for a heartbeat. Lydia didn't shout. She didn't raise her hand in a dramatic arc. She simply moved. It was a fluid, economical motion, a shove to the center of his chest, leveraging her weight and his surprise.

Julian stumbled. His heel caught on the uneven stone of the balcony floor.

Time stretched. Mara saw the realization in Julian's eyes, the betrayal, the sudden, stark terror. He flailed, his hand grabbing for the railing, but his fingers found only air. He went over the edge in a sickening, silent arc.

The scream that tore from Mara's throat in the memory was silent, a raw, ragged sound that never left her lips. She scrambled to the railing, looking down. He hit the stone patio below. There was no doubt, no ambiguity. It was a final, terrible impact.

Lydia stood at the railing for a long moment, looking down. Her shoulders rose and fell once—a sigh, nothing more. Then she turned and walked back toward the French doors, her steps steady. She paused at the threshold, looking directly at the spot where Mara was hiding in the shadows.

"Don't cry, little bird," Lydia said, her voice carrying across the wind. "He fell. It was a terrible accident. You must never forget that."

The trauma of the sight—the broken body below, the coldness in Lydia's eyes—shattered something essential in Mara's mind. The pain was too great, the guilt too heavy. In that instant of fracture, she had torn herself in two. She had locked the memory away in a dark room and thrown away the key. The part of her that remembered, the part that felt the guilt and the horror, had retreated into the shadows, becoming the Girl in Blue, waiting for a time when she was strong enough to look again.

The attic rushed back in a chaotic tumble of sensory input. The smell of cedar, the dust, the dim light. Mara was on her knees on the floor, the bear clutched tight against her chest, her knuckles white. She was gasping for air, her body shaking with violent tremors.

"Mara!" Asher was there instantly, his hands on her shoulders, grounding her. "Talk to me. You're back. You're here."

"It wasn't the fire," she choked out, her voice broken, tearing at her throat. "God, Asher, it wasn't the fire. The fire was... the fire was later. This was first. It was Lydia."

"I know," he whispered, his voice thick with grief. He pulled her against him, wrapping her in his warmth, shielding her from the cold draft of the attic. "I know. I found the records. I suspected, but I needed you to see it."

"She killed him," Mara sobbed, the dam finally breaking. "She pushed him. And she made me watch. She made me keep her secret."

"I've got you," Asher said, rocking her slowly. He smelled of pine and rain, a scent that cut through the dusty memories. "You aren't alone anymore. You don't have to carry it alone."

And then, the Girl in Blue was there.

She stepped out from the shadow of the dormer window. She wasn't transparent this time. She looked real, solid, though she glowed with a faint, pearlescent light. She stood before Mara, looking down with eyes that were filled not with terror anymore, but with a profound, sad sadness.

The girl reached out a small hand and touched Mara's forehead.

The sensation was not an impact, but a merging. A sudden inhalation of breath that filled a vacuum Mara hadn't known existed. The fractured piece of her soul, the terrified eight-year-old girl who had been trapped in the loop of that falling man, slid back into place. It was a click, a lock engaging, a painful but necessary wholeness.

The Girl in Blue faded, dissolving into motes of light that swirled around Mara and sank into her skin. The silence in the attic was absolute. The chaotic coldness vanished, replaced by a stillness that was deep and resonant.

Mara pulled back slightly from Asher's embrace, wiping her face with the back of her hand. She felt different. Lighter, yet heavier with the weight of the truth. But she was one person. She looked at the bear in her hand, the one-eyed Wick, and set it gently down on the floorboards.

"I'm whole," she whispered, the realization settling in her marrow. "She's gone. I'm me."

Asher brushed a stray lock of hair from her face, his fingers lingering on her cheek. His eyes were searching, filled with a tenderness that stripped away her defenses. "Are you okay?"

"No," Mara said honestly. Her voice was steadier now, the analytical part of her mind already cataloging the trauma, filing it away where it belonged—in the past, not haunting the present. "But I will be."

She looked up at him. The connection between them, forged in the fires of this investigation, suddenly ignited into something brighter. She saw the exhaustion in his face, the lines of worry around his eyes, but she also saw the strength. He had stayed. He had believed her when she was fragmented. He had held her while she relived her worst nightmare.

She didn't just want comfort; she wanted him. She wanted the anchor he provided.

"Asher," she said, her voice dropping to a whisper that hung in the dust between them.

"Yeah?"

"I need you."

The words were simple, but they were a surrender. A surrender of the control she had clung to for twenty years. A surrender to the chaos of emotion she had always feared.

Asher didn't hesitate. He cradled her face in both hands, his thumbs tracing the line of her jaw. "I'm right here. I'm not going anywhere."

He leaned in, and the kiss was inevitable. It was slow and tender, a sealing of trust rather than a spark of sudden lust. His lips were warm against hers, soft and searching. It tasted of salt from her tears and the lingering sweetness of the air. Mara closed her eyes and let herself feel it—the texture of his skin, the pressure of his mouth, the steady rhythm of his breath matching hers. It was a long, drugging kiss that pushed the shadows of the attic back until there was only the warmth of his embrace. She opened her fingers against his chest, feeling the steady beat of his heart, anchoring herself in the reality of the living.

For a moment, the house, the secrets, the tragedy—it all faded. There was only this. The quiet certainty of being held.

They broke apart slowly, foreheads resting against each other. Neither spoke. There was nothing to say that the kiss hadn't already conveyed.

Asher pulled her close as the house shuddered. A low groan vibrated through the floorboards, the timber settling into a new configuration. It was the sound of a structure adjusting to a shifted load.

"It's over," she said. She buried her face in the crook of his neck, inhaling his scent. The ordeal in the attic, the years of running, the fractured mind—it was done.
