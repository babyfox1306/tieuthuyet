# Chapter 7: The Shadow Points

Jude’s hand grabbed my arm, fingernails digging in. “Mara, don’t.”

I didn’t look back. The shadow at the edge of the garden had stopped moving. It stood just beyond the broken hedge, a column of deeper darkness against the moonlit grass. And it was pointing.

The arm—if it could be called an arm—lifted with a slow, deliberate motion. The finger extended toward the east side of the house, aimed at a window I hadn’t noticed before. A window that was boarded shut.

“You don’t know what that thing is,” Jude hissed. He tugged, trying to pull me backward toward the house. “We should get inside. Lock the doors.”

But my body was already leaning forward, weight shifting onto the balls of my feet. The night air had gone cold—colder than it had been when we first stepped out here, chasing the second shape I’d spotted from the parlor window. The first shadow had been the dragging presence in the hallway. This one was different. This one stood still, patient, waiting for me to follow.

“It wants to show me something,” I said.

“It’s a shadow. It doesn’t want anything.”

“Then why is it pointing?”

I shook him off and stepped forward. The grass was wet with dew, soaking through the thin soles of my sneakers. The cold bit at my ankles, but I didn’t stop. The shadow remained fixed, its arm held out like a statue’s, pointing at that window. As I walked, it began to glide sideways, mirroring my movement, always keeping the finger aimed at the same spot.

The boarded window was on the second floor, part of a small gabled section that jutted out from the east side. I hadn’t explored that part of the house yet. The boards were uneven, nailed at odd angles, and rust bled down the white paint like old tears. Someone had put them up in a hurry, long ago.

I stopped beneath it, craning my neck. The shadow had reached the base of the wall. It stood there, faceless, featureless, still pointing upward. Then, as I watched, it began to unravel. The darkness frayed at the edges, thinning like smoke caught in a draft. Within seconds, it was gone, leaving only the damp grass and the faint outline of a footprint where it had stood.

Jude caught up to me, breathing hard. “That’s not normal.”

“I know.”

“Mara, we should call someone. Asher, maybe. Or the police.”

I turned my attention back to the window. The boards were old pine, weathered to a silvery gray. Between two of them, a narrow gap let a sliver of moonlight pass through, vanishing into the darkness beyond. And as I stared at that gap, something shifted in my chest. A weight. A pull. A feeling that I had stood here before, in this exact spot, with the same cold air on my face and the same rough wood under my fingers.

Déjà vu rippled through me like a wave. Stronger than I’d ever felt before. Strong enough to make my scarred hand tingle, the twisted lines of skin burning as if remembering something my mind could not.

I’d been here before.

I knew it the way I knew the shape of a familiar voice. The angle of the house. The way the gable cast a long shadow at this hour. My feet had carried me to this spot years ago, and I’d looked up at this window with the same desperate need to see inside.

“What is it?” Jude’s voice sounded distant, muffled by the roaring in my ears.

I raised my hand and touched the rough wood. The boards felt warmer than they should have, as if heated by something inside. I slid my fingers along the edge until I found the gap. The gap was wider than it had first appeared, perhaps an inch across, enough to press my eye against.

I leaned in.

The room beyond was dark, but the moonlight coming from behind me spilled through the crack, illuminating a narrow rectangle of floor. Old floorboards, stained dark. A strip of wallpaper peeled away to reveal yellowed plaster. And there, in the corner, a child curled into a tight ball.

She was small, maybe six or seven, wrapped in a blue dress that I recognized instantly. The same dress I’d found in the wardrobe. The same blue fabric, faded and worn, with a frayed hem. Her face was buried in her knees, but I knew her. I knew the way her thin shoulders shook. The way her small hands clutched the one-eyed bear pressed against her chest.

Wick.

The child lifted her head, and I saw my own face staring back at me. Younger. Softer. Marred by a patch of dried tears on the cheek. Her eyes were the same dark brown as mine, but they held a fear I had not felt in decades. A terror locked away in a place I never visited.

She looked directly at the crack. Directly at me.

Her lips parted, moving around a single word. I read it more than heard it, the shape unmistakable: *Remember.*

My breath caught. I stumbled back, gasping, but my foot snagged on a root and I fell hard onto the damp grass. Jude was there in an instant, crouching beside me, asking if I was okay. I barely heard him. All I could feel was the echo of that word, vibrating in my bones like a struck bell.

Remember.

The child wanted me to remember. But remember what?

I looked up at the window again. The crack was empty now, just darkness and splintered wood. The vision was gone. But the cold spot on my scarred hand remained, and the name she had not spoken clawed at the edge of my consciousness like a living thing.

Jude helped me to my feet, his hands shaking. “What did you see?”

I opened my mouth, but the word that came out was not my own. It slipped past my lips like a breath from someone else:

“Wick.”

He frowned. “The bear?”

I nodded, but my mind was still locked on the child—on the younger me huddled in that room, clutching the bear as if it were the only thing keeping her alive. She wanted me to remember. The shadow had brought me here to show me. And deep down, beneath the fear and the confusion, I already knew what she needed me to do.

I had to go back inside.

I had to find the bear.

And I had to finish what my eight-year-old self had started.
