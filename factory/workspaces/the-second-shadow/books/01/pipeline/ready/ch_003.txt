The eye came loose in her palm, and the name—Lachlan—burned into her thoughts.

She stared at the small glass orb cupped in her shaking hand. It was heavier than it should be. The lamplight caught an imperfection deep inside, a tiny bubble that warped the light and threw a shadow on the wall behind her. A key. The shape was unmistakable—a skeleton key with a curved handle.

Her thumb traced the rough edge of the metal rim that had held the eye in place. The engraving was shallow but legible. Lachlan.

She didn't know that name. But her body did. Her pulse hammered against her skull, and a sharp, metallic taste flooded her mouth. The room tilted.

Then came the flash.

Not a full memory—more like a wound opening. A dark space. Basement walls. A man's voice, low and laughing. The sound of boots on concrete. Then a heavy thud, followed by a woman's scream—Lydia's scream.

No. Not scream. A shout. Angry. Cutting off the laugh.

Mara dropped the eye. It hit the hardwood floor and rolled under the dresser.

She pressed both hands to her temples, breathing hard. The room stabilized. The scent of dust and dried lavender returned. No scream. No basement. Just the quiet hum of the old house settling.

"Easy."

The word came from the doorway, low and calm. Asher stood there, still in his grey coat, watching her with steady eyes. He hadn't left after the porch. She'd forgotten he followed her inside.

She sucked in a breath and forced her hands down. "I'm fine."

"You're white as a sheet."

"That's called porcelain skin. It's genetic."

He didn't smile. He crossed the room, moving with that deliberate quiet that made her want to throw something. He crouched, reached under the dresser, and retrieved the glass eye. He held it up to the light, studying it the way a jeweler studies a flawed diamond.

"There's a name on this," he said.

"Lachlan."

He turned it over, reading the engraving. His jaw tightened. Just a flicker, but she caught it.

"You know that name?" she asked, her voice sharp.

"No."

But the pause before his answer told her something different. She filed it away.

"Give it back." She extended her hand, palm up.

He placed the eye in her hand without argument. His fingers brushed hers, a brief, dry warmth. She shoved the eye into her pocket, refusing to look at it again.

"I need to see something," she muttered, already moving past him toward the study.

The study was a small room off the hallway, crammed with bookshelves and a roll-top desk that looked older than the house. Mara had avoided it the first day—too many papers, too much of Ilse's handwriting. Now she yanked open the desk drawers, rifling through yellowed envelopes and receipts.

"What are you looking for?" Asher leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed.

"A name. Anything." She found a leather-bound journal buried under a stack of tax documents. The cover was soft, worn. She flipped it open near the middle.

The handwriting was Ilse's—slanted, elegant loops. The entry was dated July 14, 2009.

Lachlan came by again. He says he only wants the bear, but I see the restlessness in him. He remembers nothing—but his body remembers. He asked about the attic. I told him it was sealed. He didn't believe me. I should have told him the truth that summer. Now it's too late.

Mara's throat tightened. She read the entry twice, then flipped to another page. August 3, 2009.

He keeps asking about a child. A little girl in a blue dress. He says he saw her in his dreams. My heart breaks for him, but I cannot tell him what happened. I cannot break him the way the truth broke Lydia.

Mara shut the journal. Her hands were trembling.

Asher said nothing, but his presence pressed against her back, solid and waiting. She didn't want to look at him. She didn't want him to see the crack spreading through her composure.

"There's a photo," she said, more to herself than to him. "There was a box of photos in the attic."

She brushed past him, taking the stairs two at a time. The attic door still stood open from earlier. She grabbed the splintered box and carried it down to the hallway, dumping the contents on the rug.

Asher crouched beside her, silent.

She sifted through the images. Most were landscapes, faded, of the house and the lake. A few showed Ilse at different ages, smiling, carefree. Then her fingers touched a polaroid with curled edges.

The man in the photo couldn't be older than thirty. He stood in front of the house, holding Wick by one arm. The bear's single eye caught the sun, making it gleam like a real eye. The man was laughing, his dark hair falling over his forehead. He looked familiar in a way that ignited something cold in her chest.

She flipped the photo over.

Lachlan, 1989.

Her breath stopped.

"Who is he?" Asher asked, his voice low.

"I don't know." But she did know. She knew him the way she knew the shape of a nightmare you've never fully remembered. The way her left hand ached in humid weather, sending phantom pains up her wrist. The way the Girl in Blue watched her from mirrors.

She stood abruptly, shoving the photo and journal under her arm. Her skin felt too tight. The house seemed to lean in, listening.

"I need air."

She walked to the front door and out onto the porch. The night was cold, the moon riding high over the pines. She sat on the top step, hugging her arms, staring at the dark lawn.

Asher followed. He didn't sit beside her, but he stood at the railing, facing the same direction. The silence stretched long enough for her to hear her own heartbeat.

"Lachlan," she said, testing the name aloud. It scraped against something raw inside her. "I don't remember him. But I saw him. In a basement." She paused, breathing through the static in her skull. "He fell. Or was pushed."

Asher turned to look at her, his profile sharp in the moonlight. "You remember that?"

"Not remember. Feel. It's like nausea without an explanation." She laughed, a bitter sound. "I came here to sell a house. Instead, I'm chasing ghosts and digging up a name that makes my bones ache."

"Ilse kept secrets," Asher said slowly. "That was her way. She protected things—sometimes by burying them."

"Or letting them rot."

"She never wanted to hurt you."

"Then why did she leave me this?" Mara pulled the glass eye from her pocket and held it up to the moon. The imperfection cast a small key-shaped shadow on her palm. "Why make me find this?"

Asher moved closer. He didn't touch her, but his nearness cut the wind. "Maybe she thought you were ready to know."

"I'm not."

"You wouldn't be here if that were true."

Mara's throat tightened. She dropped the eye back into her pocket and pressed her palm to her chest, trying to keep the ache inside. "I don't want to be ready. I want to go back to my apartment in Chicago and forget this house exists."

"But you can't."

"No. I can't."

Her voice cracked on the last word. The fatigue, the fear, the creeping certainty that she was losing her mind—it all crashed through her. She buried her face in her hands, shoulders shaking.

Asher knelt in front of her. His hand rested on her wrist, warm and steady. He didn't rush her, didn't offer empty comfort. He just waited.

When she finally looked up, his eyes were close to hers, dark and patient. A muscle jumped in his jaw.

"This is hard," he said. "But you're not alone in it. I'm here."

"I barely know you."

"That doesn't matter right now."

She studied his face, looking for the lie, the angle. She found nothing but a quiet, stubborn honesty that undid her further.

"I don't trust that," she whispered.

"I know."

He lifted his hand to her cheek, his thumb brushing away a tear she hadn't felt fall. The touch was light, a question.

She answered by closing the distance.

Her lips found his, hard and desperate. He inhaled sharply, then his arm wrapped around her waist, pulling her against him. The kiss deepened, raw and searching, a collision of loneliness and need.

They broke apart long enough to stumble inside. The door slammed shut behind them. Asher backed her against the wall, his hands cupping her face, his mouth trailing down her throat. Mara's fingers found the buttons of his shirt, working them open with clumsy urgency.

"I need this," she gasped. "Don't make me think. Just—"

"I know."

He lifted her, and she wrapped her legs around him. They moved toward the bedroom, shedding clothes along the way. The soft click of the bedroom door, then the cool sheets against her back.

Asher moved over her, his body a warm, solid weight. His eyes met hers, searching for permission. She nodded, pulling him closer.

The sex was not gentle. It was raw, a battlefield. She clawed at his shoulders, cursed under her breath when he found the hollow of her hip with his mouth. He was quiet, focused, his breath hot against her skin. She led and he followed, then he led and she surrendered, a push and pull that left her shaking.

Afterward, they lay tangled in the sheets, sweat cooling on their skin. Mara stared at the water stain on the ceiling, her mind numb.

"I can't cry," she said, her voice hoarse. "I want to, but nothing comes."

"Sometimes that's worse."

She turned to look at him. He was on his side, watching her with an expression she couldn't read. She searched for something ugly in him—a bad angle, a moment of weakness. She found nothing.

"Why are you helping me?" she asked.

"I don't know. Because you need help. Because Ilse was good to me once, and this feels like settling a debt."

"That's not romantic."

"That's honest."

She almost smiled. "I'll take honest over pretty words."

He reached out and traced the line of her collarbone. "I'll look into the name. Lachlan. There are old records in the county clerk's office. Births, deaths, property transfers."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. We don't know what we'll find."

She shifted closer, her hand resting on his chest. "I think he died in that basement. I think someone killed him. And I think—" she paused, the words burning in her throat. "I think my mother was there."

Asher didn't flinch. He covered her hand with his. "Then we find out the truth. Together."

The exhaustion finally won. Her eyes grew heavy. She let herself drift, feeling his heartbeat under her palm, slow and steady.

When she opened her eyes again, the moonlight in the room had shifted. The air was colder.

Asher's arm was still around her, his breathing even in sleep. She slipped out from under him, pulling on a robe. Her head ached, but her mind was alert, restless.

She walked into the hallway, drawn by something she couldn't name. The house was quiet, the radiators silent. The only light came from the moon through the window at the end of the hall.

That's when she saw it.

The Girl in Blue stood at the top of the basement stairs. Her dress was a pale, luminous blue, as if lit from within. Her face was white, her eyes dark. She was young—no older than eight. Mara's eight.

She didn't move. She stood like a sentinel, one arm extended, a single finger pointing down into the darkness of the basement.

Mara's blood turned to ice.

"Not yet," she whispered. "I'm not ready."

The Girl in Blue did not look at her. She kept her gaze fixed on the staircase, her finger unwavering.

A cold draft rose from below. It carried a smell—wet earth, old concrete, something metallic and sweet.

The Girl in Blue stepped back, vanishing into the shadows of the basement door.

Mara stood frozen, her hand pressed to her mouth. The basement stairs yawned before her, dark and hungry.

Behind her, the glass eye in her pocket felt warm—almost hot.
