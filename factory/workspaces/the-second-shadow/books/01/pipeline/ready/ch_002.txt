# Chapter 2: The Girl in Blue

The floorboard outside her room creaked at exactly the same time each night—Mara counted—and on the third creak she knew she wasn't alone. It wasn't the groaning of an old house settling into the foundation, nor was it the wind rattling the loose windowpane she had taped up with duct tape an hour ago. It was a deliberate, rhythmic cadence. Step. Drag. Step.

Mara sat up in bed, the duvet clutching at her sweat-dampened skin. She reached for the lamp on the nightstand, her fingers brushing the cool metal before flicking the switch. Nothing happened. The power had died ten minutes ago, the old radiator in the corner hissing its last breath before going silent, leaving the room in a suffocating chill.

"Great," she whispered, her voice sounding thin in the darkness. "Just great."

She swung her legs over the edge of the bed, her bare feet hitting the rough floorboards. She rubbed her left hand, her thumb tracing the jagged scar that ran from her wrist to her palm, a nervous tic she couldn't shake. The logical part of her brain—the part that had gotten her through an accounting degree and a soulless corporate job—ran through the possibilities. A rat? A raccoon? A structural failure?

But the house felt static, heavy with a presence that felt distinctly, unshakably human.

Mara stood up and walked toward the wardrobe, a massive oak monstrosity that Aunt Ilse had left behind. It stood across from the bed, its dark wood absorbing the faint moonlight filtering through the curtains. She had avoided opening it since she arrived, preferring to live out of her suitcase. The wardrobe felt like a mouth waiting to close.

The dragging sound stopped right outside her door.

Mara held her breath, waiting for a knock, for a voice, for the intruder to burst in and reveal themselves as a bored neighbor or a squatter. Nothing. Just the sound of her own heart hammering against her ribs, frantic and irregular.

She turned her attention back to the wardrobe, needing a distraction. She gripped the brass handles—cold enough to burn her fingertips—and pulled.

The hinges groaned, a low, mournful sound that seemed to vibrate in her teeth. The smell hit her first. It wasn't mothballs or dust. It was lavender, dried and crumbling, mixed with the faint, sour scent of old milk.

Mara waved her hand in front of her face, squinting into the dark interior. It was empty, save for a single item hanging in the center: a child's dress.

She reached out, her trembling fingers brushing the fabric. It was blue, a pale, washed-out azure that looked grey in the gloom. She pulled it from the hanger and held it up. It was tiny, meant for a girl of maybe seven or eight years old.

"Why keep this?" she muttered, turning the dress over in her hands. "Ilse didn't have kids."

She brought the dress closer to her face, trying to see the label in the dim light. The seam of the blue dress is still stitched with a label from a store that closed thirty years ago. She recognized the font, the looping cursive of a brand that had been shuttered before she was even born.

A cold prickle walked down her spine. This wasn't just an old dress; it was a relic. A preserved moment of time that had no business hanging in a dead woman's closet.

A soft sound behind her made her spin around.

The room was darker now, the moonlight obscured by a passing cloud. But in the corner, near the window, a shape seemed to coalesce out of the shadows. It wasn't the shifting of light playing tricks on her tired eyes. It was solid, distinct.

A little girl.

She stood perfectly still, her bare feet hidden in the shadows of the heavy curtains. She wore a blue dress identical to the one Mara held in her hands. Her hair was dark, matted, hanging over her face like a curtain.

Mara's throat locked shut. Every logical possibility she had cataloged—rats, wind, hallucinations—shattered against the reality of the figure in front of her.

"Who are you?" Mara managed to choke out. She took a step back, the dress clutched tight against her chest like a shield.

The girl didn't move. She didn't speak. She just stood there, her posture unnervingly still, like a photograph that had gained three dimensions. Slowly, agonizingly slowly, she lifted her head.

Mara expected to see a face. Maybe a ghostly visage, a rotting corpse, something terrifying. But the face that emerged from the shadows was worse because it was ordinary. Pale skin, wide eyes that held no malice, only a deep, drowning sadness.

The girl raised a hand and pointed a trembling finger at the dress in Mara's arms.

Mara looked down at the fabric, then back up. "Is this yours?"

The girl nodded once. A microscopic movement. Then, her gaze shifted. She wasn't looking at Mara's face. She was looking past her, toward the open door of the wardrobe.

Mara followed the girl's line of sight. Inside the wardrobe, on the floor where the dress had been, something glinted. Not jewelry. Glass. A single, glass eye.

The memory of the attic flashed through Mara's mind—the bear, the one-eyed teddy bear she had found trapped in a trunk just hours ago. The bear she had left up there because it gave her the creeps.

"Did you come from the attic?" Mara asked, her voice rising in pitch.

The girl didn't answer. She just stared, her eyes boring into Mara's with an intensity that felt like a physical weight. The silence in the room stretched, taut as a violin string, until a car horn blasted outside, shattering the moment.

Mara flinched, squeezing her eyes shut.

When she opened them a second later, the corner was empty.

The blue light was gone. The girl had vanished. The room was just a room again—dusty, cold, and empty.

Mara slumped against the wardrobe, her breath coming in ragged gasps. She pressed her hand against her chest, feeling the frantic rhythm of her heart. She looked down at the dress in her hands. It was real. The fabric was tangible, the label sharp under her thumb.

"I'm losing it," she whispered to the empty air. "I'm finally losing my mind."

She tossed the dress back into the wardrobe and slammed the doors shut, the sound echoing like a gunshot through the house. She needed a distraction. She needed reality. She needed to hear a voice that wasn't a ghost from a past she couldn't remember.

She scrambled for her phone on the nightstand, the screen blindingly bright in the darkness. She scrolled through her contacts, her thumb hovering over her mother's name. She hadn't spoken to Lydia Vale in six months, not since the funeral where her mother had spent the entire time checking her watch and complaining about the caterer.

But she had to know. She had to know if Ilse had kept a child here. If there was a cousin she didn't know about, a niece, anyone who could explain the girl in the blue dress.

Mara hit dial. The line rang once. Twice.

"Hello?"

The voice was tight, clipped. Her mother sounded awake, which wasn't surprising. Lydia Vale was a woman who treated sleep like an inconvenience.

"Mom," Mara said, her voice shaking. "It's me."

"Mara? Do you know what time it is?"

"It's late. I know. I'm at the house. Ilse's house."

A pause stretched on the line, heavy with static. "I told you not to stay there alone," Lydia said, her voice dropping to a low warning tone. "Sell it, Mara. Tear it down. Just don't... don't stay there."

"I found something," Mara pressed on, ignoring the command. "A dress. A blue dress. In the wardrobe. Mom, there was a girl here. I saw her."

On the other end, the line went dead silent. No breathing. No background noise. Just silence.

"Who is she?" Mara asked, desperation creeping into her tone. "Was Ilse hiding a child? Who is the girl in the blue dress?"

"Mara, stop." Lydia's voice had changed. The impatience was gone, replaced by something sharp and fearful. "Listen to me. You are tired. You are stressed. Grief does strange things to the mind."

"I'm not grieving, I'm confused! I saw her, she was right—"

"There is no girl!" Lydia snapped, the force of the shout making Mara pull the phone away from her ear. "There was never a girl. There is only you, Mara. Do you hear me? Only you."

"What are you talking about? I saw—"

"Don't go looking for things that aren't there," Lydia cut her off, her voice trembling now. "That house... it holds onto things. It holds onto mistakes. You leave them buried. Do you understand me? You leave them buried."

"Mom, you're scaring me."

"Good," Lydia hissed. "Be scared. Be scared enough to leave. Pack your bags. Check into a motel. I don't care where you go, just get out of that house tonight."

The line went dead.

Mara stared at the screen, the call ended notification blinking mockingly at her. Only you. There is only you.

The words spun in her head, dizzying and nauseating. Her mother knew something. She always did. But this was different. This wasn't about controlling Mara's career or her haircut. This was fear. Raw, unfiltered panic.

Mara threw the phone onto the bed and paced the small room. She couldn't stay here. The silence of the house felt predatory now, like the walls were listening to her panic, tasting it. She grabbed her cardigan from the chair, shoving her arms into the sleeves. She needed air. She needed to step outside, just for a minute, to prove to herself that the world still existed beyond these suffocating walls.

She hurried out of her bedroom and down the hallway, her sneakers squeaking on the wood. The stairs groaned as she descended, echoing her mother's frantic warning. Leave them buried.

She reached the front door and fumbled with the deadbolt, her fingers slipping on the cool metal. She threw it open and stumbled out onto the porch, gulping in the cool night air. It smelled like rain and pine, a stark contrast to the stale lavender scent upstairs.

The moon was high now, casting long, skeletal shadows across the overgrown lawn.

"You look like you've seen a ghost."

Mara gasped, spinning around so fast she nearly lost her balance.

A figure was standing at the edge of the porch, half-hidden in the shadows of the climbing vines.

"Asher?" she breathed, her hand flying to her chest.

He stepped forward into the moonlight. He was wearing a dark grey coat, the collar turned up against the chill. His hands were shoved deep in his pockets, his posture relaxed but his eyes alert, scanning her face with an intensity that made her stomach flip.

"Sorry," he said, though he didn't look particularly sorry. He looked curious. "I didn't mean to startle you. I saw the light go out, then I saw you run out here. I thought maybe... I don't know. Maybe the heater died again?"

Mara stared at him, taking a moment to find her voice. Asher Croft. The stranger from yesterday. The man who knew her aunt's name.

"I... yeah. The power is out," she stammered, the lie tasting bitter. She wasn't about to tell him she'd just argued with her mother about a non-existent girl and a haunted dress. He already looked at her like she was a fragile, eccentric city girl ruining a historic landmark. She didn't need to prove him right.

Asher walked up the steps, his movements fluid and quiet. He stopped a few feet away from her, leaving a respectful distance. Close enough that she could smell the faint scent of cedar and rain on him, but far enough that she could breathe.

"It happens," he said, his voice low and soothing. "The wiring in this place is a nightmare. Ilse used to light candles when it went out. Said it added to the atmosphere."

Mara let out a shaky laugh, wrapping her cardigan tighter around herself. "Atmosphere. Right. That's one word for it."

She looked up at him, really looking at him for the first time. In the porch light, his features were sharper. High cheekbones, a jawline that looked like it had been carved from stone. There was a weariness around his eyes that intrigued her, a shadow that suggested he carried his own heavy burdens.

"You okay?" he asked softly.

It was a simple question, but it undid her. Maybe it was the adrenaline crash, maybe it was the fear still humming in her veins, or maybe it was just the sheer relief of seeing another living human being who wasn't a hallucination.

"No," she admitted, her voice cracking
