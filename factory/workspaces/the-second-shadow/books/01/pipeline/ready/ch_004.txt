# Chapter 4: The Basement Door

The basement door creaked open, and the smell of damp earth curled out like a living thing. Mara braced one hand against the frame, the wood rough and cool under her palm, and stared into a darkness that seemed to breathe back at her.

“You have got to be kidding me.” Jude’s voice came from behind her, low and dry. “I thought you wanted a glass of water.”

“I changed my mind.” She didn’t turn. “There’s something down here.”

“There’s definitely something down there. Rats. Mold. Probably a corpse or two.” He stepped up beside her, close enough that she caught the scent of soap and coffee. “Mara, it’s two in the morning.”

“I know what time it is.”

“Then you also know that exploring creepy basements in the dead of night is how horror movies start.”

She finally looked at him. The hallway light caught the edges of his face, the cut of his jaw, the dark hollows under his eyes. He looked tired. She’d kept him awake half the night already, pacing and talking about the name scratched into a glass eye, the flash of memory that had hit her like a physical blow—a man’s face, a scream, the smell of dust and old wood.

He hadn’t said much. Just listened, handed her water, pulled the covers over her when she’d finally fallen asleep on the couch.

Now she was asking him to go into the basement.

“I need to see for myself,” she said. “Ilse must have left more than that bear. There’s a reason she left me this house. A reason I can’t remember coming here.”

“And you think the answers are in the basement.”

“I think they’re somewhere, and I’m tired of waiting.”

Jude studied her for a long moment, his expression unreadable. Then he let out a breath and ran a hand through his hair. “Fine. But if I get bitten by a radioactive spider, you’re in charge of writing my eulogy.”

A laugh escaped her, surprised and sharp. “Deal.”

He clicked on his phone flashlight and aimed it into the gap. The beam carved through the dark, revealing narrow wooden stairs descending at a steep angle. Dust motes swirled in the light like tiny fireflies. The walls on either side were raw stone, beaded with moisture.

Mara stepped onto the first tread. It groaned under her weight.

“Easy,” Jude murmured.

She went down slowly, one hand sliding along the stone wall for balance. The temperature dropped with each step, the air growing thick and heavy. By the time she reached the bottom, the smell of earth and rust was so strong she could taste it.

The basement was smaller than she’d expected. A single bulb hung from the ceiling on a frayed wire, but the chain was too high to reach. Jude’s flashlight swept across the space, picking out an old washer, a corroded boiler, sagging shelves lined with jars of nails and dusty bottles. In the corner, a child’s bicycle lay on its side, one wheel missing.

Mara’s chest tightened. She’d had a bicycle like that. Red with white streamers. She remembered the handlebars being too high, the way her legs burned when she pedaled uphill.

She didn’t remember owning one here.

“Anything look promising?” Jude asked, his voice hushed now, as if the basement demanded silence.

“I don’t know what I’m looking for.” She moved toward the far wall, stepping around a pile of rotted newspapers. The concrete floor was cracked and uneven, littered with scrap metal and splintered boards.

Jude followed, the flashlight beam dancing ahead of her. They searched in silence for a few minutes, the only sounds their footsteps and the drip of water somewhere deeper in the walls.

Mara was about to suggest they go back up when her foot hit a board that didn’t sound right. It thudded instead of scraped. She knelt, pressing on the edges. The floorboard shifted slightly.

“Jude, give me some light.”

He crouched beside her, aiming the phone down. The board was about two feet long, darker than the others, with a rusty nail head at each corner. She wedged her fingers under one edge and pulled. It came up with a groan, revealing a shallow cavity in the concrete beneath.

Sitting inside, arranged as if placed with care, was a small metal box. Blackened with age, no bigger than a hardcover book, with a brass clasp that had tarnished green.

Mara’s breath caught.

“Well.” Jude let out a low whistle. “That’s not nothing.”

She lifted the box out. It was heavier than it looked, and cold against her palms. The clasp resisted, stuck with years of disuse. She worked it back and forth until it gave with a sharp click.

Inside, a photograph lay face-up. A black sedan with a license plate she could barely make out in the dim light—LAC-1. A man in a sharp coat stood beside the car, his hand resting on the shoulder of a child. The child’s face was obscured, blurred by motion or intent. But the man’s features were clear: angular, handsome, with dark hair and a calm smile that felt wrong somehow.

Under the photograph was a sealed envelope, cream-colored, the paper soft and aged.

Mara’s hands trembled as she set the photo aside and picked up the envelope. The front was blank. No address, no stamp.

She turned it over.

Written in faded ink across the sealed flap was a single name.

*Lachlan.*

Her stomach dropped. The word felt charged, electric against her skin. She didn’t know the name—had never heard it before—but something deep in her chest recognized it. Knew it the way the body remembers a wound after the pain has faded.

“Mara.” Jude’s voice cut through the haze. “Let’s take it upstairs. We can open it there.”

She nodded, but she didn’t move. The name burned into her retinas. Lachlan. Who was he? Why was his name on a letter hidden in Ilse’s basement?

Jude reached for the box to close it, and as he did, his flashlight beam swung over the corner where the bicycle lay. For just a fraction of a second, Mara thought she saw something blue reflected in the spokes.

She whipped her head around.

Nothing. Just shadow and rust.

Her heart hammered. She shoved the envelope into her pocket, grabbed the photograph, and let Jude help her to her feet. He closed the box and tucked it under his arm.

“Come on,” he said. “We need to get out of here.”

They moved toward the stairs, but the path was blocked by an old shelf that had collapsed somewhere in the years since anyone had been down here. Wood and glass and rusty tools littered the floor, making the narrow passage even tighter.

Jude paused, frowning at the debris. “There’s a clear path if we go around the boiler, but it’s dark. Give me your hand.”

She hesitated. Her instinct screamed at her to refuse, to find her own way. But the darkness in here was thick, oppressive, and she could feel it pressing against her back, whispering that something was waiting for her to turn around.

She slid her hand into his.

His fingers closed around hers, warm and solid. His palm was calloused, his grip firm but not tight. He led her forward, stepping carefully, his flashlight held high to illuminate the way.

Mara focused on the feel of his hand in hers. On the quiet rasp of their breathing, the scrape of their shoes on concrete. She didn’t look back.

They reached the stairs, and Jude released her hand the moment they stepped into the light. The loss of contact stung more than she expected, but she didn’t let it show.

He climbed first, and she followed, clutching the photograph against her chest, the envelope heavy in her pocket.

At the top of the stairs, in the dim glow of the hallway, she turned the sealed envelope over again.

Written in faded ink on the flap was the name ‘Lachlan.’
