I watched their faces as I laid it out. Sasha's jaw tightened. Priya's hands trembled. Wren's eyes went still. I knew the answer before they spoke.

The basement room of the abandoned bookstore on Clement Street was our most secure location. No windows, one door, a concrete floor that smelled of old dust and drier sheets from the air purifier I'd installed last month. The table was a folding metal rectangle I'd bought at a surplus store for seventeen dollars cash. No digital footprint, no delivery address, no connection to any of us.

I'd printed everything on paper. The article from the Bay City Standard with my sister's photo and Julian Croft's name circled in red ink. A transcript of the voicemail Rhodes had left me, with the phrase from our operational archive underlined. The timeline of Priya's audit request and the flagged death certificate.

"It's not just the voicemail," I said, sliding the papers across the table. "He quoted exact words from the encrypted archive Wren built for us."

Sasha picked up the transcript, her reading glasses sliding down her nose. She was forty-seven, with gray-streaked braids and the kind of face that had won acquittals for men everyone knew were guilty. Her suits were always charcoal or navy, never anything that could be described as bright. She read slowly, her lips moving slightly, and when she set the paper down, her jaw had tightened into something I recognized.

"Pause," she said.

"Excuse me?"

"I said pause, Nadia. Not cancel. Not abandon. Pause."

Priya shifted in her chair. She was smaller than the rest of us, with delicate hands that she kept folded in her lap like she was trying to make herself invisible. Her job at the medical examiner's office required precision and calm, but here, in this room, she looked like she was waiting for a door to break down.

"Pause means what, exactly?" I asked.

"It means we stop everything until we know how he got that information." Sasha tapped the transcript with her index finger. "This is not a lucky guess. This is not a good investigator connecting dots. This is someone who has access to something they shouldn't have."

"Or someone who has access," Wren said quietly.

We all looked at her. She was the youngest of us, twenty-seven, with dark curls pulled back in a messy bun and a laptop that had more stickers than I'd ever seen on a professional device. She'd joined the network two years ago, brought in by Priya after she'd helped a domestic violence case disappear someone's digital trail. Her skills were irreplaceable. Her loyalty had never been tested.

"Meaning what?" I asked.

"Meaning it could be a breach on your end, Nadia. No offense, but your operational security is good, not perfect." Wren's voice was careful, almost apologetic. "If he's as good as you say he is, he could have found a back door."

"Could he?"

"I don't know. I'd need to check."

"Then check."

Wren hesitated. It was small, barely visible — her fingers hovered over her keyboard for a half-second before she pulled her hands back and folded them on the table. "I'll need time. Full access audit, cross-referencing logs, checking every node in the chain. That's not a quick job."

"How long?"

"A week. Maybe two."

"We don't have two weeks."

"Then maybe you need to accept that we can't move forward right now," Sasha said. Her voice had the flat authority of a closing argument. "You're asking us to help you execute a man who has already demonstrated he knows things he shouldn't. The detective who flagged Priya's signature is still circling. If we proceed and something goes wrong, we're all exposed."

"Execution" was a word we didn't use often. It was too clinical, too specific. We called it "the work" or "the project" or sometimes just "it." Sasha saying it out loud felt like a door slamming shut.

"I'm not asking for help," I said. "I'm telling you what I'm going to do."

"Alone?" Priya's voice was barely above a whisper. "You can't do it alone."

"Watch me."

"Don't be dramatic, Nadia." Sasha took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "You need eyes, you need damage control, you need someone to verify the target is where you think he is. You can't do this without us."

"Then come with me."

"Not yet."

"Then when?"

The silence stretched. Priya's hands were trembling now, her fingers interlocked so tightly that the knuckles had gone white. I watched her, waiting for her to speak, and when she did, her voice cracked.

"I can't afford another flagged signature," she said. "The onsite phase is scheduled in three weeks. If I change anything before then, they'll notice. They'll look at every certificate I've signed in the last year. They'll find something. I know they will."

"Priya—"

"I'm not saying no, Nadia. I'm saying I need time. A few weeks. Let the audit pass, let the heat die down, and then we can—"

"He'll be gone in a few weeks."

"He doesn't know about us," Sasha said. "Not yet. He's testing, probing. He left the voicemail to see how you'd react. If you go quiet, he'll assume it worked."

"He'll assume he scared me."

"Good. Let him."

I looked at Wren. She was staring at her laptop, her face unreadable. "Wren?"

She looked up. "I can run the trace. See if I can find how he got the information. But I need full access to the archive infrastructure, the backup logs, the communication records. Everything."

"You already have access to most of it."

"No, I have access to the operational layer. I need the infrastructure layer. The metadata, the timestamps, the access logs for every file in the network."

Her fingers hovered over the keyboard again. That half-second pause, the hesitation before she committed to the movement.

A half-second.

"You can have it," I said. "Tonight."

"I'll start in the morning."

"Tonight, Wren."

She met my eyes. There was something there, something I couldn't quite read — caution, maybe. Or something else. Something I didn't want to name.

"I need sleep," she said. "If I'm going to run a proper trace, I need to be fresh. I'll start at six AM. I'll have preliminary results by noon."

"Fine."

The agreement felt hollow. I could feel the network fracturing, the cracks spreading through the bonds we'd built over years of careful work. Sasha wanted caution. Priya wanted time. Wren wanted distance. And I wanted blood.

I stood up. The metal chair scraped against the concrete floor, a sound that made Priya flinch.

"I'm not waiting," I said. "I'm going to proceed with the plan. If you're in, you're in. If you're out, I understand. But I'm not letting Victor Rhodes win by making me afraid."

"He's already winning," Sasha said. "He's in your head."

"No. He's in my timeline. That's different."

"Same result. You're rushing, Nadia. You're letting him dictate the pace."

"I'm letting him know that I'm not afraid of him."

"Fear isn't the issue. Survival is."

I looked at each of them. Sasha, whose legal mind had saved us more times than I could count. Priya, whose steady hands had made the work possible. Wren, whose digital shadows had kept us invisible.

And I saw it, clear as glass — the fracture that would either heal or shatter us.

"I'll do it alone," I said. "If I need damage control, I'll call. But I'm not going to sit here and let him build walls while I wait for permission to act."

Wren stood up. "I'm in."

The words came fast, almost too fast. I watched her face for the tell, for the sign that she was lying or hedging or buying time, but her expression was smooth.

"I'll run the trace tonight," she said. "And I'll help with the operation. Whatever you need."

"Thank you."

Priya looked at Sasha. Sasha looked at the ceiling. The silence was heavy, thick with things none of us wanted to say.

"I'll wait," Sasha finally said. "I'm not in, and I'm not out. But if something goes wrong, I can't contain it from inside a holding cell."

"Understood."

"Priya?"

Priya's hands were still trembling. She pressed them flat against the table, as if she could steady herself through sheer will. "I can't help with the operation. I'm sorry. But if you need me to look at something, to tell you what happened after the fact... I can do that."

"Thank you."

The meeting dissolved. Sasha left first, her heels clicking against the concrete. Priya followed, her shoulders hunched like she was bracing for impact. Wren packed her laptop, her movements precise and quick.

"Nadia?"

I looked up. Wren was standing in the doorway, her silhouette framed against the dim light of the hallway.

"I'll get you those access logs tonight. And I'll find out how he got to you."

"I know you will."

She nodded, and then she was gone.

I stood alone in the room, the papers spread across the table like evidence of a crime I hadn't committed yet. The article about Mara. The transcript of Rhodes's voice. The timeline of Priya's fear.

A new-email alert sounded.

From: vrhodes@rhodes-investigations.com
Subject: Your Network

I opened it.

"I admire your network, Nadia. Loyalty is rare. Let's see how long it lasts."

I read the message twice.

I read the email until the first rush of fear became something I could examine.

But as I read the email again, I felt something I hadn't felt in a long time.

Doubt.
