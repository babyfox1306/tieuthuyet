I played the voicemail again. The words were mine—not the podcast version, not the public version. The version stored in the network's operational archive.

Victor Rhodes's voice filled my dark apartment, warm and unhurried, the kind of voice that belonged to a man who had never been interrupted mid-sentence in his life.

"Nadia, it's Victor Rhodes. I've been following your podcast on Elise Marchetti's case. Your work is impressive — thorough, clean. The kind of precision that comes from someone who understands how the system really works. I'd love to talk sometime. Call me back when you get a chance."

I let the silence stretch after the message ended. Then I pressed play again.

Third time now. Third time through the same twenty-two seconds of audio, and each pass carved a deeper groove into my certainty.

He'd said clean.

That word didn't appear in any podcast transcript. It wasn't in my press bio, my social media posts, or the interview I'd given to True Crime Weekly last month. Clean was a word I used in the private operational records held inside the encrypted archive Wren had built and administered.

I set the phone down and pressed my thumb into my left palm. The pressure was steady, familiar, a ritual I'd developed years ago when the world first started tilting beneath my feet.

He knew.

The thought should have sent me into a spiral. Instead, it settled into my chest like a cold stone, heavy and immovable. Panic was a luxury I couldn't afford. I needed information.

I called Wren.

She picked up on the second ring, her voice low and professional. "What do you need?"

"A trace. I'm sending you a phone number. I need to know where the call originated."

"Give me five minutes."

I texted her the number from Rhodes's voicemail and waited. The apartment was too quiet. The kind of silence that amplified every small sound — the hum of the refrigerator, the drip of the faucet in the bathroom, the beating of my own heart.

Four minutes and thirty-seven seconds later, Wren called back.

Wren's voice was flat when she spoke. "Burner phone. Purchased three days ago at a convenience store two blocks from Victor Rhodes's office. Paid in cash. No security footage — the store's cameras were down for maintenance."

"Convenient."

"Very. The phone was active for exactly six minutes. It placed one call — to you — and then powered off. It hasn't been turned back on since."

"Can you track the purchase to him?"

"Not directly. No ID, no credit card. But the location is specific. Someone wanted to make sure the call looked like it came from his neighborhood."

I closed my eyes. The picture was forming, sharp and clear. Victor Rhodes hadn't just called me — he'd planned the call. He'd chosen the burner phone, the location, the timing. He'd left nothing to chance.

"Wren, I need to ask you something."

"Go ahead."

"Have you shared language from the operational archive with anyone?"

Silence. A beat too long.

"No," she said. "Of course not."

"Think carefully. Any conversation, any mention of the word clean or the way I structure my case files."

"I said no, Nadia. I know the protocol. Your notes stay in your system."

I waited, letting the silence stretch. Wren was good — better than good — but she had a tell. When she was uncomfortable, she filled the quiet with words.

"Look," she said, "if someone compromised your files, it wasn't from my end. But maybe we should tighten security. I can set up a new encryption protocol, something that flags any unauthorized access."

"There."

"What?"

"You offered to fix it before I asked."

"I'm trying to help."

"I know. I'll let you know if I need that protocol."

I ended the call before she could respond.

The deflection was subtle. Professional. Anyone else might have missed it. But I'd spent years reading people, learning the small tells that revealed what words tried to hide. Wren hadn't answered my question. She'd pivoted to a solution.

I made two more calls.

Sasha answered with her usual clipped efficiency. "Tell me you've made progress."

"Have you shared language from the operational archive with anyone?"

"The encrypted files? No. Why would I?"

"Victor Rhodes used a phrase from my personal case notes in his voicemail."

A pause. I could hear her processing, her lawyer's mind running through the implications.

"I haven't shared anything," she said. "And if I had, I would have told you. That's not how this works."

"I know."

"Do you trust me?"

"I trust that you believe what you're saying."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have right now."

Priya was next. She picked up on the first ring, her voice tight with worry. "Is everything okay?"

"No. Have you shared language from the operational archive with anyone?"

"What? No. Of course not. Nadia, what's going on?"

"I'm verifying security protocols."

"Something's wrong. I can hear it in your voice."

"I'm fine. I'll call you tomorrow."

"Nadia —"

I hung up.

All three of them denied it. All three of them sounded genuine. But one of them was lying, or one of them had been careless, or one of them had been compromised without knowing it.

I didn't know which. And that uncertainty was going to eat me alive.

I locked the apartment door, checked the window locks, and pulled the curtains closed. Then I sat down at my desk and opened the locked drawer.

The notebook was there. Black cover, unlabeled, the pages filled with family dates, interview observations, and thoughts I kept outside the operational system. I checked it because a physical intrusion was still possible, not because it contained the phrase Rhodes had used.

I flipped through the pages, looking for signs of tampering. The spine was intact. The pages were in order. No creases, no smudges, no evidence that anyone else had touched them.

There was no sign that anyone had entered the apartment.

I'd written that word throughout the encrypted archive. It was shorthand for an operation with no evidence, no witnesses, no trail back to me. Only the network could have seen it, and Wren knew the archive better than any of us.

And Victor Rhodes had used it in a voicemail to a woman he'd never met.

I closed the notebook and set it aside. A break-in was no longer the best explanation.

He knew there was a private language behind the podcast, and he wanted me to believe he knew the rest.

He was building a case against me — the same kind of meticulous, evidence-based case I built against my targets. He was documenting, collecting, waiting for the right moment to strike.

And I had no idea how much he had.

I counted backward from five until the certainty stopped feeling like panic.

I was no longer invisible.

For three years, I'd moved through the shadows, a ghost in the system. I'd studied my targets, planned their deaths, and disappeared without a trace. I'd believed that as long as I was careful, as long as I followed the rules I'd created, I would never be caught.

Victor Rhodes had just proven me wrong.

I looked at my phone. The screen was dark, but I could feel it waiting, a silent witness to the collapse of my carefully constructed world.

He'd called me. He'd used my language. He'd shown me his hand just enough to let me know he was holding cards I couldn't see.

The question was: what did he want?

Not to expose me. Not yet. If he had enough evidence to go to the police, he would have done it. The voicemail was a message, not a threat. A way of telling me that the game had changed.

I reached for my phone and opened my email. Nothing new. Just the usual flood of listener messages, interview requests, and notifications from the podcast platform.

I was about to set it down when a new message appeared at the top of my inbox.

Unknown sender.

Subject: Re: Your voicemail.

I opened it.

The body of the email was one line:

"Enjoying the game, Nadia? — V.R."

I read it three times. The same way I'd listened to the voicemail. Searching for meaning, for subtext, for the spaces between the words.

There was no attachment. No signature. No tracking information I could use.

Just a greeting from a man who had already proven he was three moves ahead.

I set the phone down and rested both hands on the cool desk, reminding myself that I was still in control of something.

I was still breathing.

I was still thinking.

I was still dangerous.

But I was no longer alone in the dark.

Victor Rhodes was there with me, watching, waiting, building his case.

And for the first time since I killed Julian Croft, I was afraid.

I whispered the words to myself, letting them settle into the silence of my apartment.

"He knows. And he's not done."

Victor Rhodes's email remained open in the darkness.

Enjoying the game, Nadia?

I closed my notebook, locked the drawer, and read the message until the words blurred.

The hunter was now the hunted.

And the game had only just begun.
