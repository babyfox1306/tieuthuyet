I had never met him, but I felt like I knew him. That's the tragedy of these cases — we only meet them after they're gone.

The words slid out of me like honey, warm and measured. I rested my fingers against the microphone stand, feeling the vibration of my own voice through the metal. The recording light glowed red. I was in my element.

"Marcus Webb was fifty-three years old. He owned a small accounting firm in Portland. His neighbors described him as quiet, generous, always willing to help with a charity drive." I paused, letting the silence stretch. "He was found dead in his home three days ago. The medical examiner ruled it a cardiac event."

I let my voice soften. "But here's what the official report won't tell you."

I folded one hand beneath the desk and kept my voice level.

"Marcus Webb's last client before his death was a man named Gareth Pence. Mr. Pence had been investigated twice for fraud against elderly investors. Twice, charges were dropped. Insufficient evidence. Good lawyers. A system that protects the wealthy."

I leaned closer to the mic. "I'm not saying Marcus Webb's death is suspicious. I'm saying that when the people who hurt the most vulnerable keep walking free, sometimes the universe finds a way to balance the scales."

There. The seed was planted. The audience would hear compassion. The audience would hear grief. They would hear a journalist struggling with the unfairness of it all.

They would not hear the woman who had slipped into Marcus Webb's house at midnight and watched his face go slack after concentrated potassium entered through the cannula in his arm. The puncture and a targeted investigation could have exposed me. Priya's false certification—not invisible chemistry—was what kept the question closed.

"Rest easy, Mr. Webb," I said, my voice dropping to barely a whisper. "You deserved better than what life gave you."

The recording light went green. I hit stop.

The silence of the booth rushed in. I pulled off my headphones and let them hang around my neck. The room smelled like old carpet and electronics. I'd spent hundreds of hours in this booth, but every time I emerged, I felt like I was surfacing from deep water.

My phone vibrated on the desk.

Sasha Okafor.

I swiped to answer. "Hey."

"The package arrived safely," she said. Her voice was neutral, professional. She could have been talking about a shipment of office supplies.

"Any issues with delivery?"

"None. The courier cleared, the route held, the paperwork is filed." A pause. "The medical examiner released the body without autopsy. The family has scheduled cremation."

"Good."

"They're calling it a tragedy. The local news ran a segment about how stress kills even the healthiest among us."

I smiled. "Stress is a killer."

"It is." Another pause, longer this time. "You sure you're okay? You sound tired."

Sasha was the only person who asked that question and meant it. She had been my lawyer since before Mara died, before everything fell apart and I rebuilt myself into something new. She knew what I was. She had chosen to help anyway.

"I'm fine. Just finishing up the episode."

"All right. Dinner next week?"

"Wednesday."

"Wednesday. I'll bring wine."

The line went dead.

I kept the phone in my hand while Sasha's coded confirmation settled in my chest like a stone dropping into still water. Another kill. Another closed file. Another guilty man who would never hurt anyone again.

Marcus had been the second man. Daniel Ortega, whose garage death I had covered that morning, was the third. Julian Croft came before both. The order mattered because I could still remember when one death became a pattern.

Marcus Webb wasn't just an accountant. He was the architect of a scheme that bled three dozen elderly people out of their life savings. He laundered the money through shell companies, offshore accounts, and a charity that existed only on paper. His victims died broke, alone, and ashamed. One of them, a woman named Helen Yarrow, took her own life when she realized she'd lost everything.

Marcus Webb walked free. His lawyer argued that his victims were "financially naive" and that he was simply a better businessman. The judge agreed.

So I fixed it.

I put my headphones back on and pulled up the podcast's comment section. Fifty-three new comments had appeared since the teaser dropped that morning. I scrolled through them quickly.

"Finally someone talking about this!"
"Marcus Webb was a saint, how dare you—"
"Love your show, Nadia. Keep digging."

And then I saw it.

A comment from a user named "Listener_47".

"Your voice has a catch when you say 'the system's blind spot.' Like you're remembering something personal. Interesting tic."

I stopped scrolling.

Because I had never said "the system's blind spot" on air. Not in the teaser. Not in any previous episode. The phrase existed only in my encrypted operational archive—the system Wren had built and administered for me. I held the root key, but she knew the architecture better than anyone alive.

I read the comment again. Then again.

It was a coincidence. It had to be. Someone had guessed. Someone who listened closely, who read between the lines. There were always listeners like that. People who thought they were detectives.

I scrolled past it.

The next comment was from a user named "MarcusFan2024": "Did anyone else notice the pause at 3:47? Like she forgot which version of the story she was telling?"

Someone replied: "It's just a tic. Leave her alone."

I closed the comment section.

Comments about my pauses had been there for months. People noticed. People always noticed. But they never connected them to anything. They were treated as quirks. Humanizing details.

I made a note to be more careful. Then I deleted the note. I'd been careful for three years. Until that morning, it had seemed to be enough.

The truth was, I wanted them to notice. I wanted someone to look at me and see what I really was. That was the part of me still desperate for connection, still broken from the night I found Mara's body.

I pushed the thought away.

The screen lit again. A voicemail notification. Unknown number.

I hesitated. Then I pressed play.

The voice that came through was male, calm, unhurried. The kind of voice that filled a room without trying.

"Ms. Cole. I'm a fan of your work. I've been listening to your podcast for months now, and I have to say — your attention to detail is remarkable. The way you weave together the threads of a case, the way you give voice to the voiceless. It's genuinely impressive."

A pause.

"I also have to say — I understand you better than you think. The way you talk about 'the system's blind spot'? I know exactly what you mean. Because I've been watching the same blind spot for years. From a different angle, perhaps. But the same blind spot."

Another pause. Longer this time. I could hear him breathing.

"I'd like to talk to you. Off the record. I think we have more in common than you realize."

The line went dead.

The voicemail remained on the screen, the caller ID reading only "Unknown." I played it again. Then again.

The third time, I focused on his voice. Not the words. The voice. There was no aggression in it, no threat. It was the voice of a man who was completely, utterly certain of himself.

I saved the voicemail. I didn't know why.

I told myself it was just a fan. A weird fan. There were always weird fans.

But the phrase — "the system's blind spot" — echoed in my head. I'd never said it on air or to anyone outside the network. It was private shorthand in the encrypted operational archive for the gap between what the law allowed and what justice demanded.

No one should know that phrase.

I looked at the comment again. "Listener_47."

I looked at the voicemail again. "Unknown caller."

Coincidence. It had to be.

I closed the app and stood up. The booth felt smaller than it did a minute ago. The walls felt closer.

I grabbed my bag and walked out into the hallway. The studio was empty that late. The lights were dimmed. My footsteps echoed on the linoleum.

I unlocked my office and closed the door behind me.

The file was waiting on my desk. A manila folder, thick with papers. The label on the tab read: "Marchetti, Elise."

I sat down. The chair creaked.

I opened the file.

The first thing I saw was a photograph. A woman with dark hair and a kind face, her smile warm and unguarded. She was standing in front of a garden, squinting into the sun. She looked happy. She looked like someone who believed the world was fair.

Below the photograph, a coroner's stamp.

"Cause of Death: Ligature Strangulation."

"Manner of Death: Homicide."

I traced my finger over the stamps. Homicide named what had happened without naming who had done it.

Elise Marchetti was twenty-eight years old. She worked at a bookstore in Portland. She volunteered at a women's shelter on weekends. She had no enemies, no debts, no criminal record.

She also had a boyfriend named Victor Rhodes, then a chief deputy prosecutor. He had since left public service to host a true-crime show and run a private investigations consultancy.

Victor Rhodes, who was questioned three times and never charged. Victor Rhodes, who had a perfect alibi and a perfect smile and a perfect lawyer. Victor Rhodes, who walked out of the station and into the sunlight while Elise's body was still in the morgue.

I knew this case. I'd studied it for months. But seeing the file on my desk, holding the photograph in my hands — it made it real.

I turned to the second page. A crime scene photo. Elise's apartment, photographed from the doorway. An overturned chair. A shattered vase. A smear of blood on the wall.

The official report says there was no sign of forced entry. No signs of struggle beyond the overturned furniture. No DNA evidence that didn't belong to Elise or her known associates.

Unofficially, the detective assigned to the case wrote in his notes: "The scene was too clean. Someone controlled the narrative."

I closed the file.

My phone was still in my hand. The voicemail was still saved. The comment was still there.

I should have deleted both. I should have forgotten the voice on the voicemail and the stranger who knew my private phrase. I should have focused on Elise Marchetti, on Victor Rhodes, on the case that would define my next season.

But I couldn't shake the feeling that someone was watching me.

I looked at the photograph again. Elise's kind face. Her trusting smile.

I would find out what happened to you, I thought. I would find out who took you away from this world.

And when I did, I would make them pay.

I folded my hands.

One. Two. Three.

The night was quiet. The door was locked. The file was open.

And somewhere out there, a man who knew my private thoughts was waiting for me to make the next move.
