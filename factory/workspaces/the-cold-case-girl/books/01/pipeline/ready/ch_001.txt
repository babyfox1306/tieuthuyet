The third time I killed a man, it was almost boring.

I eased the compression nut on the gas line loose with my gloved fingers. A fraction of a turn past the seal was enough to create a slow leak without leaving the obvious bite of a tool. The nitrile gloves reduced what I might leave behind, though they did not make me invisible.

The man himself was already unconscious in his garage. Sleeping pills in his evening whiskey, standard dosage, nothing exotic. He'd come home late from a bar, stumbled into his house, and passed out behind the wheel of his sedan with the garage door closed. That was the story the scene would tell.

I'd spent three weeks learning his routines. He left for work at 7:42 every morning. He stopped at the same gas station for coffee. He never checked his mail until Sunday. He was predictable, which made him easy.

And he was guilty.

The evidence was in a folder back in my apartment, locked in a fire safe behind a false wall. Photos of a woman named Teresa Mendez, aged twenty-four, found in a motel room with ligature marks on her wrists. The man in this garage had been arrested for her murder. The case had collapsed when a key witness recanted under pressure from his attorney. The DA's office had kicked it to the cold case unit within six months.

I'd found the witness. A terrified woman living under a different name in Arizona. She'd told me the truth, sobbing into her coffee at a truck stop diner. The man in this garage had threatened her daughter. She'd lied on the stand to protect her child.

The system had failed Teresa Mendez. The system had failed her family.

I wouldn't.

I checked my watch. 11:14 PM. My estimate put the gas at an ignitable concentration in approximately forty minutes. The water heater's pilot light should have done the rest. "Should" was doing work no calculation could guarantee; a draft, a failing pilot, or a neighbor opening the side door could still turn the scene against me.

Clean. Precise. Untraceable.

I slipped out the side door and walked three blocks to where I'd parked my car—a nondescript gray sedan registered to a shell LLC with a mail drop and no public link to me. Three miles away, I stripped the gloves into a greasy takeout bag and buried it beneath spoiled food in a commercial dumpster scheduled for collection before dawn. Collection was not destruction. It was another uncertainty I had decided to live with.

By morning, the news would report a tragic accident.

By morning, I'd be on air, voice warm and sympathetic, telling my listeners that another family had lost someone too soon.

The duality sat comfortably in my chest.

---

My podcast studio was a soundproofed room in my apartment's second bedroom. Acoustic panels on the walls, a Shure SM7B microphone on a boom arm, a Focusrite interface that cost more than most people's rent. Everything looked professional because it was. I wasn't a vigilante who played at being a journalist. I was both.

I sat down at my desk at 8 AM, pulled up my notes, and recorded the episode in one take.

"Good morning, everyone. This is Nadia Cole, and you're listening to The Unheard. I'm coming to you with a heavy heart today."

I let my voice soften, let the empathy bleed through. My listeners trusted me because I sounded like I cared. Because I did care—just not in the way they thought.

"Late last night, a man named Daniel Ortega passed away in a tragic accident at his home. Many of you may remember Daniel's name from the headlines. He was the prime suspect in the murder of Teresa Mendez, a case that went cold when the evidence couldn't hold."

I paused, letting the weight settle.

"I won't pretend to know what happened in that garage. What I do know is that Daniel Ortega was a free man when he died, despite the accusations against him. And Teresa Mendez's family is still waiting for answers they may never get."

I let a beat of silence hang.

"This isn't justice. It's not closure. It's just... an ending. A messy, unsatisfying one. And I think we all deserve better than that."

I kept my voice steady. Warm. Professional. The voice that had built a following of over two hundred thousand subscribers. The voice that had convinced three different forensic experts to share case files they shouldn't have.

The voice that had talked a grieving mother into handing over her daughter's diary, page by page, over the phone.

I finished the episode with my usual sign-off—a reminder that the system was broken, that we had to keep pushing, that every voice mattered. Then I switched off the microphone, saved the file, and uploaded it to my podcast host.

Within an hour, it was live.

I spent the rest of the morning responding to comments. Most were supportive. Some were skeptical. A few accused me of sensationalizing tragedy for ratings. I deleted those with a click and moved on.

Then I saw the comment that made me stop.

It was from a user called "TruthSeeker_42". Their profile photo was a generic question mark. The comment read:

"Did anyone else notice Nadia rubbing her thumb over her palm during the recording? Like, three times? Probably just a nervous habit. But weird, right? She never does that. Anyway, RIP to the family."

I read it twice.

I read it a third time.

My thumb was already moving across my left palm, a smooth, unconscious motion. I forced my hand flat against the desk.

Probably just a nervous habit.

Right. That was all it was. A tic. A tell. Something I'd developed years ago, after Mara died, when I started making decisions that couldn't be unmade.

Most people wouldn't notice. Most people didn't pay attention to the small things.

But "TruthSeeker_42" had noticed. And they'd pointed it out in a public forum.

I closed the comment thread and made a mental note to be more careful with my hands on camera. Then I opened my research files and started preparing for the next target.

Victor Rhodes.

His file was thin. Deliberately thin. He was a man who knew how to stay off the radar—no social media presence, no public records that mattered, no digital footprint worth following. He'd been questioned in the murder of Elise Marchetti six years ago. He'd never been charged. The case had gone cold before it was ever warm.

But I knew what I knew. He was responsible for her death. He'd walked free. And he was still out there, breathing, living, probably laughing at a system that couldn't touch him.

I couldn't kill him yet. I didn't have enough. My rule was simple: provably guilty, beyond any shadow of doubt. I needed evidence that would hold up in a court of law, even if I never intended to take it to a courtroom.

I needed to get close to him. To study him. To find the crack in his armor.

I started by pulling up his podcast appearance from three years ago. A true crime show called "The Last Word". He'd been interviewed as a "person of interest" in the Marchetti case, and he'd handled it with the smoothness of a man who'd rehearsed every answer.

I played the clip.

"Mr. Rhodes, do you have any comment on the recent renewed interest in Elise Marchetti's murder?"

Victor's voice was calm. Measured. Almost friendly. "I've said everything I have to say about that case. I cooperated fully with the investigation. I have nothing to hide."

"You were the last person seen with her."

"I was one of the last people seen with her. There's a difference."

He tilted his head when he said it. A subtle gesture, almost apologetic. Like he was sorry the interviewer had to ask such a stupid question.

I watched the clip four times. I watched his eyes, his hands, the way he breathed. He was good. Patient. Controlled.

He was exactly the kind of man I specialized in.

I closed the file and stretched my neck. It was late. Nearly midnight. The adrenaline from the Ortega job had faded, leaving behind a hollow fatigue that no amount of coffee could fix.

I went to bed and dreamed of Mara's face.
