I opened the file for the fourth time that week. Mara's diary entry was on top—the one where she mentioned the developer who came to the house. I read it again, and I felt the old rage rise, cold and familiar. This was where it started. This was where I became what I was.

The safe house smelled like stale coffee and my own sweat. I hadn't slept in thirty hours. The file sat on the metal table under a single bulb, its contents spread like evidence from a crime scene I'd already committed. Mara's handwriting was still familiar — the loop on her lowercase g, the way she dotted her i's with tiny circles. She'd been twenty-three when she wrote this. Four months before Julian Croft killed her.

"June 12th. Mom invited some developer to dinner. He was nice enough, I guess. Talked about building condos near the waterfront. Nadia would've hated him — she always knows when someone's pretending to be something they're not. I don't know. Maybe I'm too trusting."

I read the line again. "I don't know. Maybe I'm too trusting."

The memory came in fragments, the way it always did. Not a coherent narrative—just flashes from about three years ago.

The marina at midnight. The smell of diesel and salt. Julian Croft's yacht rocking gently against the dock, its hull gleaming under a half-moon. He'd been drinking—I'd watched him empty three glasses of whiskey at the bar before he boarded alone.

The engine compartment was cramped and warm. I found the fuel line by touch and closed the wrench around its brass coupling.

I remembered the slight give as the coupling's seal broke and the walk back across the dock. I was in the parking lot before Julian turned the ignition. The explosion came forty-five minutes later.

The memory ended there, with the fire reflected in the windshield and the cold certainty that I'd done the right thing.

I opened my eyes and looked at the file again. The diary. The photos of Julian Croft's face — the ones I'd taken from his social media before he died. The newspaper clipping about the boating accident that had taken the life of a "respected developer." The police report that had ruled it accidental after a cursory review.

I counted my breaths until the memory receded.

I reached for my phone and dialed Sasha's number. She picked up on the second ring.

"Nadia." Her voice was clipped, professional. "I was about to call you."

"Tell me about the lawsuit."

"It's ready." I heard papers shuffling on her end. "I've drafted the complaint and prepared the docket sheet, but nothing is filed yet. It alleges fraud, breach of contract, intentional infliction of emotional distress—the usual kitchen sink. But the timing is risky."

"How risky?"

"Rhodes has a legal team that's not stupid. If they dig into the plaintiff's background, they'll find the shell company is only two months old. That's a red flag. And if they depose the plaintiff — "

"He won't hold up."

"He won't," Sasha agreed. "I've vetted him. He's a good actor, but he's not a professional. Under oath, with Rhodes's lawyers in the room, he'll crack."

I looked at Mara's diary entry. "Maybe I'm too trusting."

"How long can you stall?"

"A week. Maybe ten days. After that, the court will push for discovery, and we'll be in dangerous territory."

"Make it work for ten days."

"Nadia." Her voice softened. "What's your endgame here? You're building all these contingencies, but you haven't told me the final move."

"I don't have a final move yet."

"That's not like you."

I ended the call. Sasha was right — it wasn't like me. I always had a plan. But Victor Rhodes had made me improvise, and improvisation meant mistakes.

I called Priya next. She answered with a low voice, the way she always did when she was at work.

"The audit?"

"Ongoing." She paused. I heard the click of a keyboard in the background. "It's routine, Nadia. The state sends someone every eighteen months to check our protocols. They're looking at chain-of-custody forms, not autopsy signatures."

"Are you sure?"

"I'm sure." Her voice tightened. "I've been doing this for twelve years. I know the difference between a routine audit and an investigation. This is still the first."

"But the timing — "

"Is coincidental. I checked the schedule. They flagged my office six months ago, before any of this started. It's a computer rotation, nothing more."

I wanted to believe her. I wanted to believe that the universe wasn't conspiring to collapse my world at the exact moment I needed it most. But I'd stopped believing in coincidence the night I killed Julian Croft.

"Keep me updated," I said.

"Of course. And Nadia — "

"Yes?"

"Be careful. You sound tired."

I hung up without answering. Tired didn't begin to cover it.

The last call was Wren. She picked up on the first ring, her voice the usual low murmur of someone who spent too much time in dark rooms.

"Tell me about the breach."

"It's mapped." She sounded distracted. I heard typing in the background. "The public production floor uses keycards, motion sensors, and a camera loop that overwrites every forty-eight hours. The evidence suite is separate—biometrics and offline storage. I can get you into the outer offices, not into that suite."

"When?"

"Any night this week. But there's a window from two to four AM when the cleaning crew is active. That's your best cover."

"How do I get the keycard?"

"I'm working on that." The typing stopped. "The office manager's name is Diane Porter. She's fifty-three, divorced, lives alone. She leaves her purse in her car during her lunch break. I can clone the card if I can get physical access for thirty seconds."

"Can you get it?"

A pause. Longer than I expected.

"I can try," she said.

Something in her voice made me pause. Wren was never uncertain. She was the one who said "I can do it" or "I can't," never "I can try."

"Is something wrong?"

"No." Too quick. "Just tired. I've been running surveillance on Rhodes's digital footprint. The man leaves no ordinary trail—no burner phones, no encrypted accounts, no dark-web presence. It's almost like he knows someone's watching."

"Maybe he does."

"I know." Another pause. "I'll have the keycard in two days. I'll let you know when."

She hung up before I could say goodbye.

Wren's hesitation echoed after the call. I'd worked with her for two years. She'd never said, "I can try."

I pushed the thought aside. Paranoia was a survival tool, but it was also a poison. If I started suspecting everyone, I'd collapse the network myself.

I looked at the file again. Mara's diary. The developer who came to the house. Julian Croft, who'd smiled at my mother and charmed my younger sister and then strangled her with her own scarf in her apartment.

I closed the file. The cardboard was soft from years of handling, the corners frayed. I ran my hand over the surface and felt everything I'd carried since that night on the boat.

"I started this for Mara," I whispered.

The words hung in the air, thin and fragile.

"I will finish it for myself."

A message arrived. I expected Sasha or Priya.

Unknown number.

I opened the message.

"The first audit sweep found nothing. But I know what you did, Nadia. I know about the boat. I know about the fuel line. I know about Julian Croft."

The room went cold. The words blurred and sharpened, blurred and sharpened.

I knew who sent it. I didn't need to check the number.

Victor Rhodes.

He wanted me to believe he knew everything.

Another message followed from the same number.

"You're not the only one who keeps files, Nadia. I have yours. And I'm not done reading."
