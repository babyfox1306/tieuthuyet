# Chapter 3: The Reflection

Day two. 06:14. Lena. Status: unaware.

I wrote the words before I saw her. Before I understood that the status light reflecting in my own window had already told her everything I was trying to hide.

The camera was mounted at 05:47, tucked into the corner of the courtyard-facing unit I'd rented through a shell corporation Grant had set up. Three rooms, furnished in beige nothingness, with a clear line of sight to Lena's balcony and kitchen window. The telephoto lens sat on a tripod I'd positioned behind blackout curtains I never fully closed—just a sliver, just enough.

I checked the status light three times before I sat down. Green meant the camera was recording. Red meant something was wrong.

It was green.

The courtyard sat empty at 06:14. The fountain in the center hadn't started its morning cycle yet. The bird that lived in the palm near the east wall was still asleep. I adjusted the focus, tested the zoom, and watched her bedroom window, which was dark.

At 06:22, the light came on.

She appeared at 06:31, wearing a gray robe I'd seen in Grant's photographs—the ones he'd included in the contract packet as evidence of her "declining state." The robe was clean, pressed, tied at the waist with a precise knot. She walked to the kitchen, and I watched her fill the kettle with the exact amount of water a single cup would require. Not a tremor. Not a hesitation.

I wrote it down. "06:31: Wakes. Routine appears stable. No visible distress."

She took her coffee to the balcony at 06:47. I watched her stretch—arms overhead, spine curved into a slow arch, neck rolling in a circle that ended with her face turned directly toward my window.

Not startled. Not searching.

Still.

Her eyes held the position for exactly three seconds before she lowered her arms and sipped from the mug. I knew because I counted. Because my hand had frozen over the notebook, the pen suspended mid-word, and I couldn't move until she looked away.

I forced myself to write the time. "06:47: Balcony stretch. Subject oriented east."

But the word "east" was wrong. She'd oriented toward me. Toward the dark glass where a man with a camera was holding his breath.

I told myself it meant nothing. She was stretching. People look at nothing when they stretch. It's a gaze without target, a muscle release, a blank space.

I told myself that, and I almost believed it.

---

She showered at 07:03. I turned off the camera. That was the rule I'd set for myself—the line I wouldn't cross, the boundary that kept this professional. The bathroom window was frosted anyway. I wouldn't have seen anything worth recording.

But I heard the water through the open balcony door she'd left cracked. The sound of it running, the rhythm of a person moving through a routine they'd performed a thousand times. Grant had asked me to document "erratic behavior." He'd used that phrase in every message so far. "Document her erratic behavior, Reed. The judge needs to see the pattern."

I'd recorded nothing erratic yet.

At 07:28, she emerged dressed—dark jeans, a cream sweater, hair still damp and pulled back. She ate at the kitchen counter. Toast. An egg. She cut the egg into quarters before she ate it, and she wiped the counter three times after she finished.

"07:28: Breakfast. Neat. Controlled."

I didn't write the word "controlled." It wasn't a clinical observation. But I thought it, and I couldn't stop thinking it.

She left for her walk at 08:04. I watched her cross the courtyard, pass under my window, and disappear through the gate that led to the street. She didn't look back.

I checked the camera feed. Still green.

---

She returned forty-seven minutes later.

I saw her enter the courtyard gate at 08:51, and I positioned myself behind the lens, expecting the same smooth trajectory—gate to door, key in lock, disappear inside. That was the pattern. That was what she'd done yesterday.

She didn't do it today.

She stopped at the threshold.

The courtyard threshold. The line between the open space and the covered walkway that led to her building. Her foot lifted, hesitated, and came down on the same tile.

Her head turned.

Not toward the camera. I was ready for that—I'd angled the lens so it was hidden behind the balcony railing of the unit below mine, invisible from ground level. She couldn't have seen it from where she stood.

But she wasn't looking at the camera.

She was looking at my window.

At the reflection of the camera's status light, which was green but which would have caught the morning sun at exactly this angle. Which would have thrown a faint red glow into the glass where a man was sitting in the dark.

I stopped breathing.

She looked at the camera first. A direct line of sight to the railing where I'd concealed it. Her eyes tracked across the courtyard, found the lens, and held.

Then she looked back at my window.

Then she walked inside.

No hesitation after that. No second glance. She opened the door, stepped through, and closed it behind her. The lock clicked. The courtyard was empty.

I sat there for thirty seconds, my hands resting on the camera body, my heart beating in a rhythm I didn't want to name.

"Coincidence," I told myself. "She saw a reflection. A bird. A shadow. Something meaningless."

I opened the footage and played it back.

The angle was good. I could see her face clearly—the slight squint as she registered the status light, the way her jaw tightened when she located the camera. The pause was 2.4 seconds. I measured it. Frame by frame.

Her lips moved. I couldn't read them.

I zoomed in on the reflection in my own window. The faint red glow was there, visible from the courtyard threshold where she'd stopped. I hadn't noticed it before. I'd been sitting in the dark for an hour, and my eyes had adjusted. But from outside, from her angle? It would have been obvious.

She hadn't looked at the camera first.

She'd looked at the light in my window. Then at the camera. Then at me.

The order mattered. I just didn't know what it meant yet.

---

My phone buzzed at 09:12.

"Grant Hart."

I let it ring twice before I answered.

"Reed."

"She find it?"

I pulled the field notebook closer. "Find what?"

"The envelope. I put it under her coffee canister this morning. Before she woke up. Did she look confused? Did she open it?"

I hadn't seen an envelope. I'd watched her make coffee, watched her stretch, watched her eat breakfast. There had been no envelope.

"When did you put it there?"

"Five-thirty. I still have the key. She doesn't know I changed the locks. She uses the old one—it still works on the deadbolt, just not the handle."

I wrote that down. "09:12: Grant claims envelope placed under coffee canister at 05:30. Subject did not interact with object during observed period."

"I didn't see her open it," I said. "She made coffee, stretched, showered, ate, and left. There was no envelope."

"She must have found it after she got back. Check the footage."

I checked the footage. I watched her walk through the door at 08:52, drop her keys in the bowl, and pause at the kitchen counter. She picked up the coffee canister, set it down, and picked up an envelope I hadn't seen her bring in.

I rewound. The envelope wasn't there when she left. It was on the counter when she returned.

She opened it, pulled out a piece of paper, and read it. Her expression didn't change. She folded the paper, slid it back into the envelope, and placed it in the drawer where she kept her receipts.

Then she did something I didn't expect.

She reached into her pocket and pulled out a red receipt. Folded once, then twice, then a third time, into a precise rectangle. She placed it on the counter beside the coffee canister—beside the spot where the envelope had been—and arranged it so the fold faced outward.

Deliberate. Ceremonial. A mark.

I wrote it down. "09:14: Subject places folded red receipt beside displaced object. Behavior logged as anomaly."

But the word "anomaly" felt wrong. It wasn't random. It wasn't confusion. It was the opposite of confusion—it was a signature, a timestamp, a record that something had been moved.

She was keeping her own timeline.

I watched her walk away from the counter, into the living room, where she sat on the couch and opened a book. Normal. Controlled. Exactly what Grant said she couldn't be.

I zoomed in on the reflection in my window again. The green light was steady now, but in the playback, in that moment at 08:51, it had been red.

Red meant something was wrong.

Red meant she'd seen it.

I closed the footage and opened my field notebook to a fresh page. I wrote the words carefully, in the same flat clinical language I'd used for two days.

"Subject paused at courtyard threshold. Possible detection of surveillance equipment. Recommend continued observation."

But my hand was trembling as I wrote.

And I knew—I knew—that the order of her glance meant something I wasn't ready to understand.
