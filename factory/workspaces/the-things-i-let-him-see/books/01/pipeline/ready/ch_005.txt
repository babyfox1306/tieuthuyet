# Chapter 5: The Private Copy

Day four. 06:14. "Lena. Status: still unaware, still making me question everything I thought I knew about observation." I wrote the words and stared at them until the ink dried. Then I crossed out the last seven and wrote: "Status: unaware." The lie felt heavier than the truth would have.

I closed the notebook and pulled up the previous evening's footage. The timestamp read 19:14—the moment Lena had turned toward the kitchen cabinet where the camera was hidden. I'd watched it twelve times already. Each time, I told myself I was looking for something I'd missed. Some sign that her gaze was accidental, a coincidence of angles and light.

But her eyes had tracked directly into the lens. Not past it. Not near it. Into it.

I zoomed in on the counter where she'd placed the utility notice envelope beside the red receipt. Both items sat in perfect alignment with the tile grid, as if she'd measured the distance between them. The envelope was pristine—no bends, no creases, no evidence of having been retrieved from shared recycling.

Grant had said he found it in the recycling bin on the third floor. He'd sent me a photograph of the envelope, crumpled, coffee-stained, lying on top of old newspapers. The envelope in Lena's kitchen was clean. Unopened. Untouched.

I saved a screenshot, labeled it "Day Three—Envelope Comparison," and filed it in the private archive.

The morning feed flickered to life at 06:32. Lena's bedroom light came on, and I watched her shadow move across the wall—a woman dressing, brushing her hair, preparing for the day with the same unhurried precision she applied to everything. She emerged at 06:41 in her walking clothes. Gray leggings. Black jacket. The same pair of running shoes she'd worn every day since I started watching.

She checked her bag. Wallet. Phone. Sunglasses case. Then she paused at the kitchen counter, picked up the utility envelope, and held it to the light.

I leaned closer to the monitor. She wasn't reading the address. She was examining the envelope itself—the seal, the paper quality, the way the light passed through it. She turned it over, ran her finger along the flap, and set it back down.

Then she left.

I tracked her through the courtyard camera. She moved at her usual pace—deliberate, measured, each step placed with purpose. The morning light caught her hair, and for a moment I forgot to breathe.

She reached the building's main entrance and paused.

Not at the door. At the threshold. The exact spot where the camera status light had reflected three days ago. She stood there for three seconds, two seconds longer than necessary to adjust a bag strap or check for keys. Her hand moved to the strap, adjusted it, and then she stepped through the doorway without looking back.

I waited for her to look up. To glance toward my window. To acknowledge the presence she seemed to sense but never confirm.

She didn't.

I exhaled. I hadn't realized I'd been holding my breath.

The next thirty-eight minutes passed in fragments. I checked the courtyard camera every sixty seconds, noting the empty benches, the still fountain, the way the light shifted across the concrete. I reviewed the morning footage three times, searching for something I couldn't name.

At 07:19, Lena reappeared in the courtyard.

She was walking faster than usual. Her head was down, her hands shoved into her jacket pockets. She crossed the courtyard without stopping, without glancing at the fountain or the benches or the windows that watched her from every angle.

She reached her building's entrance, pushed through the door, and disappeared into the lobby.

I switched to the hallway camera. The angle was tight—a narrow view of her apartment door and the section of hallway immediately surrounding it. Lena appeared in frame at 07:21. She stopped three feet from her door and looked up.

At the top edge of the doorframe.

She raised her hand and ran her index finger along the seam where the frame met the wall. Slowly. Deliberately. The same methodical assessment she used for every object she touched.

I zoomed in, adjusting the focus until the doorframe filled the monitor. There was something on the edge of the frame—a thin, transparent strip that caught the light at a specific angle. Clear adhesive. Nearly invisible from more than a foot away.

Lena had installed a seal on her doorframe.

And it was broken.

She examined the break with the same expression she'd worn when she held the utility envelope to the light. Calm. Attentive. Calculating. She traced the line of the broken seal with her finger, then pulled a small notebook from her jacket pocket—a paper planner, the kind people used before phones made everything digital.

She wrote something. The date. The time. A brief note I couldn't read from the camera angle.

Then she opened her apartment door, stepped inside, and closed it behind her.

I saved the footage. Labeled it: "Day Four—Doorframe Seal, Broken."

I didn't understand what I'd seen. A clear adhesive strip on a doorframe. Broken during a thirty-eight-minute walk. Recorded in a paper planner with the same care a detective would use to log evidence.

But the pieces were there. I just couldn't see how they fit.

The morning passed in fragments. Lena appeared at her kitchen window at 08:14, making coffee. At 09:37, she sat at her desk with her paper planner open, writing. At 11:02, she left again—this time carrying a small tote bag and her phone.

I didn't follow her. I stayed at the monitor, reviewing the doorframe footage frame by frame.

The seal was exactly where she'd placed it—a thin line of clear adhesive running along the top edge of the doorframe. The break was clean, as if something had been inserted between the seal and the frame and then pulled away. No fraying. No tearing. Just a precise separation.

I wrote in my field notebook: "Subject inspected doorframe upon return. Clear adhesive residue present. Possible security measure."

The words felt inadequate. Incomplete. I crossed out "possible" and wrote "likely."

At 13:47, Lena returned. She stopped at the doorframe again, examined the broken seal, and wrote another note in her planner. Then she opened her door and went inside.

I didn't see her again until evening.

The sun set at 17:34. I watched the light drain from the courtyard, watched the shadows stretch and merge, watched the windows across the complex flicker to life one by one. Lena's kitchen light came on at 18:02.

She was at the counter, placing the red receipt beside the displaced kitchen mug. The same mug she'd found in the wrong cabinet three days ago. The same receipt she'd folded into precise thirds and set beside it.

She stepped back. Looked at the arrangement. Adjusted the receipt by a millimeter.

Then she turned and walked to the blinds.

She pulled the cord, and the common-facing blinds descended in a smooth, even line. The slats clicked into place, blocking my view of her kitchen.

I adjusted the camera angle, trying to find a gap. There was none. The blinds were closed tight.

I leaned back in my chair and stared at the blank screen. For a long moment, there was nothing to watch. No movement. No shadow. No indication that Lena Hart existed anywhere except in the footage I'd already recorded.

Then the light shifted.

Not the kitchen light—the light inside her apartment, warm and golden, filtering through the blinds in thin, parallel lines. I adjusted the camera, turning the focus to the slats, searching for any opening wide enough to see through.

I found one. A gap at the bottom of the blinds, less than an inch wide, where the slats didn't quite meet the windowsill.

I zoomed in.

Lena was in the kitchen. She was moving—slowly, fluidly, her body swaying to a rhythm I couldn't hear. Her arms rose above her head, her hips shifted, her feet traced small circles on the tile floor.

She was dancing.

Not the kind of dancing people do when they're performing. Not the kind people do when they're trying to look good. She was dancing like no one was watching—her eyes closed, her face relaxed, her body moving with a freedom I had never seen in the observed routines.

I watched for thirty seconds. A minute. The footage accumulated on the private system, separate from the feed Grant received.

I told myself I was looking for evidence of instability. A pattern. A crack in the facade that would explain everything Grant had said about her.

But my hand trembled as I rewound the footage and watched it again.

The second time, I noticed the way her fingers traced invisible lines in the air. The third time, I noticed the slight smile at the corner of her mouth—the first genuine smile I'd seen in four days of observation.

I saved the file to the private archive. Labeled it: "Evening—Kitchen. No notable activity."

The lie sat on the page, and I did not cross it out.

The dance footage played in the background as I opened the file labeled "Grant Report—Day Four." The cursor blinked in the empty document. I stared at it for a long time, listening to the silence of the apartment, feeling the weight of the private archive in the external drive.

I began typing.

"Evening observation: Subject closed blinds. No notable activity."

I saved the file, closed the laptop, and looked at the monitor where the dance footage still waited in the private archive.

I had just lied to my client about a woman I was paid to expose.

And I had no intention of telling him the truth.
