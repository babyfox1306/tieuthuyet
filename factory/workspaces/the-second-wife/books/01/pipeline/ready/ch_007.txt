# Chapter 7: The Shared Wound

The police officer's pen hovered over his notepad. "So you found the shirt by accident?"

"Yes."

"And you've never been in trouble with the law before?"

I met his eyes. "Never."

The lie tasted like copper.

He was young, early thirties, with a wedding ring that caught the light and a face that hadn't seen enough to be properly cynical. His partner, a woman with steel-gray hair and dead eyes, stood by the window, watching the garden. She was the dangerous one. She'd heard a thousand lies. She'd probably told a few herself.

"Walk me through it again," the young officer said.

I took a breath. "I was cleaning Marcus's closet. The floorboard was loose. I lifted it, found the shirt, called you."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

He glanced at his partner. She didn't move.

"And you have no idea how the shirt got there?"

"No."

"Or whose blood is on it?"

"Clara's. The name tag—"

"We read the report."

The room was silent. The kitchen clock ticked. I could hear Marcus in the next room, his voice low and measured, answering the same questions with a different story. And somewhere upstairs, the wife was giving hers.

Three versions of the same night.

Only one was true.

The female officer finally turned from the window. "Miss Anna, you understand that withholding evidence in a homicide investigation is a felony?"

"Yes."

"You understand that lying to police officers is also a felony?"

"Yes."

"Then I'll ask you one more time. Did anyone help you find that shirt?"

I held her gaze. "No."

She didn't believe me. I could see it in the way her jaw tightened, the way she shifted her weight. But she didn't have proof. And in my world, lack of proof was the same as innocence.

"We'll be in touch," she said. "Don't leave town."

---

The interview ended at 4:17 PM. I know because I checked the clock the moment the door closed behind them.

Forty-seven minutes of questions. Forty-seven minutes of lies.

The wife came down at 4:30, her face pale, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea that shook despite the stillness of her fingers. She sat across from me in the kitchen, her eyes red, her voice barely a whisper.

"Thank you," she said. "For not telling them."

I didn't answer. I was still processing what Marcus had said, what she had done, what I had seen.

"I know you have questions," she said. "I know you think—" She stopped. Her breath hitched. "I know you think I'm lying."

"I think everyone's lying," I said. "That's the only thing I'm sure of."

She set down her tea. Her hands were trembling again. But I watched them carefully this time. I watched for the cue.

"I wasn't always like this," she said. "The fear. The shaking. The locked doors."

I waited.

"I grew up in a nice house," she said. "Big windows. White curtains. A garden with roses." She laughed, a hollow sound. "Everyone thought we were perfect. Everyone thought my father was a good man."

She looked at me. Her eyes were wet, but the tears didn't fall. They hovered, like she was holding them back by sheer will.

"He wasn't."

I felt something shift in my chest. A familiar weight. A door I'd kept locked for years.

"He used to come into my room at night," she said. "After my mother went to sleep. He'd sit on the edge of my bed and tell me that I was special, that I was his favorite, that no one else would ever love me the way he did."

Her voice cracked. She pressed her palm against her mouth.

"I was seven the first time."

The room went cold. The clock kept ticking. I sat perfectly still because if I moved, if I spoke, I would break.

"I'm sorry," I said. And I meant it.

She wiped her eyes. "I don't tell people that. I've never told anyone. But when I saw you standing there, protecting me, lying for me—" She reached across the table. Her fingers brushed mine. "I knew you understood."

I pulled my hand back.

"I don't understand anything," I said. "I'm just a housekeeper."

"No." Her voice was soft but certain. "You're not."

She looked at me. Her eyes were clear now, the tears gone, the vulnerability replaced by something sharper.

"You're running from something too."

I didn't answer.

"I can see it," she said. "In the way you move. In the way you check the exits before you sit down. In the way you never let your back face a door."

I felt my throat tighten.

"We're the same, Anna," she said. "Broken things that learned to look whole."

---

She left at 5:00. Said she needed to rest. Said the interview had exhausted her.

I stayed in the kitchen, staring at the table, replaying her words.

Broken things that learned to look whole.

She was right, of course. I had spent years building walls, painting smiles, telling lies. I had learned to make myself small and forgettable because being seen was dangerous. Because being known meant being vulnerable.

But she had seen me.

And that was terrifying.

I was still sitting there when Marcus walked in. He looked older than he had this morning, his face gray, his eyes hollow.

"They bought it," he said. "For now."

I didn't respond.

"She's good," he said. "My wife. She's very good."

"She told me about her father."

Marcus stopped. His face flickered—surprise, then something darker. "She told you?"

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Enough."

He sat down across from me. The same chair she had just vacated. The same view of the same empty garden.

"She's never told anyone that," he said. "Not even me."

I looked at him. "Maybe she needed someone who would believe her."

"Believe her?" He laughed, but there was no humor in it. "Anna, I told you. She's been lying for years. The story about her father—I don't know if it's true. I don't know if anything she says is true."

"She wouldn't lie about that."

"Wouldn't she?"

He leaned forward. His eyes were tired, but they were clear. Certain.

"Let me ask you something," he said. "When she told you that story, did she cry?"

"Yes."

"Did the tears fall?"

I thought about it. Her eyes had been wet. Her voice had cracked. But the tears—the actual tears—had never come.

"When did she last eat?" Marcus asked. "When did she last sleep? When did she last take her medication?"

I didn't know.

"She told me she was asleep the night Clara disappeared," I said. "From 10 PM to 6 AM."

"She said the same thing to the police."

"The same thing?"

Marcus nodded. "She said she was in bed the whole night. That she didn't hear anything. That she didn't see anything."

I looked at the clock. 5:23 PM.

The night Clara disappeared, I had been awake until midnight. I had been sitting in the kitchen, watching the garden, listening to the house settle.

And I had heard footsteps.

Light footsteps. Quick footsteps. Moving through the east wing at 11:30.

Not heavy like Marcus's.

And I had heard a woman's voice. Whispering.

---

"Why are you asking about the timeline?" Marcus said.

I didn't answer.

"Anna. What are you thinking?"

I looked at him. "She said she was asleep. But I heard someone in the east wing that night. At 11:30."

Marcus went still. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

"And it wasn't me?"

"I know your footsteps. You walk heavy. You drag your left foot slightly." I had noticed it the first day. "The footsteps I heard were light. Quick. Like someone who knew exactly where they were going."

Marcus stared at me. "You think it was her."

"I don't know what I think."

"You think she was awake. That she lied."

"She already lied about the application form. She already lied about my name."

"Then she could be lying about everything."

I didn't say anything.

Marcus stood up. He walked to the window, his back to me, his hands in his pockets.

"Anna," he said. "You need to be careful."

"Careful how?"

"She's getting closer to you. She's telling you things she's never told anyone. She's making you feel special." He turned. "That's how she operates. That's how she got Clara."

I stood up. "Clara was locked in a room. Clara died. How is that getting close?"

"Because Clara trusted her," Marcus said. "Because Clara believed her. And when she had what she wanted—when Clara knew too much—she got rid of her."

I felt a chill run down my spine.

"What did Clara know?"

Marcus looked at me. "The same thing you're starting to know."

He walked out of the kitchen. His footsteps echoed down the hall. Heavy. Dragging.

I stood alone in the kitchen.

The clock ticked.

I looked at the time.

5:37 PM.

The wife said she was asleep from 10 PM to 6 AM.

But I had heard footsteps in the east wing at 11:30.

Light footsteps. Quick footsteps.

And I had heard a woman's voice. Whispering.
