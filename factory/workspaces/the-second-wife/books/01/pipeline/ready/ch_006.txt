# Chapter 6: The Confrontation

I walked into his study with the shirt in one hand and the watch in the other. "Explain these," I said. "Or I'll explain them to the police."

Marcus was sitting behind his desk, a glass of whiskey in front of him. He didn't look surprised to see me. He looked tired. The kind of tired that came from waiting for something bad to finally happen.

"Close the door," he said.

"No."

"Suit yourself." He took a slow drink, his eyes on the shirt. The bloodstains were dark brown in the lamplight. Clara's name tag still glowed white against the fabric. "Where did you find that?"

"Your closet. Under the floorboard. The one that's loose because you pried it up in a hurry."

He set the glass down. "And the watch?"

"Your desk drawer. Unlocked. Because you're either arrogant or careless. I haven't decided which."

Marcus leaned back in his chair. The leather creaked. He looked at me the way you look at a chess piece you're about to lose. "You've been here less than a week."

"Long enough."

"Long enough to break into my private study. Long enough to steal from my desk. Long enough to decide I'm a murderer based on a shirt and a watch you found in two places you had no business being."

"I found them because you hid them badly."

He laughed. It was a short, hollow sound. "You think I'm stupid enough to hide evidence of a murder in my own closet? In my own desk? While a new housekeeper is in the house?"

"I think you're arrogant enough."

"I think you're being played."

I stepped closer. The shirt was cold in my hands. Old blood had stiffened the fabric. "Explain it, then. Explain the blood. Explain the watch. Explain Clara."

Marcus stood up. He was taller than me by a head, but I didn't step back. I'd faced bigger men in worse places. "Clara was my wife's carer before you arrived. She was here for six years. She left suddenly one morning. That's what I told you."

"That's not what happened."

"No," he said quietly. "That's not what happened. She didn't leave. She died."

The word hit me like a slap. I'd suspected it. I'd found the shirt. But hearing him say it out loud made it real.

"Did you kill her?"

"No."

"Then who did?"

He looked at me for a long moment. His face was hard to read. Not angry. Not scared. Something else. Something that looked almost like pity.

"My wife," he said.

I almost laughed. "Your wife. The woman who can barely walk to the kitchen. The woman whose hands shake so badly she can't hold a cup of tea. That wife?"

"She's better at pretending than you think."

"Bullshit."

"She's been doing it for years. Every carer we've hired. Every housekeeper. Every nurse. She plays the victim. She makes them feel sorry for her. And then she turns them against me."

I held up the shirt. "This has Clara's name on it. Clara's blood. Hidden in your closet."

"Because she put it there."

"Right. The woman who can barely stand dragged a body and hid evidence in your closet."

Marcus walked around the desk. He stopped a few feet away from me. Close enough that I could smell the whiskey on his breath. "You've noticed the locked room in the east wing, haven't you?"

I didn't answer.

"The bolt is on the outside. You've already figured that out. You're smart. Too smart for this job. That's why she hired you."

"She didn't hire me. You did."

"No. She told me to hire you. She saw your application and insisted. Said you had 'the right look.' I thought she meant you looked capable. Now I think she meant you looked desperate."

I felt a cold trickle down my spine. "What's in the locked room?"

"The room where Clara died."

The words hung in the air. I could hear my own heartbeat. The watch in my hand felt heavy, the crystal digging into my palm.

"She was found in that room," Marcus continued. "My wife said Clara had attacked her. Said she'd gone mad. I came home and found her on the floor, blood everywhere. My wife was sitting in the corner, crying, her hands covered in it."

"And you believed her."

"I had no reason not to. She was my wife. She was fragile. She was scared."

"But Clara was dead."

"Yes."

"And you just... covered it up."

"No. I called the police. I told them exactly what happened. But my wife changed her story. She said Clara had locked herself in the room. That she'd found her that way. That I was lying."

"Why would she do that?"

"Because she wanted me to look guilty. She wanted everyone to think I was the monster." He shook his head. "The police investigated. They ruled it suspicious. But they couldn't prove anything. And my wife was so convincing. So broken. So scared. They believed her."

"And the watch?"

"E.M. stands for Eleanor Mercer. That was the name of the carer before Clara. She also died in this house. My wife said it was an accident. A fall down the stairs."

My throat was dry. "You're telling me your wife has killed two people."

"I'm telling you that I've been living with a predator for ten years. And she's very, very good at hiding it."

I wanted to believe he was lying. I wanted to believe the shirt in my hand was proof of his guilt. But something in his voice was too steady. Too calm. The voice of a man who had told this story before and been called a liar.

"You have no proof," I said.

"Neither do you. You have a shirt that could have been planted. A watch that could have been stolen. A story that only one person can verify."

"And that person is your wife."

"Yes."

"She'll tell me the truth."

"Will she?" Marcus stepped closer. "She's already lied to you, Anna. She told you she saw your full name on the application. But you only wrote 'Anna.' How did she know your full name?"

I felt the ground shift beneath me. He was right. I'd never told her my last name. I'd never written it on the application. But she'd known it.

"She could have guessed," I said weakly.

"Did she guess? Or did she know because she looked you up? Because she knows who you really are?"

I didn't answer.

"Tell me, Anna. What did you do before you became a housekeeper? What are you running from?"

The door burst open.

The wife stood in the doorway, her face pale, her eyes wide and wet with tears. She was trembling. Her hands were shaking so badly she had to grip the doorframe to stay upright.

"Don't listen to him," she sobbed. "Please. Don't listen to him."

She stumbled into the room. Her robe was loose, her hair disheveled. She looked like she'd been crying for hours.

"He's going to kill me," she said. "He's going to kill me like he killed Clara. Please. Call the police. Call them now."

Marcus stepped back. "Lydia—"

"Don't touch me!" She flinched away from him, pressing herself against the wall. "You see? You see how he treats me? He's been threatening me all week. He said if I told anyone the truth, he'd bury me in the garden."

I looked at Marcus. He was standing still, his hands at his sides, his face unreadable.

"Anna," the wife said, her voice cracking. "Please. I'm begging you. Call the police. Before he does something."

I looked at the shirt in my hand. The watch. The evidence.

I looked at Marcus. Guilty. Dangerous. A murderer.

I looked at the wife. Broken. Terrified. Begging.

"Call them," she whispered. "Please."

"She's lying," Marcus said quietly. "Just like she lied about Clara."

"Don't listen to him," the wife said. "He's the one who killed her. He's the one who locked her in that room. He's going to do the same to me."

I made my choice.

I pulled out my phone.

"Anna," Marcus said. "Don't."

I dialed.

The wife sobbed behind me. Her tears were loud, desperate, real. I could hear her gasping for breath, her hands gripping the wall.

I pressed the last digit.

And then I looked at her.

She was standing against the wall, her face buried in her hands. Her shoulders shook with sobs. Her body was trembling.

But her hands.

Her hands were folded in her lap.

Perfectly still.

Not a tremor.

Not a tear.

And I knew, in that moment, that I had made a terrible mistake.
