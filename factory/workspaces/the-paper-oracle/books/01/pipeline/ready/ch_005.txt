# Chapter 5: Your Handwriting

"If anyone asks, you never came through this door," the orderly said, and punched in a code Iris had not given him.

The door clicked open. The air on the other side was different—colder, thinner, scrubbed of whatever scent the main hospital carried. It smelled like disuse and floor wax.

Iris memorized the code. 7-2-9-4. She didn't ask how the orderly knew she was coming, or why he was helping. That information cost more than she was willing to pay.

The corridor stretched ahead, lined with doors that had small whiteboards mounted beside them. Most were blank. A few had dates written in faded marker. The carpet was a patternless beige, worn thin in the center where wheelchairs had traced the same path for decades.

"Room 229," the orderly said. He did not walk with her.

Iris moved down the hall alone. Her footsteps made no sound on the carpet, but her heartbeat was loud enough to fill her ears. She counted doors. 221. 223. 225. The numbers felt like a countdown.

Room 229 was halfway down, the door slightly ajar. A wedge of pale light fell across the carpet. She pushed it open with her fingertips.

The woman inside was sitting in a vinyl armchair facing the window. Her back was to the door. Silver hair, thin and flat, fell past her shoulders. She was dressed in a hospital gown layered under a cardigan that had been mended at the elbows with mismatched yarn.

On the windowsill, a row of paper cranes.

Dozens of them. Some were yellowed and soft with age. Others were crisp white, folded from the stiff paper of meal tickets. They stood in formation, their wings sharp, as if waiting for permission to fly.

Iris stepped into the room. "Ms. Vole?"

The woman did not turn. Her hands were moving. Fold, crease, smooth. She was working on another crane, her fingers operating with the muscle memory of a practiced routine.

"A nurse from the registry," Iris said, the lie settling easily on her tongue. "I'm doing an audit of old equipment. They said you might remember a piece of machinery from the seventies." She paused. "A label printer."

The hands stopped moving.

Marguerite Vole turned her head. Her face was lined, her eyes clouded with the film of years. But the look she gave Iris was not the vacant stare of a lost mind. It was sharp. Suspicious.

"You're too young to remember the seventies."

"I'm following a trail."

Marguerite turned back to the window. "The printer doesn't exist."

The room was quiet. A clock ticked somewhere. The fluorescent light hummed at a frequency just below pain.

Iris crossed the room and sat on the edge of the bed, putting herself in Marguerite's line of sight. She pulled the blank slip from her pocket and held it out.

"This came out of it. Tonight."

Marguerite looked at the paper. Her hands resumed folding, but faster now, the movements tight. She finished the crane and placed it on the sill with the others.

"It's blank," Iris said. "But I've seen it print names. Times."

Marguerite's eyes stayed on the crane. Her lips pressed together.

"Lena Zarate. 03:47." Iris spoke the words slowly, testing them. "Arthur Bell. 04:12. Those came true."

A tremor ran through Marguerite's fingers. She clasped her hands together to still them.

"Show me the paper again."

Iris handed it over.

Marguerite held the blank slip up to the light. Her thumb traced the perforated edge. She brought it close to her face, then pulled it back, as if the distance might change what she saw.

"It only ever prints your handwriting."

The words landed like a stone dropped into still water.

Iris stared at her. "What?"

Marguerite lowered the paper. Her voice was thin, barely above a whisper. "The machine doesn't make its own letters. It reads yours. The way you write a name. The way you write a time. It takes the shape of your hand and puts it on the paper."

"That's impossible."

"Is it?" Marguerite looked at her. Her eyes were clearer than they had been, as if the conversation had cut through the fog. "You've seen it print. Does it look like a typewriter? A computer font?"

Iris opened her mouth. Closed it.

The labels had text. She had read them. But she hadn't looked at the "shape" of the letters. She had been too focused on the information to notice the handwriting.

Bell, Arthur. 04:12.

She closed her eyes. The memory of the slip rose in her mind. The print was neat. Precise. Slight slant to the right.

Slight slant to the right.

The way she wrote on charts.

Her eyes snapped open.

"Your handwriting, Ms. Vole?"

Marguerite shook her head. "No. I never touched it. I watched it work. It takes the nurse. The one who touches it. The one who feeds it." Her voice cracked. "It learns you."

Iris pressed two fingers to her own wrist. Pulse, 112. Too fast. She forced her breathing to slow.

Arthur Bell.

She thought back to the sequence of events. The machine printed his name at 03:55. She admitted him to Room 3 based on the label. She over-triaged him, skipping the standard chest pain protocol to push him straight into critical care.

She remembered the code team's faces. The paramedics' confusion. The attending on duty questioning her judgment.

And she remembered the time. 04:12.

What if she hadn't moved him?

The thought hit her like a physical blow.

She had been trying to beat the machine. She thought if she admitted him to critical, he would get faster attention. Better monitoring. That she could break the prophecy.

But the critical bay had a different protocol. A different nurse-to-patient ratio. A different threshold for intervention.

The code team had pushed nitroglycerin faster. They had escalated his care earlier. They had followed the urgency she had written into his chart.

Her handwriting.

Her intervention.

She had not beaten the machine. She had become its hands.

"I tried to stop it," Iris said, the words coming out rough. "The second patient. I put him in critical care. I thought if I changed the variables, the time wouldn't hold."

Marguerite's face went pale. "You moved him."

"Yes."

"You changed his treatment path."

"Yes."

Marguerite closed her eyes. "Then you were the agent."

"I don't understand."

"The machine doesn't predict the future," Marguerite said. Her voice was shaking. "It sees the present. It sees what you will do with the information. And it prints the result of your choices."

"That doesn't make sense. I was trying to save him."

"You were trying to control the outcome. That's not the same thing."

Iris stood up. Her legs felt unsteady. She paced to the window, then back to the bed. The paper cranes watched her with their folded eyes.

"So if I do nothing, the time doesn't come true?"

"I don't know."

"If I do something, I make it come true?"

"I don't know."

Iris stopped pacing. She looked at the cranes. At the careful, deliberate folds that turned a meal ticket into something that could not be used for its original purpose.

"What do you know, Ms. Vole?"

Marguerite was silent for a long moment. Her hands moved to the windowsill, touching the cranes one by one. Counting them. Reassuring herself they were still there.

"I know I got out," she said finally. "I know I stopped touching it. I know I started folding paper instead of writing on it. And the machine went silent."

"Silent?"

"It sat there. For years. No one touched it. No one fed it. It was just hardware gathering dust." She looked up at Iris. "Until you."

Iris felt the words land somewhere deep in her chest. Not fear. Not yet. Something colder.

The machine had been dormant. Waiting. She had stationed herself at that triage desk. She had touched it. Put paper in it. Moved it.

She had woken it up.

"There's more," Marguerite said. Her voice dropped lower, almost inaudible. "But I can't hold onto it. It slips. The memories don't stay."

"What do you mean?"

Marguerite pressed her palm against her forehead. "The machine doesn't just take your handwriting. It takes your hand. It takes the part of you that believes you can fix things. And then it gives you a choice. Every time. A choice."

"A choice to follow the time or not."

" A choice to be the nurse who saves or the nurse who condemns." Marguerite's eyes were bright, wet. "But the machine prints regardless. It prints what you will do."

Iris wanted to ask more. She wanted to demand names, dates, studies, proof. But Marguerite's face was closing off, the clarity receding like water pulled back by a tide.

"I need you to focus," Iris said. "Please."

"I can't." Marguerite's hands returned to her lap. She began to smooth the hem of her cardigan. "It hurts to hold it. It hurts to remember."

Iris looked around the room. The cranes. The faded bedspread. The clock that had stopped ticking.

She was alone in this. There was no one to call. No colleague to consult. Dr. Ovid was a voice on a phone, and she could not trust him. The orderly had disappeared.

She pressed her fingers to her own wrist again. Pulse, 98. Slowing.

She steadied herself.

"One more thing," she said. "Before I go."

Marguerite did not look up.

Iris knelt beside the armchair, bringing herself to the woman's eye level. "The machine. Where did it come from? Who brought it to the hospital?"

Marguerite's hands stilled. Her lips parted.

Then her hand shot out, thin and strong, and clamped around Iris's sleeve.

"Listen to me," she said.

Her voice was clear. Exact. Like a chart note being read aloud.

"It did not start with the patients."

Iris felt the words cut through the hum of the room.

"It started with us."

The grip tightened. Marguerite's eyes locked onto hers.

"With the nurses. With the ones who touched it first."

Iris's blood went cold.

"It was never meant for the patients."

Marguerite released her grip and sank back into the chair, the effort of the words draining the color from her face.

The clock on the wall resumed ticking.

Iris stood up. Her legs carried her to the door, past the cranes, past the faded numbers.

She did not look back.
