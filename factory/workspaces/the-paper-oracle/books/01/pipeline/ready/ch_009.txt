# Chapter 9: Iris Callahan at 05:19

"It printed me," Iris said, and the words came out steadier than her pulse.

She held the slip between thumb and forefinger, the paper still warm from the mechanism. Her name in the same Courier typeface. A time: 05:19. No room number. No patient category. No diagnosis. Just her name and a minute that sat thirty-seven minutes in the future.

The machine hummed behind her, settled back into its ordinary beige silence. It had delivered its provocation. Now it waited.

Iris read the slip again. *Iris Callahan — 05:19.* 

She should have felt terror. Instead, a strange clinical calm settled over her, the same dissociation that kicked in when a trauma bay went sideways and there was no time for panic. She pressed two fingers to her wrist. Ninety-two. Elevated but not tachycardic. Good enough.

The name meant something specific now. She had spent the night proving that the machine did not predict. It reflected. The slip for Arthur Bell had carried a time and a bed assignment, and when she had treated him with ordinary protocol instead of fear-driven special handling, he had survived past the printed minute. The machine had shown her what she *would* do, not what *would happen*. The slip was a photograph of her own hand.

So this slip—her name, her time—was not a prophecy of her death. It was a warning about a choice she had not yet made. Some decision in the next thirty-seven minutes would turn her from the person making the triage decisions into the person on the table. Unless she recognized the trap before she stepped into it.

She sat down in the chair facing the machine, the slip flat on her thigh. She needed to think like a clinician. Identify the dangerous responses. List them. Eliminate them.

One: flee the ER. Walk out, drive away, let someone else take the shift. If she ran, she would leave the department short-staffed at the most vulnerable hour, and some other patient would pay for her absence. The machine would have achieved its goal through her cowardice.

Two: hide the slip. Pretend it never printed. But hiding was a form of denial, and denial led to blind spots. The year-old death—the one she had buried under protocol—had started with her looking away from a decision she did not want to own.

Three: self-medicate. There was a locked cabinet with benzodiazepines in the psych holding room. One pill and the edges would soften, but so would her reflexes.

Four: call a code on herself. Manufacture a crisis that would pull attention to her body, force a team to treat her, and prove the machine wrong by surviving her own time. But that would waste resources on a false alarm while real patients waited.

Five: redirect staff away from her. Send the only other nurse on shift to the far end of the corridor, isolate herself so that when something happened, no one would be near enough to help. That was the subtlest trap—making her own protection into a weapon against her.

She pressed her palm flat against the slip. The paper was cool now. She would do none of those things.

She would stay. She would work. She would treat every patient who came through the doors with the same attention she gave anyone else, and she would not bend the shift around her own name.

The ER was quiet. The board showed two patients in beds, both stable. Arthur Bell had been moved to the step-down unit an hour ago, his chest pain managed, his vitals boring. The label had failed because she had refused to let it dictate her care. She could do the same for herself.

But the year-old death sat in her chest like a fragment of shrapnel she had never removed. That patient had seemed stable too. The decision had been small—a delay in calling for a consult because the attending seemed busy, because she told herself it could wait until morning. The patient had coded at 04:30, and by the time she reached the bedside, the rhythm was already flat. She had told herself it was protocol. That any nurse would have made the same call.

But she had known, even then, that the delay had been fear wearing a uniform. She had been afraid of looking stupid, of paging an attending for something that might resolve on its own. The patient had died because she had chosen comfort over action.

That was the machine's language. Not fate—consequence.

Iris folded the slip and slid it into her breast pocket. She needed to see Marguerite Vole.

The memory-care corridor was dim, the night lights casting long shadows across the linoleum. Marguerite was awake, sitting upright in her chair, her hands folded on the blanket. The nurse at the desk nodded Iris through without a word.

Marguerite looked at her with clearer eyes than before. "You came back."

"I have something to show you." Iris pulled the slip from her pocket and held it out. "It printed my name."

Marguerite took it with trembling fingers. She held it close to her face, then lowered it. Her breath came out in a long, slow exhale, and her eyes glassed over.

"It printed mine too," she said. Her voice was dry, like paper crumbling. "Once. Just once. I didn't understand until later."

"Did you survive your time?"

Marguerite's mouth twisted. "I left. I walked out of the ER at 03:00 and never came back. I told myself it was the right call—that I was protecting the patients from the thing I might do. But I was protecting myself." She tapped the slip with a fingernail. "You can't run from it. The mechanism doesn't stop. It just finds someone else to work through."

Iris felt the words settle into her bones. "You quit because you couldn't live with being part of it."

"I couldn't live with the weight of seeing myself in every slip." Marguerite's eyes met hers, and for a moment, the confusion was gone. "You see yourself too, don't you? That's the part that doesn't go away."

Iris nodded.

"The only way out is through," Marguerite said. "And even then, you carry it."

Iris took the slip back, folded it, returned it to her pocket. "I think I can do this."

"Good." Marguerite closed her eyes. "Because the machine will keep printing until you prove you already know what it's trying to teach you."

Iris left her in the dim room and walked back toward the ER. The hallway stretched ahead, empty and fluorescent, the clock above the exit reading 05:15.

Four minutes until her printed time.

The ambulance bay doors slammed open.

Iris turned. Two paramedics wrestled a gurney through the narrow opening, one pumping a bag-valve mask, the other holding an IV bag aloft. The patient on the board was a man in his fifties, his face dusky, his lips blue. A fisherman, by the look of the slicker still half-tied around his neck. His airway was closing—stridor audible even from ten feet away, a high, wet whistle that meant epiglottitis or anaphylaxis or something worse.

The paramedic shouted numbers: "Found him on the dock, family said he was fine ten minutes ago. Oxygen sat eighty-two on the way in, dropping. No IV access."

Iris moved. Her body knew what to do before her mind finished the checklist. Airway support, epinephrine if anaphylactic, prepare for intubation.

She reached the gurney, grabbed the cold IV bag from the paramedic's hand. The fluid warmer was down—of course it was, at 05:16 on a Sunday. She tucked the bag under her armpit, pressed it against her side, let her body heat start to warm it while she pulled gloves on with her free hand. The plastic was cold through her scrub top, a sharp, grounding sensation.

The clock above the nurses' station read 05:17.

The patient's eyes were open, terrified. Iris leaned close. "I'm Iris. We're going to help you. Don't fight us."

The stridor worsened.

She needed a laryngoscope, a tube, suction. She opened her mouth to call for help, but the words caught.

The printer in the back room whined.

It was a faint sound, easily lost under the chaos, but Iris heard it. A mechanical start, the feed rollers engaging, a new sheet of paper sliding into position. The machine was printing again.

She did not have time to check it. The patient's saturation was dropping. She had to secure the airway.

But the knowledge sat in her chest like a second pulse. Another label. Another name. Another time.

The clock ticked to 05:18.

One minute until her printed minute. The printer continued to feed, the blank label emerging, waiting for the type hammer to strike.

Iris grabbed the laryngoscope, tilted the fisherman's head back, and forced herself to focus on the cords. She did not look at the clock. She did not look at the paper. She looked at the patient, because that was the only thing she could control.

But the machine kept printing, and the label kept feeding, and 05:19 was coming whether she was ready or not.
