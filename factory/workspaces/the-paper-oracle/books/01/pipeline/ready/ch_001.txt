# Chapter 1: The First Slip

The label printer shrieked once at 2:10 a.m., then spat out a name no one had entered.

Iris Callahan looked up from the discharge paperwork. The printer sat on the counter between the phone bank and the cardiac monitor, a squat gray box that usually took orders from Registration. She tore off the strip. White paper, waxy to the touch. Thermal printing.

LENA ZARATE – 03:47

No room number. No diagnosis. No patient ID. Just a name and a time.

She held it up to the light as if it were a radiology film. Nothing. No barcode, no cross-reference number.

"Cross-talk", she decided. Registrations computer was having a seizure in the middle of the night, sending label requests to the wrong port. She propped the slip on the lip of the counter. Evidence for the morning shift. Let IT sort it out. She had a laceration in Room 2 that needed a tetanus shot and a seventy-year-old man with a belly ache that could be an AAA.

The ER was quiet. Not the civil quiet of a well-run department, but the hollow quiet of a bone hour. Two in the morning at a small coastal hospital meant you either had a train wreck or nothing. Tonight was nothing, which meant Iris had time to inventory the supply drawers and pretend she didn't hate the silence.

She walked past the pediatric cart. Someone had left a roll of stickers open on the top drawer, half-unfurled. A patient must have used them after a suture. The manufacturer had miscut the roll. Every smiling sun was sheared cleanly through one eye. It gave the yellow faces a damaged, winking look that made her chest tighten. She tucked the roll back into the drawer and closed it.

02:47.

She checked the slip on the counter. LENA ZARATE – 03:47. An hour away. "A glitch," she told herself. A misrouted patient label from a daytime visit. Nothing to think about.

She thought about it anyway.

The ambulance bay doors slammed open at 03:12.

Eight-year-old female. Severe respiratory distress. Sats in the sixties on room air. Inbound with a silent chest.

Iris met them at the bay. The child was small, pale, with dark hair plastered to her forehead. Her chest was heaving but no air was moving. The medics were bagging her with a BVM, but the resistance was high, the chest barely rising.

The name on the chart clipped to the gurney.

ZARATE, LENA.

Iris's brain snagged on it. The same name. The slip. The time.

Thirty-five minutes.

She pushed the thought down. Focus. BVM, O2 sat, pulse. She ripped open an IV start kit while the paramedics transferred the gurney to the bed.

Page Respiratory. Call the attending on call. Dr. Reyes was in the building, finishing a consult on the third floor. She heard her own voice giving the orders, flat and controlled, while her hands moved.

The child was crashing.

Iris worked the IV. The vein was small, rolling under her thumb. She held her breath. Stuck. Flashback in the chamber. She taped it down, pushed the saline lock.

Airway. They needed an airway.

Dr. Mateo Reyes appeared in the doorway. He was calm in a way that felt like an insult to the chaos. His white coat was clean. He had not been sprinting. He looked at the numbers on the monitor, at the child, at Iris.

What do we have, Callahan.

She gave him the rundown. Asthma exacerbation. Status asthmaticus. Unresponsive to nebs in the field. Breath sounds absent. Sats deteriorating.

I need an airway tray.

She was already reaching for it on the crash cart. So was he. Their fingers met on the handle of the Miller blade.

Iris.

Just her name. Low. Controlled. She looked up. His eyes were steady, dark brown with a ring of something lighter. Calm.

We have her.

She let her breath out. She had not realized she was holding it. His fingers closed briefly over hers, a pressure that lasted less than a second, and then he was pulling the laryngoscope, tilting the child's head, the moment already gone.

Going in.

She was the nurse again. She handed him the tube. Suctioned. Watched the cords float into view. He slid the tube in one smooth motion. She bagged. The chest rose. The EtCO2 trace appeared on the monitor.

Good tube position. Sats climbing.

But not climbing enough.

The monitors told the story she did not want to read. The child's lungs were too tight. Peak pressures climbing. Oxygen saturations dropping again. The team worked. Albuterol nebulized through the circuit. Epi. Steroids. Compressions when the rhythm slipped.

Iris's mind became a spreadsheet. Time. Dose. Rhythm. You could not fall apart at the numbers. The numbers were the only thing that mattered.

And then the numbers stopped mattering.

The monitor flatlined. Sinus rhythm gone. Asystole.

She was in another room. A different trauma. A different body. A young woman with a shattered skull from a car that had rolled on the highway. Iris had done everything by the book. The patient had died at 02:14 on a Tuesday.

Unavoidable.

She had written that word in the chart herself.

Unavoidable.

The memory snapped back with a metallic smell and the sharp weight of a glass syringe in her hand. Her chest ached. Her fingers tingled.

Her right hand lifted from the crash cart. Two fingers pressed against her own throat, just below the jaw, over the carotid.

Thump-thump. Thump-thump.

Still going.

She dropped her hand. Focused on the screen. Dr. Reyes was doing compressions. Someone was pushing another round of epi.

03:47.

Dr. Reyes stopped compressions. He looked at the clock. His voice was flat, clinical.

Time of death, 03:47.

The team went still. Iris's hand found the edge of the crash cart. The slip of paper was still in her pocket. She could feel it through the fabric of her coat.

LENA ZARATE – 03:47.

The child had been in the ER for thirty-five minutes. The exact window on the slip.

Coincidence. It had to be.

She helped clean the room. Wiped down the equipment. Labeled the lines. Her hands moved on autopilot while her brain circled the same thought like a shark in a tank.

She did not throw the slip away.

She pulled it out of her pocket instead. The paper was still warm from the printer, or maybe from her own body heat. She folded it along the long edge, sharp crease, and slid it into the deep front pocket of her white coat next to her penlight.

For the IT report. She needed it to document the malfunction.

The printer sat on the counter, quiet now. Gray. Inoffensive.

She should unplug it. That was the first step in any equipment complaint. Unplug it and label it so no one else used it.

She crouched behind the counter. The back of the printer was a smooth plastic shell. She traced the vents with her fingers. The slot for the label roll. The sticker that read Property of St. Jude Medical Center, Est. 1962.

No black cable.

She felt around the sides. The bottom. The top.

No cord.

She pulled the machine forward, lifted it off the counter. The base was a single piece of molded plastic. No seams. No battery compartment. No port for a power cord.

It was warm.

The thermal print head was warm. It had just printed something. It was on.

But there was no way it could be on.

She turned it over in her hands. It was surprisingly light. Empty. A sealed box with no throat and no heartbeat and no way to speak.

She set it back down on the counter. Her fingers traced the warm plastic one more time.

No cord.

The machine had spoken. And it had no way to be plugged in.
