# Chapter 3: The Manufacturer's Mark

"Stop asking about that printer," Mr. Hanley snapped before Iris had finished saying its name, and the archive key in his hand struck the desk hard enough to make the metal drawers rattle.

She pulled her hand back from the counter. The old clerk's face was pale under the buzzing fluorescent light, his eyes fixed somewhere behind her shoulder, as if the machine itself was standing in the doorway.

"I just want to know where it came from," she said. "Who ordered it. How long it's been in Room 3."

"It wasn't ours."

"What does that mean?"

Hanley leaned back. The chair creaked under his thin frame. He was a man who had spent too many years breathing hospital dust, his bones pressing against skin that had gone gray from lack of sun. He worked the archive shift because the living didn't bother him down here.

"It means it was never hospital property," he said. "No purchase order. No asset tag. No serial number. Maintenance has no record of it because it was never their job to fix it."

Iris felt the corner of the blank slip press into her palm through the pocket of her scrub pants. The machine had printed her name. No time. Just her name, embossed into blank paper like a gravestone waiting for a date.

"It's still in the building," she said. "Someone brought it here."

Hanley hesitated. His hand moved to the archive log book, but he didn't open it. He just tapped the cover with his fingernail, a nervous staccato.

"There was an older one," he said slowly. "Same build. Compact. A drawer that pulled out for the paper. No manufacturer plate." He looked at her directly for the first time. "Had a brass rivet on the back. Someone painted over it. Hospital equipment didn't have brass rivets in those days. Standard stuff had plastic tags and stamped metal."

Iris's pulse ticked up. She kept her hands still.

"Who used it?"

"A nurse. Older woman. Worked the night shift here in the sixties. She brought it with her from somewhere else. Nobody asked where she got it. She was good at her job. Quiet."

"What happened to her?"

Hanley's face closed. He opened the log book, running his finger down a column of dates. "Transferred."

"Where?"

"Memory care wing. Three floors below ground level. The long-term ward."

He pulled a thick folder bound with string from the drawer. As he untied it, a pink plastic cafeteria token fell out and spun across the desk. A lighthouse was stamped into the center, the color worn off from decades of handling. 1963.

Iris's breath caught.

Hanley picked the token up without a word and tucked it back into the folder.

"What's her name?" Iris asked.

"I don't remember."

"Mr. Hanley—"

"I don't remember." He said it flatly, a wall dropping into place. "It was thirty years ago. She had a name. It's gone. I'm a clerk, not a historian. I keep the records, I don't study them."

He was lying. Iris could hear it in the rhythm of his breathing, in the way he wouldn't look at her. He remembered exactly who that nurse was, and he had no intention of telling her.

"Why are you asking about this now?" Hanley's voice turned sharp again. "That machine has been gathering dust in storage for twenty years. Until last week, when you pulled it out and plugged it into Room 3."

"I didn't pull it out of storage. It was sitting on the counter."

"Same thing. Someone moved it there. Someone wanted you to find it."

The statement landed in her chest like a physical weight. She thought of the blank label. The smell of ozone. The paper that had appeared out of a dead printer.

"Who?" she asked.

Hanley snapped the log book shut. "I'm done. I have work to do."

"You haven't told me anything."

"I've told you more than I should have. If you're smart, you'll put that machine back in storage and forget you ever saw it. Whatever happened with that old nurse, it didn't end well. And it's not your problem."

He turned his back to her, filing the folder into a cabinet.

Iris stood in the doorway of the archive office, pressing two fingers to her own throat. Her pulse was a machine gun under her skin. She forced herself to breathe evenly, counting the seconds on the exhale until the rhythm slowed.

She left the office and walked deeper into the records room. The air was cold down here, the kind of cold that settled into bone and never left. Fluorescent lights buzzed in long tubes overhead, casting everything in a flat, sickly yellow-white.

The printer was on a steel table in the corner, covered with a dust cloth.

She had carried it down herself an hour ago, after Arthur Bell had stabilized and been moved to ICU. The code had taken every ounce of her skill. She had pushed the epinephrine herself, counting compressions out loud, watching the monitor flicker back to a sinus rhythm at 04:11.

One minute before the deadline.

The label had been wrong. Or she had been right.

Or the machine had let him live.

She pulled the cloth off the printer. The metal casing was cold, the same stubborn gray as the lid of a mortuary drawer. She turned it over, examining the back panel.

No manufacturer logo. No serial number. No asset sticker. No engraved brand name. Nothing to tell her where it had come from or who had built it.

But there, on the lower left corner of the back panel, was a small brass rivet.

It was painted the same gray as the rest of the casing, but the paint was cracking in a tight web, spiderwebbing away from the head of the rivet. Someone had tried to hide it, had covered the brass with a thick layer of industrial enamel, but time had undone their work.

Iris pulled a pen from her pocket and used the capped end to scrape at the paint.

The flakes fell onto the steel table, small grey curls. She scraped harder, working the edge of the cap into the groove of the rivet head.

The brass shone through, dull and aged.

She leaned closer, turning the printer to catch the light.

Etched into the brass, in a tight, neat script, was a single line:

"Vole, 1963"

A name and a year. No initials. No title. Just the name Vole.

She ran her thumb over the engraving, feeling the faint ridges. The metal was warm under her touch, warm from the machine's heat or from her own handling, she couldn't tell.

The machine had a maker. Or an owner. Someone named Vole, who had brought it to this hospital in 1963.

Hanley's face floated into her mind. The old nurse. The memory care wing.

Vole.

She was so close to something. She could feel it in her teeth, a vibration that hummed up through the floor. The machine wasn't a glitch. It wasn't a random piece of discarded equipment. It was a deliberate thing, brought here by a specific person for a specific reason.

And that person was still in the building.

Three floors below ground level. The long-term memory ward.

Iris straightened, her knees popping. She dug her phone out of her pocket. The screen glowed 04:47 AM.

Her thumb hovered over Dr. Ovid's name.

He would listen. He would probably tell her to stop playing detective and focus on the living patients. He would remind her that she was a nurse, not an investigator. He would be calm and rational and absent.

She pressed the power button. The phone went black.

She didn't need his voice. She needed answers.

She was alone in this. Just her and the machine and the name etched into brass.

She reached for the printer again, meaning to wrap it back in the dust cloth and hide it in the bottom drawer of the cabinet.

A sound stopped her.

It came from the corridor outside the records room. A slow, deliberate tread. Footsteps on the linoleum, weighted and careful, moving toward the closed door.

Iris's hand froze. Her pulse jammed in her throat.

The footsteps stopped.

She looked at the door. The glass panel was dark, too dark to see through. The corridor lights were on a motion sensor, and they had just gone out.

She shoved the printer into the bottom drawer of the steel cabinet, wincing at the clatter it made. She grabbed the dust cloth and stuffed it in after the machine.

The door handle turned.

Iris lunged for the lamp switch on the desk. Her fingers found it and jerked it down.

The records room fell into darkness.

She stood perfectly still, her back against the cabinet, her breath held in her chest.

The footsteps stopped just outside the door.

She heard the faint creak of the floor settling. The hiss of the ventilation system.

And then silence.

Someone was standing on the other side of that door.

Waiting.

Iris pressed her fingers to her own throat. Her pulse was a machine gun under her skin. She counted. She counted to keep herself from screaming.

The door did not open.

The footsteps did not leave.

She held her breath and waited for the dark to decide what it wanted from her.
