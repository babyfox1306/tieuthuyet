# Chapter 22: The Unfinished Thread

The city outside the studio was washed in wet gray, lights and traffic blurring through Iris Kane’s view of the soundproof glass. She sat across from Celia Ward, the journalist’s posture attentive but unhurried, her hands folded on the sheaf of documents that formed, between them, a silent bulwark against every threat and intimidation that had filled the past year. The on-air light glowed red. A producer’s hand signaled, and Celia’s voice, gentle but unflinching, reached the world.

“Today we speak with Iris Kane, former Senior Compliance Lead, National Integrity Directorate. She is here to explain—on the record and for the record—the evidence package submitted last night: a complete forensic sequence of the forged load certification coupons that authorized the Renn superstructure, internal communications from Meridian’s materials chain, and a federal audit tying every coupon back to their certifier’s override. Iris, thank you for joining us. We know this comes at considerable risk.”

Iris felt the shift in her lungs, the memory of sleepless days, the echo of sirens in the cold before dawn. But here, live, she found a certain serenity. She didn’t look at the camera—she spoke to Celia, and to the tens of thousands listening, who’d trusted the Directorate to keep the country whole.

“The risks,” Iris said, “are public now. That’s the only safeguard left.”

Celia inclined her head toward the documents. “Your package includes the original Meridian logs, the full chain of custody for the override tool, and a technical analysis of the unique tool-mark found on the forged coupons. For those unfamiliar, can you summarize—what did you find?”

Iris kept her answer spare. “Every forged certification for the Renn project, and a sequence trailing back to earlier infrastructure works, contains a tool-mark with seven compression points, a fifth-position lateral drag, and an asymmetric guide bite. In some cases, you’ll see secondary deformities: a damaged manufacturer die on the jig plate. This combination, present on every suspect coupon, matches only one manual override tool—logged as ‘certifier Lund’s issuance.’ The usage logs and badge access records show Petra Lund was the certifier on shift for every instance. She is in federal custody, facing processing.”

Celia’s eyes flickered, but her voice remained steady. “And the purpose of these forgeries?”

“To meet impossible deadlines and unlock compensation lines,” Iris replied. “But the intent wasn’t just corner-cutting. The coupons created a shadow inventory, masking out-of-spec materials across entire segments. The trail of signatures led to sign-off by Gerald Halveston, whose role as financier and sponsor for strategic procurement guaranteed the money flowed where the architecture demanded.”

Celia turned a page. “You included correspondence with Nadia Okafor. Her steering of your team is clear. Is she also implicated?”

Iris weighed that carefully—what to say, what not to betray. “Nadia Okafor steered me through every impasse. Her guidance is on record, and she has accounted for her actions fully with the federal investigation. She is not a materials certifier, nor the forger. She was my investigative partner.”

There was a beat, filled only by city noise far beyond the glass.

Celia lifted a legal sheet, heavy with redactions and hand-scrawled notes. “The co-owners’ ledger for the key asset remains incomplete. Parts are still redacted: corporate entities, nominee signatures. What do you know?”

Iris exhaled, tracing her memory of the page’s blank blocks. “I know that whoever owned the original asset—which the Directorate was built to monitor—still owns pieces of this network. The actual names haven’t cleared. My audit, the new one, pulls the routes as far as federal subpoena landed. But the thread is unfinished. Who signed as nominee and which shell owns what remains redacted. That’s the work left for whoever comes after me.”

Celia’s brow knit. “After you?”

“It’s public,” Iris said, voice stripped of pride or exhaustion. “My resignation is submitted. I won’t inherit the captured Directorate. Stellan Marsh architected the National Integrity Directorate as a capture instrument: the reform built to prevent the next Kessler is the mechanism by which he and the co-owners who financed Renn now control national procurement, laundering the same fraud at the scale of a country behind the credibility of the body meant to stop it.”

Celia’s hand hovered on a final folder. “For the record: Marsh is in federal processing. The audit confirms, from his own on-record routing approvals and policy authorizations, that he designed the structure?”

Iris nodded, feeling the fatigue in her bones. “He confirmed the procurement routes. His policy signatures and exception requests are all in the audit. The body meant to guard procurement was suborned by his own design and his sign-off. The same actors who certified the fraud moved with him, from old ownership into the new system. The evidence has been seized, and nothing in the findings is ambiguous.”

Celia’s posture softened. “Do you believe, after this, the system can change?”

Iris paused, images flickering behind her closed eyes: Nadia’s gloved hand passing her a file in a parking structure; Lund, pale in the interview suite, eyes burning with a fury that was half-defensive, half-defeated; Marsh’s signature on a thousand memos, invisible until it became all she could see.

“The ledger is incomplete,” she said, quietly. “Federal auditors have the evidence. Lund is in custody for coupon forgery; Okafor has accounted for her steering; Halveston’s signatures are matched to financier approvals. Marsh is in federal processing as the architect. But the true ownership is still in shadow. If the next iteration of this body is independent—if the public demands the redactions are lifted—maybe. But so long as the co-owners’ page is blank, it’s unfinished.”

“Why step away?” Celia pressed, with neither blame nor malice.

“Because the structure isn’t mine. It was captured before it existed. If I inherit it, I only validate the lines Marsh drew—the same architecture under a new name. Someone outside the pattern must finish the thread. That’s what makes the evidence public: it’s not for the Directorate, it’s for everyone who trusted it.”

Celia offered her the last word, and Iris only said, “Let the record remain open.”

The interview ended. The lights dimmed. Producers swept in to harvest cables and folders. Celia moved to unclip her microphone, but stopped, turning to Iris with a look that was both question and solidarity.

“I have the remaining materials secured,” Celia said quietly. “The tool-mark images, the badge reader logs, Lund’s override manifest. All tied to the chain. If anything happens—”

“It’s already happened,” Iris replied, gently. “They can air it. The sequence is enough. The unfinished thread is the only thing left.”

Celia packed her folders in silence. “You’re certain you want to step away?”

Iris smiled, bleak and tired. “I’ve already left. Before the door closed.”

The corridor echoed with institutional hush as Iris walked out. She passed the security desk, badge surrendered, the city’s stormlight hardening and reflecting in every pane. Her phone was quiet. Nadia’s last message—*We made it through*—remained unread, the weight of victory indistinguishable from the residue of loss.

In the morning, the feeds exploded. Petra Lund’s processing was public; her role as materials certifier and coupon forger, defined and inescapable, every signature matched to her badge records and the tool-mark sequence—seven compression points, the fifth-position drag, the asymmetric guide bite. Nadia Okafor’s guidance was footnoted, not as a conspirator but as an investigator who steered through the storm and then stood accountable. Gerald Halveston’s financier sign-off, less visible but unmistakably his, tied the purse strings to every exception. And Stellan Marsh, the architect, was named, federal processing status confirmed, his routes and policy authorizations the core of Iris’s submission.

Still, the ledger page for the co-owners remained: incomplete, blocked by redaction, the open wound through which all certainty bled away. The evidence package was online now, a mirror held to every public promise.

Iris walked the city, unknown in the crowd, unburdened of secrets but not of memory. She thought of the thread, unfinished, and wondered who would find the strength to follow it beyond what she could see—beyond Marsh and Lund, past Halveston’s ledger, into the silent spaces where ownership still hid.

She could only hope that, this time, the public would not look away.
