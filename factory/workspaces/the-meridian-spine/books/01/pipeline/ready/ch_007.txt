# Chapter 7: The Verdict Before the Test

"You lost the only evidence that justified your accusation," Stellan Marsh said, pushing an empty evidence slide tray across the polished mahogany table toward Iris Kane.

The sharp clatter of the tray against the grainless surface lingered longer than it should have. Iris didn't flinch, her thumbnail tracing the edge of her notepad instead, searching it for hairline imperfections that weren't there. She knew his type of game—control the frame, set the tone, and leave no room for the opposition to breathe. It was an art form Marsh had perfected over years of public life. Unfortunately for him, Iris wasn't in the business of suffocating before the facts did.

"I didn't lose it," Iris replied, her tone crisp enough to slice through glass. "It disappeared from Directorate storage. That distinction matters, though perhaps not to you."

Marsh leaned back in his chair, his cufflinks gleaming as he adjusted them with a deliberate flick of his fingers. The movement was slow, intentional, a gesture that said he wasn’t in a hurry to meet her at her level. "Perhaps not to the Directorate either," he said. "But to the public? Once this becomes an inquiry, you'll be the forensic authority who brought an accusation without substantiating evidence. That's an unkind epitaph for someone of your reputation."

Iris’s stomach twisted—not from fear, but from the sheer gall of his maneuvering. Stellan Marsh, the patron saint of "benevolent reform," wrapping his hands around her findings as though they were clay to be shaped. Her fingers stilled against the notepad as she met his gaze head-on. 

"The evidence existed," she countered. "The damage pattern on Batch 743 matched the Kessler override method at seven points, down to the same asymmetric bite and drag. That’s not an opinion. It’s the material speaking. The loss of a micrograph doesn’t erase the facts."

"It does in a courtroom," he said with disarming calm. "And it does in the court of public opinion, where this project's survival is debated every day. What you now have is inference, not proof."

"Your reform Directorate misplaced the proof," she snapped, her control slipping just slightly. "Not me."

Marsh inclined his head, acknowledging the barb as if it were a small curiosity instead of a direct hit. "Misplaced, stolen, or simply misfiled—I've already asked my staff to review the chain of custody on the imaging. But you and I both know that the longer we dwell on assigning blame, the worse the optics become. The Spine cannot afford protracted uncertainty."

Iris's jaw tightened. "Convenient that a premature conclusion solves your optics problem."

"No," Marsh corrected her. "Necessary. And your choice is remarkably simple. Either we certify the current record or..." His voice trailed, drawing out the pause like a seasoned performer. Then, with a flick of his hand, he sent a single sheet of paper sliding across the table toward her. "Or we authorize you to cut a replacement coupon. Forty-eight hours. Independent custody and testing."

She couldn’t even feign surprise—not fully. It was too neat, too prepared. The replacement would be a hollow gesture, a kind of procedural theater designed to appease her objections while keeping his narrative intact. Iris didn't reach for the paper immediately. Instead, she glanced at the hollowed-out evidence tray again, the absence of the slide more tangible now than it had been even in Ironwood.

"And when we find the same damage pattern as before," she asked, her voice cold, "will you let that rewrite the public narrative? Or will we see another slide vanish into the void?"

Marsh’s lips curved into a faint, almost paternalistic smile. "The Directorate's material chain is under corrective audit. I've already ordered the replacement coupon's custody to remain under your personal supervision from extraction to evaluation. If that’s not satisfactory, be direct."

Iris’s eyes narrowed. She didn’t need his benevolence; she needed a system that hadn’t been engineered to fail. “I refuse to sign any preliminary summary until after I’ve personally witnessed the entirety of the replacement process,” she said. “That includes extraction, storage, testing—everything. If you think I’ll certify anything based on someone else’s assurances, you’ve called in the wrong expert.”

Marsh didn’t flinch. "Understood," he said simply, as though her rebuttal had been predicted in his script all along. "The extraction is scheduled for fourteen hundred hours tomorrow. The forgery-proof containment has already been delivered to the site. You’ll have a full Directorate team at your disposal, headed by Okafor. You’ll supervise each step."

The mention of Nadia Okafor sent a flicker of tension across Iris’s mind. That particular name had already begun to knot itself into too many threads. If Okafor was steering her toward dead ends while fighting to shield the Institute’s reputation, then how was Iris supposed to trust her supervision on this? Marsh watched her, giving no indication that he perceived her hesitation.

"You seem remarkably prepared for something you’ve only just decided," Iris remarked dryly. "Prewritten statements of trust don’t make them true."

"Preparedness is policy here," he said, standing. His height gave him a quiet gravity, one he knew how to wield without ever being obvious about it. "You have your forty-eight hours. Take them. But understand this, Ms. Kane—the country doesn’t have forever. Succeed or fail, a conclusion will land. Make sure it’s one you can live with."

He left the empty slide tray and the authorization sheet behind. The click of his office door closing had a finality to it that somehow said "no appeals". Except there would always be appeals. Not to Marsh himself, but to the physical world and its refusal to compromise with fiction.

Iris didn’t waste time lingering. Taking the authorization form in hand, she committed every word to memory. Then she reached for the briefing packet he had pushed to the side, turning the thick folder to examine its edges. The Directorate seal blazoned its cover like a banner of legitimacy. Within moments, her thumbnail caught on something—a subtle irregularity along the third punch hole near the binding edge.

She tilted the paper, angling it under the light. One tooth of the archive puncher hadn’t gone clean through. A microscopic crescent of paper remained attached inside the hole, like a tiny hinged trapdoor. It wasn’t the kind of anomaly most people would notice, but to Iris, it was a fault line—a clue waiting to fracture.

That wasn’t all. Beneath the stack of documents, something far more damning revealed itself. Her eyes halted on the text stamped clearly on the final page: "National Integrity Directorate -- independent certification; Meridian Spine verified clean."

It continued: "Release scheduled for 0900 hours, one day prior to certification deadline." The timestamp was unmistakable. The press release stating the Meridian Spine’s cleanliness wasn’t just prepared—it was already scheduled to go out. And all of it "before" the replacement coupon’s results could possibly exist.

The room seemed to compress around her, her breath growing slow and deliberate as she stared at the impossible sequence in front of her. The conclusion had already been made. She could practically hear Marsh’s words echo in her ears: "Succeed or fail, a conclusion will land."

This wasn’t about physical evidence—not really. It was about shaping the narrative beyond the facts, deciding the public’s truth before reality had a chance to interfere. Marsh’s offer, his measured willingness to authorize her demands, wasn’t proof of fairness. It was insulation—a safety valve to preserve the structure of the machine he guarded, no matter what her forty-eight hours discovered.

The question burned now sharper than ever: Just how deep did those unseen gears turn—and who had written the script Marsh was now calmly performing?

Her fingers held the page tighter, the edges biting into her skin, but her eyes stayed locked on the words beneath the timestamp. Six hours. Six hours before she could find or confirm anything, the official story was already set to air. The Meridian Spine was certified clean by a process that hadn’t even happened yet.

She closed the folder, mind racing ahead of the clock. It wasn’t just about the micrograph. It wasn’t just about Petra Lund’s jig or the missing signatures. It was the entire structure of certification itself—and whether it had been designed to fail from the beginning.

And that crescent. That tiny, imperfect paper hinge. It had been punched into the briefing packet given to her today. The question wasn’t just "why". It was "who".
