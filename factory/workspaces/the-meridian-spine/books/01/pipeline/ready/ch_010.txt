# Chapter 10: The Phantom Key

"My credential number is on this access ledger," Iris Kane says, placing the printout on Nadia Okafor's desk. "At a time I was at Ironwood. Explain it."

Nadia did not look at the page. They had both watched the ghost entry emerge beneath the false one, both seen the scraped paper fibers and mismatched printer strike. The printout was not evidence for Nadia.

It was a demand.

"I already told you what I think," Nadia said. "Someone inside the building altered the ledger."

"No. You described the geography. I asked you to explain the route."

Nadia's office held almost nothing personal. A Directorate procedures manual sat square to the desk edge, its green tab dividers trimmed to identical lengths. Beside it, a ceramic cup contained six black pens, all capped, all aligned tip-down.

People who arranged pens like ammunition did not enjoy surprises.

Iris tapped the timestamp. "The biometric reader failed at thirteen fifty-two. The bay was opened at fourteen seventeen. The ledger was altered afterward. My credential number replaced a seven-character entry."

Nadia's gaze shifted to the printout at last. "We established that."

"We established what happened to the paper. We haven't established how someone chose a credential with an airtight alibi."

"They may have checked your calendar."

"My calendar showed field work. It did not show Ironwood."

"Your driver request did."

"I drove myself."

A pause.

"Then the gate log," Nadia said.

"The gate log is isolated. Ironwood does not transmit visitor records until close of business."

Nadia sat back. "You think someone followed you."

Iris ran her thumbnail along the printout's edge. The paper was cleanly cut except for one raised fiber near the corner. A tiny defect, almost nothing. Enough to catch skin.

"I think someone knew where I would be before the Directorate's ordinary records could tell them."

"That still gives us dozens of possibilities."

"Give me three."

Nadia's expression tightened. "This isn't an interrogation."

"Then it should be easy."

"Iris."

There it was. The careful use of her name, smooth and low, meant to turn a demand into an emotional excess. Nadia did that whenever facts started taking up too much room.

Iris pulled out the chair opposite the desk but did not sit. "You requested my Ironwood itinerary."

"For operational coordination."

"At eleven twelve that morning."

"You were removing evidence from a secured storage bay under hostile supervision. I needed to know where you were."

"You already knew where I was."

"I needed the timing."

"So did the person who selected fourteen seventeen."

Nadia's eyes sharpened. "Be precise about what you're implying."

"I am being precise. You had my destination, departure time, expected return, credential number, and authority profile."

"As your investigative partner, yes."

"You also delayed my request for the failed biometric reader's maintenance history."

"Because Facilities sent an incomplete packet."

"You returned the incomplete packet instead of preserving it."

"It was not responsive."

"It contained the technician assignment sheet."

"It contained an unsigned draft."

"Physical drafts have authors."

Nadia's jaw flexed once. "You are treating administrative judgment as obstruction."

"Administrative judgment is obstruction with nicer stationery when it consistently points in one direction."

Nadia rose. She was taller than Iris by enough to make the movement feel deliberate, but she kept both hands flat on the desk.

"You have exposed Petra Lund's falsified certification method," Nadia said. "You have evidence that the Meridian coupon bay was breached. You have a ledger entry designed to compromise you. Every hour we spend accusing internal staff without a complete chain gives Lund room to challenge your work."

"Lund is guilty of manipulating the coupon."

"And someone may be trying to make your proof unusable."

"Those are not competing facts."

"The public will treat them as competing facts."

The public. Nadia reached for it whenever the physical world became inconvenient, as if an audience could sand away tool marks.

Iris watched her. "Whose conclusion are you protecting?"

"The Directorate's ability to act."

"That wasn't my question."

"It is the answer that matters."

A soft chime came from the wall display. Nadia glanced at it before the notification expanded, which told Iris she had expected it.

A message appeared beneath the Directorate seal.

FINAL REMINDER: MERIDIAN SPINE CERTIFICATION STATEMENT

The prepared language followed.

National Integrity Directorate -- independent certification; Meridian Spine verified clean

Release authorization was scheduled for eighteen hundred.

Iris read it twice. No qualification. No mention of the repeat test, the resealed bay, the fractured coupon, or Petra Lund's override. The conclusion had survived every fact introduced against it.

Nadia touched the display and dismissed the notice.

"You knew that was coming," Iris said.

"We all knew."

"You knew the wording."

"It's the approved holding language."

"It isn't holding anything. It's declaring the result."

"The communications office requires a stable position until your repeat test is complete."

"My repeat coupon came from a bay that was opened after certification and resealed. There is no clean repeat test."

"Then document that."

"I did."

"Formally."

"I sent you the preservation notice forty-three minutes ago."

Nadia reached for one of the aligned pens, then stopped before disturbing the row. "It needs review."

"By whom?"

"Evidence governance."

"Name."

"The duty panel."

"Names."

Nadia's stare cooled. "You know that panel assignments rotate."

"So do machine heads. They still leave identifiable damage."

Silence tightened between them.

Iris picked up the printout. "I want the credential system audit."

"You cannot run that audit yourself."

"Why?"

"Because your number appears in the disputed ledger. You are now part of the evidence chain."

There it was. Not a threat. Worse. A procedure, ready in advance, shaped exactly to remove her hands from the proof.

"Who initiated that restriction?" Iris asked.

"No one initiated anything. It's standard conflict protocol."

"Show me."

Nadia turned to her terminal. Her fingers moved quickly, but Iris caught the half-second hesitation before she opened the policy directory. Not uncertainty. Selection.

Nadia brought up a document and rotated the screen.

The policy required recusal when a credential holder's actions were under investigation. The revision date was six months old. The approval block carried three departments, none belonging to Nadia.

Iris read the operative line.

"This applies when conduct is under investigation," she said. "My conduct isn't."

"Your credential was used."

"A number was printed in a paper ledger."

"The distinction may not survive public scrutiny."

"Then public scrutiny can learn to read."

Nadia closed the document. "You are too close to this."

"And you are remarkably eager to move me farther away."

Something flickered across Nadia's face. Not guilt. Iris distrusted easy expressions, especially the ones people expected investigators to collect like trophies.

It looked more like anger at a calculation refusing to balance.

"You think I altered the ledger," Nadia said.

"I think you have withheld records, redirected questions, and tried to make Halveston the endpoint before the procurement dates support it."

"Halveston financed the project."

"After Aegis entered the chain."

"He controls Veridian Trust now."

"Now is not three weeks before the first override."

"Money leaves trails that paperwork doesn't."

"So do tools."

Nadia moved around the desk, stopping just short of Iris. Her voice dropped.

"The statement releases in less than four hours. If you challenge it without an admissible replacement finding, Marsh's office will call your work incomplete. Lund's counsel will call it contaminated. The project authority will proceed under the existing certification."

"That sounds rehearsed."

"It sounds inevitable."

"Only to people who prepared for it."

For one dangerous second, Nadia's gaze fell toward the badge clipped inside Iris's jacket.

Then it returned to her face.

The movement was small. Too small to accuse. Large enough to preserve.

Iris stepped back. "Why did you look at my access card?"

Nadia's expression did not change. "Because this conversation is about your credentials."

"It's about the number printed in the ledger."

"Those credentials correspond to a physical card."

"Do they?"

Nadia folded her arms. "What exactly are you asking?"

"Whether the bay reader logs a number transmitted by the card or a number assigned by the system after authentication."

"You know how proximity credentials work."

"I know how they are supposed to work."

Nadia glanced toward the dismissed message on the wall. "Security can examine your card."

"No."

"If you believe it was cloned, that is the appropriate route."

"The same Security office that maintains the failed reader?"

"The reader outage and the ledger alteration are separate issues until evidence links them."

"Then my card stays with me until I make that link."

Nadia's restraint thinned. "You cannot treat every internal control as hostile."

"I don't. Only the ones that keep touching the evidence."

Iris left before Nadia could answer.

The credential laboratory occupied a windowless room two floors below materials testing. Officially, it belonged to access administration. In practice, it was a narrow workbench, a magnifier, two aging encoders, and a locked cabinet containing blank magnetic cards.

The technician had gone to lunch. Iris preferred the absence.

She signed into the optical station using the guest function, disconnected its network cable, and removed her badge. The front showed her photograph, authority class, and expiration date. A shallow crescent marked one corner where she habitually pressed it against secured doors.

Ordinary wear.

She turned it over.

The magnetic strip looked smooth beneath the bench light. Under ten-times magnification, dust became pale boulders and scratches crossed the surface in random arcs. Nothing suggested deliberate alteration.

At twenty-times, she found the lines.

They ran across the lower third of the strip: tiny, almost invisible, perfectly parallel. Too uniform for wear. Too shallow for a blade. The spacing remained constant through a length no wider than a grain of rice.

Iris adjusted the light.

The lines brightened, vanished, then brightened again.

A machine had touched the strip.

Not a door reader. Reader heads contacted broad areas and left soft abrasion over time. These marks were narrow and repeated, a mechanical chatter created during a controlled pass.

She took three micrographs and measured the interval. Each line sat twenty-eight microns from the next. There was a slight double strike at the end, where the head had paused before lifting.

The pattern bothered her.

Not because it was unfamiliar.

Because she had seen its shape that morning.

Iris opened the locked evidence sleeve containing the access-ledger printout. The false entry itself offered no magnetic trace, but the reverse side of the original ledger page had carried a dark transfer smear near the binding. She had assumed it came from storage against an old badge sleeve.

Under magnification, that smear resolved into parallel ridges.

Twenty-eight microns apart.

She placed the images side by side.

Same interval. Same terminal double strike.

Someone had rested, dropped, or pressed a freshly encoded card against the ledger before the strip residue had fully settled. The false entry and a duplicated credential had occupied the same physical workspace.

Her phone vibrated against the bench.

A message from Marsh's office filled the screen.

FINAL CERTIFICATION LANGUAGE WILL PROCEED AT 18:00. Submit any supported amendment by 16:30. Unsupported allegations will not delay release.

Measured. Rational. Perfectly framed.

Iris checked the time.

16:07.

Twenty-three minutes.

She looked toward the locked cabinet. Its inventory seal appeared intact, but the paper strip over the latch had a narrow puncture near the adhesive edge. Not torn. Lifted and reset using something thin enough to preserve the fibers.

The same kind of precise interference she had found in the archive.

Iris slid her thumbnail beneath her access card's edge, feeling for a separation in the laminate. The surface held firm. Whoever had copied it had not needed to dismantle it.

They had needed proximity, an encoder, and access to the credential template.

Her badge had been issued inside this building.

The access-ledger alteration had been performed inside this building.

And Nadia had looked at the card before Iris mentioned cloning.

Footsteps sounded in the corridor beyond the laboratory door.

Iris removed the micrographs from the printer tray and sealed them inside a clear evidence sleeve. The handle moved once, stopped, then moved again.

Someone on the other side had a key.

She held her Directorate access card beneath the white bench light, the faint etching on its strip a silent accusation. Her identity had not merely been borrowed. It had been copied, imprinted with a signature she now recognized.

And the system that had issued her badge was compromised.
