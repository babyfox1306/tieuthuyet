# Chapter 17: The Systemic Loophole

“Ms. Kane, you’ve been explicitly directed to remain confined to the Meridian Spine records,” the legal officer stated, his voice clipped and impersonal. Iris ignored him, her attention fixed on the column of file numbers that blinked on the terminal screen. She didn’t look up.

“You know as well as I do that confinement to one record pool won’t answer the questions I’m asking.” Her tone was calm, but her fingers betrayed her urgency as they tapped through another layer of dense data blocks.

“You are overstepping—”

“Am I?” she shot back, her eyes narrowing. “Or are you just underestimating what’s at stake here?”

The legal officer’s presence loomed for a moment longer, but when Iris continued scrolling—her movements deliberate, unyielding—he pivoted on his heel and stalked out of the room. She waited for the door to hiss shut before she exhaled, leaning back in her chair. The faint murmur of the Meridian Spine archive systems hummed around her like the undercurrent of a machine bigger than anyone present dared to acknowledge.

The air was stale under the white panel lights, tinged faintly with ozone from the servers, and Iris’s shirt clung to her spine. The only sound was the low tumble of cooling fans and the blip of her own keystrokes, the quiet symphony of secrecy.

She couldn’t stop here. Something larger was moving behind the curtain, and she had to see its shape, no matter how hard they tried to obscure it.

The oversight diversion clause was what had caught her attention, embedded in the dry language of internal financial agreements she’d accessed two days ago. The clause had surfaced in an unrelated calibration log related to the load-tests—one of Halveston’s closed operations. At first, it had seemed minor, a mere procedural note intended to direct inconsistencies to Advisory_Panel_C008. But now it was everywhere.

C008 again.

Iris rubbed her forehead, scanning the lines as her eyes stung from the pale blue light. The clause was simple enough to skim past. It rerouted questions or “irregularities” flagged during audits away from external evaluators and toward an internal panel—a quiet, automated detour that likely raised no alarms. Advisory_Panel_C008 became the sole node of accountability.

That might have been fine, she thought, if C008 itself weren’t a locked door. No names. No public structure. Just a filename that whispered through the documents like a ghost.

She cross-referenced the clause with the Meridian Spine data pools they had begrudgingly left open to her. It wasn’t just in Halveston’s operations. It wasn’t just in Meridian Spine. Wherever she tracked large-scale infrastructure funding—even those projects that barely grazed Halveston’s influence—the clause appeared like a signature buried in the folds of legal minutiae.

“Every time,” she muttered. Her fingers hovered over the terminal.

The room’s recycled air carried the faintest scent of electronics, static, and ink-smeared paper, stacked in neglected boxes against the far wall. A light flickered twice overhead before settling.

“I take it you’re not staying within your lane,” a voice drawled.

Iris startled, turning sharply to see Nadia perched in the doorway, arms crossed. Her face bore its usual sardonic detachment, but there was a skittish energy undercutting her posture.

“Why are you here?”

“Relax,” Nadia said, stepping inside. “I’m not here to report you to your new babysitters. Trust me, I’ve got better things to do than cozy up to legal.”

Iris didn’t trust her, but she didn’t have time to turn this into a standoff. “Then what do you want?”

Nadia’s gaze flicked to the terminal. “You’re still digging into C008, aren’t you?”

Iris didn’t answer, but her fingers pressed a quick sequence that blanked the most sensitive query logs from the active display. Just in case.

“Suit yourself,” Nadia continued. “But if you think you’ll get to the bottom of that rabbit hole through the front door, you don’t know how this system was designed to work.”

Iris froze. “What do you mean by that?”

Nadia gave her a thin smile. “I mean you’re trying to untangle something that was built to confuse. Everyone knows the clause is there—it’s been standard for decades. But no one follows the trail because they’re either too distracted by the surface, or they know better than to pry.”

“Too distracted by what, exactly?”

“By whatever Advisory_Panel_C008 deems fit to toss them as a diversion,” Nadia replied. “Or by the little fires Halveston and its peers are so good at starting. The clause isn’t the secret. It’s the design.”

Iris studied Nadia carefully, searching for holes in her words. After Nadia’s earlier betrayal, Iris had no faith in her former colleague’s sudden generosity. But some part of what she said resonated with Iris’s own conclusions.

“You’re saying it’s by design. On whose orders?”

Nadia snorted. “You think they’d have left a name on that?” She gestured flippantly at the terminal. “If you’re looking for a smoking gun with someone’s initials engraved on it, you’re going to be disappointed. Follow the money, Kane. It’s the only real breadcrumb trail they can’t erase.”

Iris wanted to press further, but Nadia stepped back toward the doorway, her expression flattening. “Don’t say I didn’t warn you. You keep pulling at threads, and you might not like what unravels.”

When she was gone, Iris returned to the terminal. Her mind buzzed with Nadia’s suggestion—Follow the money. She hadn’t needed the advice, but Nadia’s cryptic endorsement confirmed something crucial: the clause wasn’t a one-off anomaly. It was embedded in the system itself.

She cross-referenced contract frameworks again, this time expanding her parameters beyond any one company, project, or region. The same language—slightly adapted, but fundamentally identical—slipped through trade agreements, infrastructure budgets, technology licenses. Even where Halveston wasn’t directly involved, the clause reappeared, tying oversight to Advisory_Panel_C008.

Iris’s gaze drifted beyond the screen to a frost-glass window smeared with the city’s neon haze. She squinted, forcing herself back to the dull, blinking cursor and the vault of file indices. Her hands moved harder, a dull ache spreading in her knuckles. She adjusted the chair’s height by an inch, searching for the last bit of comfort before the next wave of files.

She leaned closer to the terminal, her pulse quickening. This wasn’t corruption confined to Halveston’s network of subsidiaries. The clause had metastasized across national infrastructure systems, treated like boilerplate language, unexamined, and unquestioned. How many projects had been quietly funneled through this loophole? And who controlled the advisory panel at its center?

She switched files, calling up archived Halveston correspondence where she could. A few snippets seemed to acknowledge C008’s existence, but all operational language around it was clinical, detached—a shield of plausible deniability. It was clear no one at the ground level had control over this mechanism. Halveston had been a tool, not the root.

Her chest tightened as she realized the implications. This wasn’t just negligence. It wasn’t just greed. It was systemic. Someone—or something—had designed it this way. And Halveston’s “fund” operations had only been a single piece in a much larger machinery.

Iris’s thoughts raced. If this clause was present across national contracts, its reach was unimaginable. She needed access outside of Meridian Spine’s networks. But how much longer would they let her continue before slamming the door shut completely?

Her terminal pinged softly. Another record loaded—a sprawling grid of contractor codes linked to the national-level projects she’d flagged. Advisory_Panel_C008 echoed through them all, its quiet authority stamped across a staggering range of critical infrastructure.

She scrolled faster, her breathing uneven. Suddenly, the screen froze. An external override error flashed across the terminal, and the files locked out, one by one.

Iris cursed, slamming her fists against the terminal’s edge. They were tightening the noose, and it wouldn’t be long before someone higher up came to shut her down for good. But it wasn’t the interference that left her shaken.

It was the realization that the clause wasn’t just an aberration clinging to Halveston’s crumbling operation.

It was everywhere.
