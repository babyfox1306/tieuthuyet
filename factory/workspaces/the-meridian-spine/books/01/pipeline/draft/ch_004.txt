# Chapter 4: The Executor's Purchase

"The fracture on the Spine coupon matches the Kessler override at every point," Iris Kane says, placing the micrograph on Petra Lund's desk. "I need to know who gave you the jig."

Petra did not touch the image.

That restraint told Iris more than denial would have. Innocent people grabbed for evidence. They squinted, argued scale, demanded context. Petra sat behind her immaculate desk with both hands flat against the surface, as if any movement might leave a print.

"You arrived without scheduling this meeting," Petra said.

"Fraud rarely respects calendars."

The micrograph lay between them, a black field crossed by a pale crescent. Under magnification, the fractured coupon edge carried seven compression points, each spaced less than a millimeter apart. The same sequence appeared in the Kessler override records Iris had kept for years.

Not similar. Not suggestive. Identical.

Petra's office overlooked the Directorate testing floor. Beyond the glass, technicians in white coats moved between machines with the careful calm of people who believed procedure protected them.

Iris had once believed procedure could protect people too. Then Kessler taught her that a procedure was only a promise written by whoever controlled the paper.

Nadia Okafor stood near the closed door, silent and composed. She had offered to attend before Iris asked, which meant either impressive foresight or a strong interest in controlling which questions reached Petra.

Iris had not decided which.

Petra looked at Nadia. "Is this an authorized examination?"

"The coupon is Directorate evidence," Nadia said smoothly. "Iris has authority to evaluate it."

That was not an answer.

Iris placed a second micrograph beside the first. "Left image, Kessler coupon. Right image, Meridian Spine Batch 743. Seven pressure points. Same spacing. Same shallow drag at position five. Same asymmetric bite where the guide plate shifts under load."

Petra's jaw tightened.

"Independent labs can use equivalent tooling," she said.

"Equivalent tooling produces equivalent dimensions. It does not reproduce damage."

Iris slid a clear evidence sleeve across the desk. Inside it rested the fractured Spine coupon recovered from the certification line. One corner was missing. Its broken edge exposed the internal deformation caused before the load reading had been recorded.

Petra's gaze dropped to the sleeve.

There it was. Recognition, quick and ugly.

Iris ran her thumbnail along the edge of the micrograph, feeling for a flaw that existed only in her head. "The override method required the coupon to be held below the sensor axis while the machine registered a false peak. That takes a custom jig. You used one."

"I certified the recorded result."

"You created the recorded result."

"I followed an approved exception protocol."

"Then show me the approval."

Petra lifted her chin. "It was verbal."

"How convenient. Words leave so little residue."

Nadia shifted her weight. "Iris."

The warning was mild enough to pass as professional concern. Iris ignored it.

She opened a slim document folder and removed the Batch 743 testing sheet. A rectangle of adhesive damage marked the lower corner where the equipment label had been removed.

"The original serial sticker was here," Iris said. "It was stripped after testing. The digital record lists the machine, but the jig field is blank."

"Accessory serials are not mandatory," Petra said.

"They were added to Directorate protocol after Kessler."

"For primary fixtures."

"This jig altered the load position. That makes it primary."

Petra's hands finally moved. Her right thumb pressed into her left palm.

A nervous tic, disguised as patience.

Iris leaned closer, not enough to invade, only enough to ensure Petra could not pretend the evidence was far away. "You used the same method employed at Kessler. You ordered a custom jig. Then someone removed its serial number from the testing record and peeled the physical label from this sheet."

"I did not remove anything."

The denial came too fast.

Iris watched Nadia instead of Petra. Nadia's expression remained professionally blank, but her eyes had settled on the damaged label area.

Interesting.

"I didn't ask whether you removed it," Iris said.

Petra went still.

Silence pressed against the glass walls. On the testing floor, a hydraulic ram descended with a low mechanical hum.

Petra spoke carefully. "A fabrication supplier modified an existing fixture."

"Name it."

"I don't recall."

Iris almost smiled. Petra remembered delivery dates, batch tolerances, calibration intervals, and the exact language of obscure exception protocols. Apparently, only supplier names caused sudden neurological collapse.

"Then the purchase file will refresh you."

Petra's eyes narrowed. "Those files are restricted."

"So was the coupon you altered."

Nadia stepped away from the door. "Petra, refusing access will trigger an automatic preservation order."

Petra looked at her. Something passed between them, too brief for Iris to define. Not surprise. Calculation.

Petra opened a drawer and removed a brass access wafer no larger than a postage stamp. Its surface was stamped with a tiny ladder pattern, each rung marking a different authorization level.

That was the signature detail Iris would remember later. Not Petra's face, not her denial, but the faint half-moon of dried red sealing wax caught in the wafer's lowest groove. Someone had used an old physical procurement seal on a modern access device.

Petra placed the wafer against the reader beside her monitor.

A procurement archive opened.

"Search Batch 743," Iris said.

"I know how to use my own system."

"Recent evidence argues otherwise."

Petra entered the batch number. Four purchases appeared: replacement grips, thermal sleeves, calibration blocks, and a custom alignment fixture.

The supplier field read Aegis Fabrication.

Iris felt the click of one physical fact locking into another.

"Open the fixture order."

Petra hesitated.

Nadia said, "Open it."

The purchase order filled the screen. A custom offset jig had been commissioned eleven weeks before Batch 743 testing, with tolerances matching the displacement visible in the fractured coupon. The order included a serial number.

AF-J9-4417.

Iris wrote it down.

"Print the original," she said.

Petra gave a short, humorless laugh. "You think paper will make this more real?"

"Paper has fibers. Fibers are harder to revise."

The printer beneath the cabinet woke with a sharp internal snap. One sheet slid into the tray.

Iris picked it up by the edges.

The paper felt heavier than standard Directorate stock. When she held it toward the ceiling panel, a watermark surfaced beneath the text: a narrow shield surrounded by the Latin words Lex ante lucem.

Law before light.

She had seen the same phrase pressed into correspondence from the legal office that handled Kessler's private ownership filings. Not on public reports. Not on court exhibits. On the pages that had arrived already redacted.

Her pulse gave one hard beat.

"Where did Aegis obtain this stationery?" she asked.

Petra's expression changed by less than a degree. Enough.

"It's a purchase order," Petra said. "Ask procurement."

"I am asking the person whose authorization code appears at the bottom."

Petra looked toward Nadia again.

Nadia's gaze remained on the watermark. "We should verify the serial before drawing a connection."

A sensible suggestion, delivered at exactly the moment the paper threatened to widen the inquiry.

Iris folded no accusation around that fact. Suspicion was not proof, however beautifully it dressed itself.

She photographed the watermark beside the printed order number, then sealed the sheet in an evidence sleeve.

"AF-J9-4417," she said. "Is that the jig you used?"

Petra's lips compressed.

Iris placed the fractured coupon over the diagram on the purchase order. The offset dimensions aligned with the crush pattern. Even through plastic, the relationship was visible.

"The jig held the coupon six millimeters below center," Iris said. "The fifth contact point dragged because the guide plate was machined out of square. That defect is on the fracture. It is also on this fabrication diagram."

Petra stared at the two pieces.

"I used the fixture," she said at last.

Nadia's eyes sharpened.

Iris did not feel triumph. Admission was air. The fracture was fact.

"To falsify the load result," Iris said.

"To bring the testing sequence within the required acceptance range."

"That sentence deserves an award for administrative cowardice."

Petra pushed back from the desk. "You think I invented this? The method was already validated."

"By whom?"

"I received the technical parameters with the supplier recommendation."

"From whom?"

"No name. A procurement packet."

"Physical or digital?"

"Physical."

"Where is it?"

"Destroyed under routine document retention."

Routine. The word had buried more crimes than secrecy ever managed.

Iris lifted the purchase order. "Aegis Fabrication supplied the jig."

"Yes."

"And you approved its use."

Petra's stare hardened. "Yes."

The executor, then. Guilty of her own act, not a confused technician following a mislabeled diagram. Petra had authorized the tool, applied the method, and signed the false result.

But the method had reached her already formed.

Iris straightened. "Your access is suspended pending preservation of the testing floor, the procurement archive, and every Batch 743 coupon."

Petra looked at Nadia. "She cannot suspend me alone."

"No," Nadia said. "But I can countersign."

For the first time, Petra looked afraid.

Nadia placed her credential against the reader. A red banner spread across Petra's monitor: ACCESS FROZEN.

Efficient. Almost eager.

Iris collected the micrographs, fractured coupon, and printed order. "No one enters the Batch 743 bay."

Petra's voice followed her toward the door. "Aegis built what it was paid to build. If you want the source, follow the purchase."

Iris stopped.

"That is what evidence does," she said. "It follows itself."

In the verification lab, she entered AF-J9-4417 into the manufacturer's registry. No result appeared.

She tried the batch prefix. Aegis records displayed serials AF-J9-4409 through AF-J9-4416, then resumed at AF-J9-4418.

The missing number was not random. It sat precisely where the Batch 743 jig should exist.

Nadia stood across the terminal. "A deleted serial proves a record gap."

"The fabrication diagram proves the object. The coupon proves its use. The missing registry entry proves someone removed its public trace."

"Or failed to enter it."

Iris turned to her. "Do you usually offer clerical innocence before or after the physical match?"

Nadia's face did not move. "Usually before we accuse a supplier with no chance to answer."

Reasonable again. Nadia had a talent for putting walls where doors had just appeared.

A notification sounded on the terminal.

An internal email had arrived from Directorate Corporate Review. Subject: Aegis Fabrication, change of control.

Iris opened it.

The message contained a scanned acquisition notice dated three weeks after the Meridian Spine contract had been executed. Aegis Fabrication had been purchased through a holding company whose beneficial owner was listed as Veridian Trust.

Nadia read over her shoulder. "After the contract."

"Yes."

"So Veridian did not own Aegis when the jig was ordered."

"No. It acquired the supplier after the tool entered the chain."

The timing mattered. It did not erase Aegis's role, and it did not prove Veridian had commissioned the jig. But purchasing the company after the contract meant purchasing its records, liabilities, tooling files, and control over whatever remained visible.

Iris opened the attached asset schedule.

Line forty-two listed specialized certification fixtures.

The serial range ended at AF-J9-4416.

Line forty-three began at AF-J9-4418.

The same hole had survived the acquisition.

Nadia exhaled slowly. "Halveston should be asked about Veridian's acquisition strategy."

There it was: a direction, delivered as an obvious next step.

Perhaps it was the right one. That did not mean Nadia was not steering.

Iris closed the email and preserved it to the evidence archive. "First, Aegis's physical tooling inventory."

"The company site was consolidated after acquisition."

"Where?"

"I'll find out."

"I'll find out too."

Their eyes met. No warmth, no trust, only professional recognition sharpened by the fact that neither woman had said what she suspected.

Iris returned to Petra's office long enough to serve the preservation notice. Petra signed without reading it.

"Whoever sent the procurement packet knew the Kessler method," Iris said.

Petra pressed the pen so hard its tip cut the paper. "I told you what I received."

"You told me after the coupon told me first."

Petra shoved the notice back across the desk. "Then perhaps your coupon can tell you why Veridian bought Aegis."

Not yet, Iris thought.

But paper had fibers. Metal carried wounds. Institutions left pressure marks of their own.

By the time she returned to her office, the corridor had emptied. A single white page waited beneath her door, aligned perfectly with the threshold.

Iris stopped before touching it.

No envelope. No handwritten impression. A typed message centered on plain stock.

She photographed it, lifted it with evidence forceps, and read.

"You're looking at the wrong supplier. Veridian Trust acquired Aegis after the fact. The real tooling vendor is still inside the Directorate. — A friend."
