# Chapter 5: The Inside Vendor

"You need to stop digging into our own procurement," Nadia Okafor says, her voice low. "The Directorate is the institution that needs to survive, not your private investigation."

Iris Kane doesn't look up from the note. She reads it a third time, memorizing the curve of each typed character.

*You're looking at the wrong supplier. Veridian Trust acquired Aegis after the fact. The real tooling vendor is still inside the Directorate. — A friend.*

She slides the paper into a transparency sleeve and sets it inside her evidence box.

"Iris." Nadia's fingers tap against the doorframe.

"I heard you." Iris closes the box lid. "I'm not investigating procurement for its own sake. I'm tracing how a custom jig entered the certification flow. That path goes through the people who authorize purchase orders inside this building."

Nadia steps inside and shuts the door. Her voice drops. "If you chase an internal supply clerk, you waste time the real architects will use to vanish. Halveston is the vector. He financed the Spine. He owns Veridian Trust. He has the money and the motive."

"Halveston bought Aegis after the jig was already in the chain. The acquisition was clean. It didn't create the tool. It only bought the company that held the paper. The decision happened before the purchase."

"Then the decision was Petra Lund's. She used the method."

"Petra used the method. She didn't approve her own requisition." Iris pulls up the file on her laptop. "Someone in procurement authorized a sole-source jig purchase with no competitive bid, no inventory serial number, no oversight. That's a procurement officer with override level."

Nadia's expression shifts almost imperceptibly. "Who?"

"Alan Torvik. He approved the requisition for Aegis Fabrication." Iris turns the screen. The scanned form shows Torvik's signature on the approval line. Dated three weeks before the first override.

Nadia reads it in silence. Her thumb runs along the edge of the desk before she speaks. "When did you pull this?"

"Twenty minutes ago."

"Did you talk to him?"

"I went to his office. He wasn't there. His assistant said he was in a meeting. I left a message." Iris closes the laptop. "I plan to go back."

Nadia straightens. "Then you have a name on a page. That's not a confession."

"Of course not. But it's a name that doesn't appear in any of the Spine's internal audit reports. Torvik is not listed in the procurement log for special tooling. The requisition was coded as a standard consumable. That's deliberate concealment eight inches below the radar."

"Concealment that could have been done by someone using his access," Nadia says. "His badge, his password. Or a forged digital signature. You don't know it was him."

"I know I have to ask him."

Nadia takes a breath. "Do you understand what happens if you pull that thread inside the building? The Director has given you extraordinary access. You've used it to confront a senior materials authority. Now you want to interrogate a procurement officer. The optics are a purge. The media will call it a witch hunt."

"The media will call it an investigation if it produces a conviction."

"Convictions are built on evidence that survives scrutiny. If you accuse Torvik and he's clean, you lose your standing. The Directorate will close ranks. You'll never get near the real source."

Iris stands. Their eyes meet. "You've been steering me toward Halveston since before I found the coupon. You came to Petra's office without being asked. You pushed me to question Halveston, and now you're pushing me to drop the internal trace. Every time I get close to something inside this building, you redirect."

Nadia's face remains still. "I'm protecting the institution that gives you the authority to investigate at all. The Directorate is not your enemy."

"It's not my ally either." Iris picks up her evidence case. "Neither are you, until I know whose direction you're following."

She walks past Nadia and into the corridor. The door swings shut behind her.

The procurement floor is on the fourth level. Iris takes the stairs. The building hums with the vibration of the testing floors below.

Alan Torvik's cubicle is in a row of identical gray partitions. His desk is clean. A single framed certificate on the wall—completed training in contract compliance. The computer is off. The chair is pushed in.

Iris stands in the opening. An assistant approaches from the next cubicle.

"He's still in the all-hands. Should be out by four."

"I'll wait."

The assistant hesitates. "I think he's booked solid after. Maybe you can send him an email."

"I'll wait." Iris sits in the visitor chair beside the desk. She pulls out her phone and reviews the micrograph image—the seven compression points, the drag mark, the asymmetric bite. She memorizes the pattern again. If the slide is lost, she still has the digital file. But digital files can be questioned. The slide was the primary.

She looks up at the ceiling. Two hours until four o'clock.

The minutes pass. People glance at her as they walk past. No one stops.

At 3:47, a man in a gray suit rounds the corner. He is tall, balding, carrying a laptop case. He sees Iris and slows.

"Ms. Kane?"

"Alan Torvik?"

He nods, eyes wary. "I got your message. This is about one of my requisitions?"

"Yes." Iris stands. "Aegis Fabrication. A specialized calibration jig requisitioned three months ago."

Torvik's face tightens. "I handled dozens of tooling orders for the Spine. I'd need to see the specific file."

"I have it here." Iris pulls out the printed requisition. "Your signature. AF-J9-4417."

He takes the paper, reads it. "Aegis. Yes, I remember. A standard calibration fixture for the new test frame. The supplier was the only one certified for that specific model."

"The requisition doesn't mention a test frame. It says 'custom calibration jig for specialty use.'"

Torvik looks up. "That's the same thing. The frame needed a custom jig to calibrate the sensor alignment."

"I reviewed the purchase records. The jig was never logged into the inventory system. No equipment serial number was assigned. The testing machine had its own serial number, but the jig field on the certification record was left blank."

He hands the paper back. "That's not my department. I processed the purchase order. After that, it's logistics and equipment management. I don't track what happens to the tool after it arrives."

"The purchase order was coded as a consumable under the general supply budget. Not as a capital tool. That classification bypassed the serialization requirement."

Torvik's jaw hardens. He glances at the assistant, then back at Iris. "The coding was provided by the requester. Petra Lund's office. They sent the category code."

"Petra sent you the code?"

"That's what I said." He crosses his arms. "If there's an issue with the classification, you should take it up with her. I approved what was submitted."

Iris holds his gaze. He doesn't blink. Either he is telling the truth, or he has prepared for this question.

"Thank you. I may need to follow up."

"Fine." Torvik steps past her into his cubicle and sets his bag down. He does not ask what the investigation is about. He does not ask why she is interested in one jig out of hundreds.

Iris walks away, her mind cataloging the gaps. Torvik's answer is plausible. It is also convenient. The category code came from Petra—and Petra is already exposed. He is deflecting responsibility onto a target she has already burned.

But if he is right, the procurement records alone will not prove him culpable. They will prove the paper path, not the intent.

She reaches the stairwell and stops.

Behind her, the corridor hums. The building is full of people, each one a possible source or a possible wall.

She takes the stairs down to the evidence locker.

The basement level is colder. The air smells of metal and concrete dust. She swipes her badge at the locker room door.

The lock clicks open.

Her evidence box is on the third shelf, label facing out. She pulls it down. The tamper seal is intact. She cuts it and lifts the lid.

Inside, the paper evidence is there. The printed procurement records, the tool-mark images, the note.

But the slide tray is missing.

The cardboard sleeve that held it sits at the bottom of the box. She lifts it out. Empty.

She tilts it under the light. A fine white powder catches the glare—fingerprint powder. Someone wore gloves, removed the slide, and didn't wipe the interior.

She photographs the sleeve with her phone. She photographs the empty box from three angles. Then she closes it and returns it to the shelf.

The logbook by the door records every entry. She signs out the log sheet for the last seventy-two hours.

Three names: her own, from earlier today. A security patrol at midnight. And, at 2:07 AM, a signature she does not recognize.

Initials: AT.

Alan Torvik.

She stares at the line. The ink is consistent with the other midnight entries. The time stamp matches the shift schedule.

If Torvik entered the locker at 2:07 AM, he had access to her evidence. And he knew the tray was there because she had logged it in that same morning.

She takes a picture of the log sheet.

Then she stands in the middle of the locker room, the empty sleeve in her hand, and the weight of what she has lost settles into her chest.

The micrograph was her only physical proof that the Batch 743 coupon had been manipulated with the same method that collapsed the Kessler Bridge. Without it, her case against Petra Lund rests on her word, the digital image, and the fractured coupon—which could still be challenged as contaminated evidence.

Someone inside the Directorate took the slide.

Someone who knew exactly which box to open, which sleeve to pull, and which surface to wipe.

And the initials in the log point to the same man she just interviewed.

Iris slides the photo of the log into her phone and walks out. The corridor is empty. The lights flicker.

She has Alan Torvik as a suspect for evidence theft. She has no slide. She has no confession.

And every question she asks from this point forward will be answered by people who already know she is blind in one hand.
