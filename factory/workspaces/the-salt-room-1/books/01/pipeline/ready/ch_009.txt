# Chapter 9: The Tape That Wanted Her

“Say her name first,” Agnes’s recorder clicked, and Nora’s knife stopped a breath above the final reel.

The blade hovered over the hub where the tape was spooled in its most damaged state. One cut and she could open the case, inspect the splices, decide if the oxide was too far gone for playback. The words from Agnes’s log— "To open, speak her name first" —had been buried in the last notebook entry, but the phrase replayed now in Agnes’s recorded voice, dry and matter-of-fact as a recipe instruction.

Nora lowered the knife to the worktable. The final reel sat in its box with the tidal-pool label half peeled, the paper brittle enough to crumble under a fingernail. She had already restored everything else Agnes had hidden. Labels, location keys, the log that named Della beside her in the salt room. This was the last physical object connected to the summer she was seven, and Agnes had wrapped it in a condition that required a choice.

Say her name first.

Nora pressed her thumb to the ridge of the reel case. The plastic was warm from the reading lamp. She could destroy the tape. Burn it in the hotel fireplace, grind the oxide into the salt that still dusted the corridor floor. Ruth had taught her that silence was the safest container for grief. But Agnes had preserved proof, and Agnes had built an instruction into the last piece of evidence.

Nora opened her mouth. The name sat in her throat like a stone lodged for twenty-two years.

“Della.”

The air in the room did not change. The tape did not unspool on its own. Nora felt only the release of a muscle she had not known she was clenching. She pulled the reel free, set it on the splicing block, and reached for her restoration kit.

Her phone buzzed on the desk. Ruth’s name lit the screen.

Nora let it ring twice before she picked up. “I’m working.”

“Don’t open that reel.” Ruth’s voice was tight, the same tone she had used when she warned Nora not to sleep in the salt room. “You will hate yourself for what you find.”

“You mean what you helped erase.” Nora slid the reel hub onto the cleaning stand. “Della had a life. You made sure no one remembered it.”

“I made sure you survived.” The line crackled. “There are things a seven-year-old cannot carry. I carried them for you. That tape will put them back on your shoulders.”

Nora held the reel up to the light. The oxide was faded in a crescent arc where the tape had been repeatedly wound and rewound to the same section. Overhandled, as if someone had listened to the same span of seconds again and again. Agnes, or Ruth, or someone else who needed to confirm what was there.

“If you destroy that reel,” Ruth said, “you go back to the version of your childhood that you can live with. If you play it, you become the girl who watched. You will not forgive me for letting you find it.”

“You did not let me find it,” Nora said. “Agnes did. She left a trail. You left silence.”

She ended the call.

The quiet wrongness came when she connected the reel to the playback deck. The restoration software initialized the file and assigned a creation timestamp that matched the current date, but when Nora opened the metadata, the work file listed a recorded date from the summer she was seven. She checked the laptop clock. Correct. She checked the software log. The file had been created two minutes ago. Yet the metadata showed a date in July, twenty-two years earlier.

Nora exhaled. The software had pulled the original recording date from the reel’s magnetic signature. That was normal for old tape. The quiet wrongness was that her laptop’s internal clock and the metadata should not have matched the year unless the reel had been played on this identical model of deck at that time.

She closed the metadata window and threaded the tape across the head block.

The first restoration pass delivered nothing but hiss. Nora adjusted the azimuth and ran a second pass. A sliver of room tone broke through, then a girl’s breath. Her own breath, she recognized the rhythm from the sessions in the salt room. She had been seven, standing near a microphone while Agnes checked levels.

“Say your name,” Agnes’s voice came through, distant and kind.

“Nora.” The child voice was high and unguarded.

“And who is standing next to you?”

A pause. Fabric rustle. The tape dragged, and Nora’s hand hovered over the splicing block. She wanted someone else’s hand there, someone steady who could tell her to stop or to keep going. The space beside the block was empty. Her fingers shook, but she let the tape continue.

“Della,” the child voice said. “My sister.”

Water against stone. A low, hollow sound that could have been the tidal pool filling at high tide. Agnes’s footsteps, then a count. “Pool marker seven. The girls are on the east shelf. Clear day, low breeze, water rising. Recording for baseline reference.”

Nora’s throat tightened. She was there. She and Della were standing at the pool, and Agnes was making a recording. The reel had been handled so many times at this exact point that the oxide was thin. Someone had listened to this section over and over, trying to hold onto the moment or trying to understand what it meant.

She ran the restoration again, pulling the sound cleaner. The child breath continued, then Della’s voice, almost identical to Nora’s but pitched a fraction lower. “The water is cold, Aggie.”

“I know, sweetheart. Stay on the shelf.”

“Nora is holding my hand.”

The words landed in Nora’s chest. She had no memory of that hand, none of the weight of Della’s fingers in hers. But the tape said it was true. She had been present, touching her twin, standing at the pool while the water rose.

The tape ended in a gap. A stretch of silence where the recorded signal vanished for exactly four seconds, then resumed with Agnes humming and packing the equipment. The gap had been created by someone pressing the pause button or physically lifting the tape from the head. Deliberate. A cut.

Nora rewound and listened to the gap three times. The tone between the last word and the silence held a faint pressure wave, as if a child had started to speak and the machine had been muted before the sound resolved.

She did not have the full memory. The tape refused to give her whatever came after Della’s last sentence. But she had proof that her seven-year-old self was there, that Della had been real, and that the tidal pool was the location where something had been cut out.

Nora unspooled the restored file and transferred it to her portable recorder. She dropped the recorder into her jacket pocket, closed the laptop, and left the splicing block empty.

The corridor to the back door was dim, the hotel humming with the low push and pull of the sea. She walked past the salt room with its closed door, past the scratched photograph in the frame, past the phone in the kitchen that Ruth had called from earlier. She stepped outside into the salt air and followed the path toward the cliffs.

The tidal pool gate was rusted, the hinges crusted with dried salt. Nora reached for the latch.

The portable recorder in her pocket woke without being touched.

Room tone spilled from the speaker. Old, dead room tone from the final reel, layered with the breath of two girls and the wet sound of stone. Then Agnes’s voice, counting for the log.

“Pool marker seven.”

A child’s breath caught. Not Nora’s. Della’s.

And beside it, the name.

“Della.”

Nora’s hand stopped on the latch. The recorder held the moment, waiting. She had not pressed play. She had not opened the gate. The recording wanted something from her on the other side.
