# Chapter 6: The Language of Shimmer

The palette knife jerked in Elara's grip as silver light struck the blade once, twice, three times.

She did not drop it. She held the knife steady and watched the way the metallic shimmer gathered against the steel. It did not scatter. It did not drift toward the windows or fade into the grey afternoon. It clung to the blade like a living thing testing its shape.

She moved the knife to the left. The light followed.

She moved it to her right. The light stayed where it was, holding against the empty air until she brought the blade back.

Not random. Not a trick of the glass.

She set the knife down flat on the wooden table. The shimmer abandoned it instantly and traveled across the surface of the table, across the scattered charcoal sticks and dried tubes of paint, until it reached the far wall.

The seam.

Not the seam she had opened in chapter four. A different one. Lower. Closer to the floorboards. The shimmer struck the plaster at the edge of the baseboard and held there, pulsing slowly.

Elara's throat tightened.

She picked up the knife again and crossed the room. Her knees pressed into the cold floorboards. The shimmer did not move. It waited.

"Show me," she whispered.

The light pulsed. Once. Twice. Three times. A pattern. A language without sound.

She pressed the tip of the palette knife into the seam. The plaster was soft, older than the rest of the wall. It crumbled at the edge of the blade like dried earth.

She worked the knife deeper.

The shimmer did not flicker or fade. It stayed fixed on the exact spot where the blade entered the wall. Patient. Silent. Awaiting discovery.

She drew the knife downward. A line of white dust traced her path. The board behind the plaster groaned. She set the knife aside and hooked her fingers into the crack.

The board came loose with a sound like a snapped bone.

She pulled it free and set it on the floor.

Inside the cavity was darkness. But it was not empty.

She reached in. Her fingers touched something soft. Dry. She drew it out.

It was paper. Rolled tight, pressed into the space behind the wall. She unrolled it.

Charcoal. Anatomical. A human arm rendered in precise, terrified lines. The hand was open. The fingers were spread. And from the wrist, branching upward through the forearm, was a root system. Black. Threaded. Exactly like the ink under her own skin.

But this drawing showed it finished. The roots reached the elbow. The shoulder. The neck.

She turned the paper over.

On the back, in a hand she did not recognize, someone had written a single word in smudged graphite: "Run."

Her breath stopped.

She ran her thumb over the word. The graphite smeared. It was recent. Weeks old, maybe. Not years.

She reached into the cavity again. Her fingers found more paper. Ash. A fine layer of ash that coated everything inside the wall.

She drew her hand back.

The ash clung to her skin. Dark. Greasy. And mixed with it were filaments. Hair-fine black threads that stuck to her fingers like cobwebs.

She held them up to the dim light.

They were not dust. They were the same material as the threads under her skin. But dried. Hollow. Brittle. She pressed one between her fingers. It crumbled into black powder.

This was what was left behind.

She thought of the cured patient she had seen in the lower corridor. The one being taken down the stairs despite being declared healthy. The hand that had dangled from the stretcher had been covered in black threads. Thick ones. Alive ones.

This ash in her palm was what happened when the threads dried up. When the body finished converting.

Or when the body was taken somewhere else.

She looked at the drawing again. The root system in the arm matched her own anatomy. The angle of the wrist was the same as the position she woke in every morning.

Tobias had drawn this.

Or someone like him. Someone who had seen what was coming and tried to warn the next patient.

She folded the drawing carefully and pressed it into her pocket. Then she reached for the small fold of paper she had brought with her.

The coarse alpine salt.

She had stolen four grains from the jar in the kitchen two days ago. Small pieces of grey-white salt with damp edges and a faint iodine smell. She had slipped them into her pocket when Cora had turned to check a delivery manifest. It had been a small act of rebellion. Now it felt like a key.

She pinched one grain between her thumb and forefinger.

She held it over her exposed wrist.

The black threads under her skin were visible in the grey light. They lay flat against the tendons, a branching map of quiet lines. They had spread overnight. They reached past her elbow now. She could see them when she flexed her hand.

She brought the salt closer.

The recoil was immediate.

The threads drew back from beneath the salt as if she had touched them with a live wire. They curled inward, bunching at the edges of her wrist, leaving the skin beneath pale and puckered. A low burn spread through her arm, deep in the bone.

She dropped the salt. It scattered across the floorboards.

Her heart hammered against her ribs. Her throat was dry.

The threads slowly, reluctantly, crept back into their original pattern. But they were thinner now. Fainter. As if the encounter had cost them something.

She stared at her wrist.

The salt was not a seasoning. It was not a kitchen supply. It was a suppressant. A restraint. A weapon against whatever was growing inside her.

She remembered the locked cabinet in Cora's office. The inventory sheets. The way Finch had looked at her when she had asked about the taste of the meals. The way the kitchen portioned everything exactly.

The clinic was built around salt. Not as a condiment. As a control mechanism.

She pressed her back against the cold wall. The ash was still on her fingers. The drawing was in her pocket. The salt was scattered on the floor.

She looked at the door.

The corridor was silent.

She wanted to show someone. She wanted to tell someone what she had found. She wanted a hand to reach through the dark and steady her.

The corridor was empty.

No footsteps. No voices. No one coming.

She was alone.

The thought was a door closing. There was no trusted hand. No one to run to. Tobias was locked in his room. Cora was the one she was hiding evidence from. Finch was the one who had put the threads in her veins.

She was the only one who would carry this.

She pressed her lips together.

She scraped a few grains of the fallen salt from the floorboards and folded them into a clean corner of the paper she had used to steal them. She added a pinch of the black ash. She added one of the dried filaments.

Then she took the palette knife.

The handle was thick. Varnished wood. She pressed the tip of the blade into the seam of the handle and twisted. The wood split slightly. She pushed the folded paper into the crack, deep enough that it would not fall out, deep enough to hide.

She would not confess. She would not hand the evidence to anyone in the clinic.

She would carry it herself.

She tucked the knife back into the metal drawer of the art table. She wiped her fingers on her skirt. She pushed the loose board back into place as best she could.

The shimmer was gone. The room was grey.

She heard the key turn in the lock.

It was not the sound of arrival. It was the sound of being found.

She pulled her sleeve down over her wrist just as the door swung open.

Nurse Cora stood in the frame. Her uniform was crisp. Her face was flat. She did not look at Elara's face.

She looked at the wall.

At the seam.

At the raw plaster and the scattered powder on the floor.

"Dr. Finch wants to know what you cut out of there."
