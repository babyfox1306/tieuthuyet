# Chapter 3: The Knife's Edge

Marget Halloway touched the edge of the knife blade to the moss and the moss seemed to shrink from it — then it grew back before she could blink.

The black-green film split open where the steel kissed it, revealing the pale, fractured stone beneath. She watched the wound seal itself, dark moisture pushing up from the fissure and spreading across the gap like a living thing closing a cut. The new growth was glossy, almost black, and it pulsed once, twice, before settling into the same texture as the rest.

She pulled the knife away and checked the blade. No residue. No stain. The metal was cold.

She scraped another patch. This time the moss did not wait for the cut. It flowed before the blade finished its arc, a slow, deliberate movement, as if the entire surface of the stone was a single organism anticipating the wound. The steel dragged across clean rock for less than a heartbeat before the moss covered it again.

Marget set the knife on the stone wall and rubbed her thumb over the callus on her index finger. The skin was smooth. She pressed harder. Still nothing. She put her thumb to her mouth and bit down. She felt the pressure of her jaw, but not the tooth against the skin.

The numbness was climbing.

She pulled out her ledger from the waxed canvas bag and flipped to the page she had started the night before. The damp had curled the edges of the paper. She wrote with her right hand, the one that still had feeling, while her left flexed uselessly at her side.

"Entry: Stone 1, East Face. 0817. Scrape test with steel blade. Regrowth interval: <3 seconds. Tissue response appears anticipatory rather than reactive. Temperature differential at wound site: -6°C relative to ambient surface. Behavior indicates active substratum with sensory capacity."

She stopped writing. Her left hand was still flexing. She held it up in the grey light. The tips were white. The nails had a bluish tint. The pale color spread down the fingers like milk being poured into water, stopping just short of the knuckles.

She tried to make a fist. The fingers curled slowly, clumsily, as if guided by someone else. She couldn't feel the nails against the palm.

"You're early."

She didn't startle. She had heard the clean, dry crunch of his shoes on the gravel path. Arthur Penhaligon stood at the gate, his hands in his pockets, his gloves immaculate. His shoes were dry. They were always dry, even when the ground was wet enough to soak through leather in minutes.

"The moss regrows instantly," she said without turning. "Did you know that?"

He said nothing.

She turned. "If you scrape it clean, it comes back in seconds. That's not normal. That's not any moss I've ever seen."

"You're not a botanist."

"I'm a stone reader. I know what growth on a surface looks like. This is defensive. It's reacting to the metal."

Arthur walked through the gate. He stopped ten feet away, his eyes scanning the stones, never resting on her. He pulled out his pocket watch, glanced at it, and tucked it away. There was no reason for him to check the time. The sun hadn't moved in the sky all morning.

"There's a reason the carving is done first, Miss Halloway. The tools are blessed."

"Blessed?"

"Tempered in salt water. It's the only thing that stops the cold from climbing the blade into the hand." He gestured at the knife on the wall. "That's a common blade. It will carry the cold into the hilt. Into your palm."

She looked down at the knife. It was an ordinary steel blade, the kind she used to clean the edges of the stones before reading. The metal was cold. Too cold for the ambient temperature. She touched the hilt with her right hand. The cold bit into her fingers.

"And if I don't have salt-tempered tools? What happens to me?"

He was silent for a long moment. The mist curled between them.

"You lose the hand. Eventually the arm. It stops at the shoulder. Usually."

"Usually?"

"Some people don't have the sense to stop digging."

She met his eyes. They were the same grey as the stones, flat and unreadable.

"What are we actually digging up from here, Mr. Penhaligon?"

"Marker stones. The job description hasn't changed."

"You're lying."

He didn't deny it. He just turned his back and walked toward the gate. "Watch which stones you touch," he said over his shoulder. "The central one has been there a hundred years. The others are younger. They aren't safe."

The warning hung in the air, cold and heavy. He knew exactly what she was dealing with. He just had no intention of telling her.

She left the knife on the wall and walked deeper into the graveyard. The mist thickened with each step. The ground beneath her boots was soft, spongy, the moss growing in thick carpets between the stones. She could feel the cold rising through the soles of her boots, seeping into the bones of her feet.

The raven was on the central stone, as it always was. It didn't move. It only turned its head to watch her pass, its black eye tracking her movement with an unnatural stillness.

She stopped at Stone 4, a smaller marker near the outer edge. The carving was newer, the letters sharper, less worn by the weather. She knelt and pressed her palm against it.

She felt nothing.

She blinked. Her hand was on the stone. She could see the pale skin of her palm against the dark, wet surface. She could feel the pressure of her arm pushing, the strain in her shoulder. But the stone itself was absent from her senses. It was a void, a hole in her perception.

She pulled her hand back and looked at it. The palm was pale, almost translucent. The white line had crossed the knuckles. It was spreading toward the wrist.

She touched the fingers of her right hand to the palm of her left. The right felt the left. The left felt nothing.

She pulled out the ledger again and wrote with her right hand, the letters coming out jagged and crooked.

"Nerve loss progression: Sensation absent in all fingers of left hand. Palm surface now registering zero thermal or tactile input. Estimated spread: 3.5cm from fingertip toward wrist since yesterday."

She tucked the ledger away and moved to Stone 7, then Stone 12. She knelt at each one, pressing her palm flat, hoping for a flicker of warmth, a grain of stone, anything. Each stone was the same. A cold void. A hole in her map of the world.

The warm spots were the fungal threads, she guessed. The cold was the stone itself, dying. She measured the temperature differentials, marking the coordinates in her ledger with the fingers that still worked.

By noon the mist had thickened into a white wall. She could barely see ten feet ahead. The raven's shadow never moved across the ground that afternoon — as if the sun did not reach that particular stone at all. It sat on the top of the central stone, an unmoving silhouette. The shadow was a black pool beneath it, untouched by the pale, thin light that fell on the rest of the graveyard.

She walked toward it. Her steps were heavy. The cold was rising from the ground, seeping through the soles of her boots. She stopped before the central stone.

It was larger than the others. Darker. The carving was deeper and older, the letters worn smooth by centuries of wind and rain. She didn't need to read it. She just needed to know.

Arthur's warning echoed in her mind. "The central one has been there a hundred years. The others are younger. They aren't safe."

She raised her left hand. The palm was open, pale, the fingers curled slightly from the stiffness. She pressed it against the dark, wet surface of the central stone.

The raven shrieked once, a raw, scraping sound, and took flight. It didn't go far. It landed on her shoulder.

She didn't flinch. She couldn't feel the weight of it. She couldn't feel the grip of its claws. The connection between her and the world was fraying.

She pressed her palm harder into the stone.

Her palm against the stone: she felt nothing. She pressed harder — still nothing. The numbness had crossed from fingers into flesh. It was in her palm. It was climbing toward her wrist.

She pulled her hand away and looked at it. It was a dead thing, pale and foreign, attached to her arm by habit alone.

"It reached my palm," she whispered. The words were swallowed by the mist.

The raven shifted its weight on her shoulder. It knew. It had always known.
