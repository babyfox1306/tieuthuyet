# Chapter 12: The Blow Marget Halloway Cannot Feel

Marget Halloway swung, and Arthur Penhaligon caught the maul with both gloved hands.

The impact jarred up her arms but she felt none of it — only the vibration in her shoulders, the way her grip slipped because her fingers could not register the handle. He had come from the barrow entrance without a sound, and now he stood between her and the central stone, his breath steady, his eyes fixed on the maul as if he were disarming a child.

"You will not," he said.

She tried to pull the maul back. His grip did not yield. The leather of his gloves creaked against the wooden haft, and for a moment they stood locked, neither strong enough to claim the weapon entirely. The raven watched from the central stone, its head angled, its beak closed.

"The network dies now," she said.

"You have no idea what you are ending."

"I know exactly what I'm ending." She wrenched the maul sideways, breaking his hold. The momentum carried the head past his shoulder and the iron struck the ground beside the central fracture, throwing up dirt and shattered moss. The blow landed wrong — the fracture held, the stone remained intact.

Arthur stepped back and began pulling at his gloves.

"You read my ledger," she said. "You flooded the lower graves. You knew what was happening here, and you let it continue."

He did not answer. His fingers worked the leather free of his right hand, then his left. The gloves came off slowly, deliberately, and Marget saw what he had been hiding since the day they met.

His palms were crosshatched with black root scars that branched toward his wrists. Tiny circular punctures dotted the pads of each finger, some healed white, others rimmed with red where the skin had not closed. And the skin itself — it was not the skin of a man his age. Patches of young, smooth tissue grew in narrow islands between stretches thin as old paper, translucent enough that she could see the blue of veins beneath.

The harvest had marked him the way stone reading marked her.

"This is what it costs," she said.

Arthur flexed his fingers and the scars pulled taut. "You read stone until your hands die, and for what? To send corpses to cold earth? I use what the network gathers. I do not let it rot."

"You fed it Oswin Kerrow."

"Kerrow understood the arrangement. He chose the anchor so the others would remain whole. I did not force him."

"He was your friend."

Arthur's jaw tightened. "He was useful."

The raven shifted on the stone, wings rustling. The cold that had pressed against Marget's skin since she entered the barrow seemed to gather, thickening around her legs, her arms, her chest. She could not feel it as temperature — her hands were beyond that — but she felt it as weight, as memory, as the shape of someone who had once held her steady.

She lifted the maul again.

Arthur did not move to stop her. He stood with his exposed hands at his sides, watching her align the head with the fracture line. She had to look — she could not feel whether the iron was square to the crack. Her fingers were dead things wrapped around the haft, obeying only sight and memory.

"If you shatter that stone," Arthur said, "you lose everything. The network feeds on the dead. When it dies, the ground will sink. The stones will settle. Half the graves will be unreadable within a decade."

"They were never yours to use."

"The dead do not care."

"Oswin cared." She set her feet. "He carved his mark into that bone so someone would find him. So someone would understand. He did not choose to become your engine."

Arthur said nothing. His silence was the only confirmation she would ever get.

She swung.

The maul came down in an arc she could not feel, guided only by the angle of her shoulders and the line she had drawn in her mind. The iron struck the central stone at the apex of the fracture, and the sound that came back was not the ring of metal on granite but something else — a wet, splintering crack, like bone giving way.

The fissure widened. Black filaments writhed at the edges of the break, curling back as if the stone itself had been alive and was now bleeding air.

She swung again.

The second blow split the stone in two. The halves fell outward, and the cavity inside was not solid rock but hollow — a chamber lined with grey moss that had grown thick as felt, pulsing with a slow, dying rhythm. At the centre of the chamber lay a femur, white and polished, carved with the same date she had read in the fragment. Beside it, pressed into the moss like a fossil, was the shape of a man — ribs, spine, skull, all consumed into the matrix, all that remained of Oswin Kerrow.

The cold released.

It did not fade gradually. It vanished as if it had never been there, as if the barrow had been warm all along and she had only now become aware of it. The pressure left her chest. The frost on the grass began to bead into water. The filaments that had stretched from the stone across the graveyard floor turned grey and brittle, curling into dust.

The raven opened its beak.

It made no sound. It simply released its grip on the broken stone — the motion unhurried, deliberate — and pushed itself into the air. Its wings beat once, twice, and then it was circling, circling above the place where the central stone had stood, its feet lowered as if expecting purchase that no longer existed.

Arthur Penhaligon picked up his gloves from the ground.

He did not look at the shattered stone. He did not look at the remains of Oswin Kerrow. He looked at Marget — at her hands, still wrapped around the maul — and his expression was not anger but something older, something tired.

"You cannot feel that," he said.

She looked down. The haft was in her grip. She could see her fingers curled around it, but she could not feel the wood, the grain, the vibration of her own pulse. She could not feel whether she was holding too tight or too loose.

She let go.

The maul fell. She heard it land, felt nothing.

"Your hands," Arthur said.

"Gone."

"Then you are finished."

She raised her empty palms and looked at them. They were the same hands she had always had — callused, scarred, the nails short from nervous biting — but they were no longer hers in any way that mattered. She could move them. She could close them. She could not read stone. She could not feel cold. She could not hold the last trace of Oswin Kerrow's presence, that familiar pressure she had carried since she first entered this graveyard.

She could not even feel grief.

Arthur turned and walked toward the barrow entrance. His bare hands hung at his sides, the scars catching the pale light. He did not hurry. He did not look back. The residual warmth of the harvested essence — whatever he had managed to preserve — still clung to him, a faint shimmer in the cold haze, and he carried it like a second skin.

Marget did not follow him.

She stood in the silence of the barrow, surrounded by the bones of the dead, the dying moss, the settling ground. The central stone lay in pieces at her feet. The femur of Oswin Kerrow rested in its hollow, polished and clean, untouched by the collapse.

She knelt and reached for it.

Her fingers touched the bone. She felt nothing. She pressed harder, trying to find the carved date, the chisel marks, the texture of his labor. There was only pressure — if she could even call it that — a distant sense that something was against her skin, but no warmth, no cold, no grain.

She could not tell if she was holding Oswin's bone or a piece of the stone.

She did not know if he had wanted to be freed. She did not know if he had known she would come. She did not know if the cold was him, or if the cold was only the network using his body as an anchor, and what she had felt all those weeks was nothing but hunger.

"Oswin," she said.

The name hung in the air. No cold answered. No pressure gathered at her back. The barrow was empty of everything except the dead and the woman who could no longer touch them.

Above the empty place where the central stone had stood, the raven circled once more, lowered its feet toward nothing, and beat its wings against the fall.
