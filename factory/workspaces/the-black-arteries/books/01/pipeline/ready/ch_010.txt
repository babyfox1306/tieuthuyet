# Chapter 10: The Peat Routes

One path keeps her, and the other black route opened under the floorboards.

Clara stood at the junction where the vascular threads forked in opposite directions. The left branch climbed the wall toward the upper archive, dense with sleeved records she had preserved over eight days. The right branch had torn a seam across the floorboards, splintering the aged oak into a dark mouth that exhaled the same blood-warm air she had tasted in the stairwell.

She knew now what fed the system. Eleanor had not died in any ordinary sense—she had dissolved into the forbidden records, her memory and physical mass merged with something ancient that lived in the peat below the estate. The black ink was Eleanor, and every document Clara saved was fuel.

The left path wanted her to keep climbing. To continue preserving. To feed.

The right path had opened when she spoke the truth aloud.

She shouldered her satchel, checked the position of her brass pocket watch—winding it twice, the way she did when her pulse climbed—and stepped into the opening.

The descent was not a stairwell. It was a sloped tunnel lined with roots so fine they looked like hair pressed into wet clay. The walls pulsed with faint movement, the black strands contracting and relaxing in a rhythm that matched her own breathing. She counted steps to keep herself anchored. Forty-two until the floor leveled into a chamber whose ceiling hung so low she had to crouch.

The air smelled of wet iron and decayed vegetation. No perfume. No breath. Eleanor was here only as the stain that rippled across the water pooled at her feet.

Clara knelt and pressed her palm to the floor. The flagstones were slick with a film of black water that reflected no light. Beneath them, she felt a steady vibration, deep and slow, like a heart beating through stone.

She took a loose scrap from her satchel—a corner of unsaved paper she had carried since the first day, unprocessed, unstained. She laid it flat on the black water. For three seconds nothing happened. Then a thin root rose from the film, touched the paper's edge, and withdrew. The scrap remained dry, unchanged.

She tried again with a sleeved page from her satchel, one she had preserved in Mylar the previous afternoon. The moment the sleeve touched the water, the roots responded. They coiled around the plastic, split the seal, and sank into the paper. Within seconds the page blackened, the ink rising from the fibers like blood wicking into gauze.

The preservation fed it. Every careful act of keeping made the roots stronger.

Clara pulled the page back and watched the black stain bleed across her fingers. She wiped them on her coat and stood.

The chamber had three exits: two narrow tunnels and one open grate set into the floor. The grate was iron, corroded to orange brittleness, and beneath it she could see water moving. Not the stagnant flood she had waded through above—this was darker, thicker, carrying grains of peat that swirled like sediment in a river.

She lifted the grate with her crowbar. The hinges screamed but held.

Below was a channel carved into the raw earth, its walls lined with the same black vascular roots she had seen threading through the archive. The water in the channel was the color of old tea, almost black, and it moved with a purpose the floodwater lacked. It had direction. Flow.

She dropped a pebble. It landed with a soft splash and the roots around it contracted, then released.

Physical conduits. The archive above, the observatory, the lower vaults—they were all connected to this network. The same peat-dark channels that ran under the entire estate, feeding the bog, feeding the parasite, feeding Eleanor's preserved memory.

She found a crack in the ceiling where a sliver of afternoon light broke through. She took her magnifying glass from her satchel and focused the beam onto a root that extended from the wall. The moment the concentrated sunlight touched it, the root blackened further, crisped, and crumbled into brittle charcoal that fell into the water and dissolved.

When she touched the same root afterward, it was dead. Inert.

But the moment she brought a preserved document—a journal page she had sleeved the day before—within an inch of the water surface, new roots erupted from the channel. They climbed the wall, reached for the paper, and the dead strand on the floor began to pulse again.

Sunlight killed it. Preservation resurrected it.

Clara sat on the edge of the grate for a long minute, watching the cycle repeat. The truth was a loop she couldn't break without destroying the very thing she had been trained to protect.

She took out her pocket watch and wound it. Twice. Three times. The mechanism clicked against her palm like a heartbeat she could control.

When she stood again, her legs were unsteady. She walked the perimeter of the chamber, cataloguing what she saw: iron rings embedded in the walls, the remains of wooden sluice gates rotted to mulch, a pressure-gauge dial whose face was obscured by green corrosion. She wiped the glass with her sleeve and read the numbers beneath.

The gauge was calibrated in feet. The needle rested at forty-eight, which she guessed was the depth of the water table below the observatory's foundation. There was a second needle, painted red, fixed at zero.

She pried the gauge from its mounting with the crowbar. Behind it, carved into the stone, was a map.

The map showed the observatory as a rectangle overlaid with concentric lines—contours of the peat basin beneath. A series of tunnels branched outward like the roots she had been following, and at their terminus a notation in faded red ink: "Pressure gates — 6 positions — flood return to basin."

Flood return. The channels could be opened. The peat water could rise and fill the observatory, submerging the archive completely. Drowning the roots. Starving the parasite of the dry records it fed on.

She traced the tunnels with her finger. The notation was precise, angled, written in a hand she did not recognize. It listed gate positions and the volume of water each release would admit. The total was enough to sink the entire structure twenty feet below the bog's surface.

Clara pulled the map from the wall carefully, protecting the edges from crumbling. She slid it into a new Mylar sleeve from her satchel—and stopped.

She had just preserved a document that described how to destroy the archive.

The irony pressed against her ribs. She sealed the sleeve anyway. The map was a record. It deserved the same care as every other piece of paper in this building. She would keep it, study it, find another way.

But as she turned to leave, the black water in the channel changed.

The roots that lined its walls detached in long ribbons, drifting upward toward the grate she had opened. They did not climb toward her. They arranged themselves on the surface of the water in a pattern she recognized from the sluice gauge: six parallel lines, crossed by a seventh at the midpoint.

The exact shape of the pressure gates.

And beneath that pattern, the peat water rose.

No sound. No ripple. The level climbed one inch, then held, as if the channel were testing her permission.

Clara held the sleeved map against her chest, her breath locked in her throat. The roots held the shape. The water waited.

She did not lower the grate.

She did not agree.

She only stood there, watching the black ink trace the architecture of drowning, while the silence pressed in from every side and no one spoke her name and no hand touched her shoulder and the only proof that Eleanor Vance still existed was the stain, the water, the rising dark.
