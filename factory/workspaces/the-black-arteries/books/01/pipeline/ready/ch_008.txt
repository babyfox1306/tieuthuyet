# Chapter 8: The Sister-Linked Route

"The map is under the floor," Clara said, as the glass plates lifted one blackened edge toward the drowned stair.

The words were not spoken to anyone. She said them the way she catalogued a water stain or measured a shelf's deflection. A statement of fact. The branching pattern from the star chart was not symbolic. It was structural.

She dropped to her knees. The water in the corridor was knee-deep, cold enough to numb through the rubber of her boots. The glass plates were set into the iron gridwork that formed the observatory's main floor. Most were fogged with age or clouded by the black fungus that grew in the stagnant air. But this one section had cleared.

The light from her lantern hit the water at an angle that turned the submerged floor into a developing photograph. The black roots, usually a tangled chaos, were aligned. They formed a specific line, a pattern she had traced onto graph paper the night before. The negative space matched exactly.

She wound her pocket watch. Once. Twice. The sound of the spring coiling was a ritual, a prayer against the disorder of the archive.

"Confirmed," she said. "The vascular pattern from the C008 plate is structurally identical to the root distribution beneath the main corridor. The route exists."

She followed it.

The water deepened as she moved away from the staircase. Up to her thighs. Her waist. The hem of her oilskin coat dragged through the surface, sending ripples across the black-stained water. The roots on the submerged shelves trembled as she passed, reaching toward her coat, her hands. She held them away.

The route ended at a square recess in the iron grid. A hatch, sealed with a thick black deposit. The roots had grown into the hinges, forming a living lock that glistened wetly in the lantern light. The metal of the ring was pitted with rust, oxidized into a orange crust that flaked at her touch.

She found a crowbar in the old workshop, a tool forgotten in a drawer of dry rust. The weight of it was solid in her hands, the only clean metal she had touched in days.

She brought it back to the hatch. The crowbar bit into the seal. The roots shredded, releasing a thick black sap that dissolved into the water like blood in rain. She pried the hatch open.

The iron groaned. The roots screamed in silence, their filaments snapping as the seal broke.

A narrow stairwell descended into absolute darkness. The water was ink-black, so dense the lantern light barely penetrated the surface. The steps were slick with algae and silt, each one a hazard.

She went down.

The stairwell was a throat. The water grew warmer as she descended, losing the chill of the main floor. It felt like blood temperature. She counted the steps. Twelve. Fifteen. Twenty.

The lower cabinet stood at the bottom of the stair, half-buried in silt and debris. It was a steel strongroom, ancient, with a door that had been forced open by decades of water pressure. The interior was a ruin of collapsed cardboard boxes and dissolved paper pulp.

But the central drawer was intact.

It was made of thick oiled mahogany, sealed with a brass lock that had resisted the water. The nameplate on the drawer was worn, but she could read the old accession number etched into the brass. C009. The number from the notation she had found on the star chart.

She took her lock picks from her coat. Her hands trembled as she worked the tumblers. The lock sprang open with a dry click that echoed in the flooded chamber.

She pulled the drawer open.

The air that escaped was cold and smelled of old earth and something else. Something dry. Preserved. The smell of a specimen case that had never been fully submerged.

Inside, the records were arranged with deliberate care. A childhood label in faded pencil on aged paper. A small glass vial containing the curled skeleton of a pressed fern. Three accession slips in Eleanor's exact handwriting, the looping crossbar on the 'E' unmistakable. A fragment of a letter, the paper blackened at the edges but the writing still visible.

She leaned closer to read the letter. The ink was brown with age. "The bog will preserve everything, Clara. You must trust the method."

She recognized the handwriting. It was Eleanor's. The same hand that had written birthday cards. The same hand that had filed specimens in the observatory's original collections.

The roots on the rim of the drawer stirred.

They uncurled from the wood, stretching toward the drawer. Toward the name slips in Eleanor's hand. The childhood label. The letter fragment.

Clara's hand hovered over the documents. The roots paused. They trembled. They bent toward her fingers.

She could feel the pull. A magnetic draw. A hunger in the root tips.

She marked her waterproof paper with clipped precision. "Correlation confirmed. Root velocity is significantly higher around the sub-sample of Eleanor-linked records. Branching density is concentrated on the childhood label and the letter fragment. The reaction appears directional."

She did not say what she felt. That the roots seemed to recognize the pattern. That they knew the name.

She waited for a response.

Silence.

No sound from the water. No scent from the roots. No ghost shape forming in the air. Just the visual evidence. Thicker roots. Faster roots. Roots that bent toward her as she reached into the drawer.

She sleeved them.

One by one. The name slips. The childhood label. The letter fragment. She slid each one into a clean Mylar sleeve, her movements precise, her breath steady. The roots reacted violently. They lashed against her hands, leaving thin black lines on her skin. The water in the drawer began to drain, sucked away by the expanding root mass.

She knew the truth of what she was doing. Every preserved record strengthened the black growth. Chapter six had proven that beyond doubt. The Mylar sleeve would act as a greenhouse. The roots would thicken. The records would become vessels.

She sleeved them anyway.

"I am an archivist," she said. Her voice was flat. Procedural. "Preservation is the primary directive. Cataloguing the correlation is the secondary directive. The consequences are tertiary."

She closed the drawer. The roots held it open, too thick to seal the wood.

The water level had risen. It was over the lip of the cabinet now, seeping into the drawer where she had left the rest of the collection.

She backed up the stair, the crowbar still in her hand. The water in the main hall was higher than when she had descended. It must have been rising while she worked.

She turned back to the open hatch. The water inside the cabinet was black and still.

The light from her lantern hit the surface and refracted.

Inside the lowest drawer, every neutral record lay flat. They floated on the surface of the black water, dissolving into the liquid. The accession slips from unrelated collections. The blank paper. The dried specimens that had no name attached.

But the Eleanor-linked slips stood upright.

They pierced the surface like white grave markers, each one perfectly vertical. The Mylar sleeves caught the lantern light and reflected it in pale flashes. Around each sleeve, a halo of black roots. Thick, pulsing, awake.

The roots bent toward her marked knuckles.

She looked down at her hands. The ink had settled beneath her skin in a pattern of branching lines. It was the same pattern as the roots. The same pattern as the star chart. The same pattern as the map beneath the floor.

She wound her watch again. The spring was tight. It could not be wound further.

"I am the map," Clara whispered. "She drew my route into the paper."

The sister-linked route was not a path through the building.

It was a path into the archive.

And she had followed it all the way to the bottom.
