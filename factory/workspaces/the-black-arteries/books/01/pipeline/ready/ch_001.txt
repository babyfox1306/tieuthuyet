# Chapter 1: The First Black Thread

Do not let the ledger touch the black water, Clara told herself, and caught it one second before the current took the names.

The spine was slick with moisture, the leather cover soft as skin where flood had soaked it. She held it at arm's length, examining the damage while her boots settled into the inch of standing water that covered the observatory floor. A brass label-holder had been nailed to the front board—the metal was pitted with verdigris, the glass cracked, but the paper inside was still legible: "Sky Catalogue, 1887–1892, Shelf E-4."

Clara shifted the weight of her satchel and balanced the ledger on her forearm, then took a breath that tasted of rust and old paste. The room was a collapsed Victorian dome, the original glass roof half gone, replaced by tarpaulins that sagged under their own collected rain. Starlight had once been recorded here. Now the only light came from her battery lamp and the green-brown glow that filtered through the algae-thick windowpanes.

She stepped forward, and the water rippled around her ankles.

The archive had been submerged for at least a year, maybe longer. The floor was scattered with fallen shelves, their contents fanned into the shallows like rotted leaves. The shelving that still stood leaned at improbable angles, their legs stained with a dark tide line that rose almost to waist height on some. Oxidation had painted everything in shades of ochre and rust. But the paper—the paper was what mattered.

Clara set the rescued ledger on a relatively dry shelf and pulled a roll of provisional number stickers from her pocket. She wrote "Obs-001" on a strip of waterproof label, pressed it to the spine. One down. She did not know how many hundreds remained.

Her assistant was supposed to have arrived this morning. He had not come. The ferryman had said the road was washed out, but she suspected the man had taken one look at the observatory and decided a sick relative was a better excuse than drowning alone in a bog with a woman who catalogued dead records. She could not blame him. The silence here was not empty; it had a texture, a weight that pressed against the eardrums.

Clara wound her brass pocket watch without removing it from her vest—three half-turns, the practiced motion steadying her hands. The rhythm was familiar, a small order imposed on a disordered room. She let the ticking sound fill her ears for a moment, then turned back to the shelves.

She began with the star charts. Stacked on a tilted shelf, they were rolled in waxed paper tubes, most of which had split at the seams. She pulled one carefully, and the chart inside slid out in her gloved hands—a celestial map of the northern hemisphere, hand-drawn in sepia ink, with annotations in a cramped Victorian hand. The edges were soaked, the paper soft as cloth, but the centre was still legible.

She had already labelled it "Obs-004" when she noticed the black thread.

It ran from the bottom edge of the chart, curving upward through the constellation Lyra, then branching into a finer network of lines that ended in tiny, perfect nodes. She held the lamp closer. The thread was not ink; ink bled, diffused, spread in irregular blooms. This line was too narrow, too consistent in width, and the branches split at angles that looked organic.

"Vascular," she thought. "Like capillaries."

She turned the chart over. The black did not bleed through to the back. It sat on the surface as if painted there last week, not a century ago.

Clara set the chart aside and made a note in her journal: "Obs-004: black contamination, possibly chemical reaction with floodwater sediment. Surface deposit, non-penetrating. Isolate for later analysis." The words felt rational, clean. She did not want to think about the way the line seemed to follow the curve of the constellation as though it were tracing a path.

She filed the chart in her waterproof carrier and reached for the next tube.

Paper after paper revealed the same story: flood damage, buckling, ink that had run in pale brown fingers across the pages. But on three more charts she found the same black thread—thin, branching, and utterly dry, as if it had come after the water had receded. She labelled each one, recording the location of the thread as a notation of damage, but her jaw was tight.

The silence became too exact then. The wind outside dropped. The water stopped lapping against the legs of her boots. The entire room held its breath.

Clara wound her watch again. Two half-turns. The clicking sound was a small defiance.

She straightened the label on "Obs-001", then the stack of charts she had set aside. She adjusted the corner of a folder that had curled upward. The gestures were automatic, the archivist's compulsion to align what had been disarranged.

She moved deeper into the observatory, toward the curved wall that still held its original windows. The glass was fogged with condensation, and behind it, the bog stretched into a twilight that seemed neither day nor night. Somewhere out there, Eleanor had walked. Eleanor had last been seen at the observatory, on the night the flood began. Clara had not found a single record of that night—no logbook entries, no personal notes, nothing but hours of unrecorded darkness.

She pressed her palm to the windowpane.

The fog cleared under her glove, and she saw the black branch.

It was on the inside of the glass, traced against the moisture like a vein in an eyelid. The pattern was identical to the threads on the star charts: a main line that curved upward, smaller offshoots, and at the ends, tiny rounded nodes. She wiped the surface, but the branch remained. It was not on the surface; it was "in" the glass, or under it, or between the layers of condensation. She could not tell where the stain ended and the glass began.

Beneath the window, a shelf held a row of glass plate negatives. She lifted one to the light—the emulsion was ruined, the silver halides bloomed into patches of grey, but across the centre of the plate, the black thread ran in a diagonal that matched the angle of the branch on the window.

Clara set the plate down carefully. "Mildew," she told herself. "Mineral staining from the bog mist. Flood rot that found a dark crevice and colonised it."

She climbed the spiral stair to the upper gallery, where a small reading room had been built into the cupola. The floor there was dry, the furniture draped in white sheets like a room of ghosts. A single stack of ledgers sat on the table, bound in red cloth, their spines intact. She opened the top one and found rainfall records—daily measurements, neatly kept, the ink faded but unsoiled.

On the last page, an entry for the night Eleanor disappeared: "Heavy rainfall. Barometer dropping. River rising. All personnel evacuated by 9 p.m."

And below it, in Eleanor's handwriting: "Except C."

Clara's throat tightened. "Except Clara." Eleanor had stayed behind to wait for her.

She closed the ledger. The label on the cover read "Rainfall Ledger, 1910–1912". She could not destroy it. She could not discard it. Even with the water stains, even with the word "Except" looking like a scar, she would preserve it until every word was transcribed and the truth of that night could be extracted from the rot.

She wound her watch again. Three half-turns. The motion was steadier now.

She returned to the ground floor, waded back to the shelf where she had left the stack of star charts. The water around her boots had grown still again. One of the charts had slipped from the pile and fallen into the shallow water. She retrieved it—"Obs-011", a map of the southern sky, the paper curling at the edges.

She peeled it open carefully. The black thread was there, running from the bottom margin straight to the centre, where the star Vega was marked.

But it was not a line. It was a word. Or the beginning of one.

She turned the chart fully, and the water dripped from its corners. The thread divided into smaller branches that formed shapes. Letters. The first letter was "E". The second was "L".

Clara's breath caught.

The next chart was in her hands before she thought to stop. She peeled it open, spreading it across a tilted shelf. The black thread had traced a name across the surface of the sky map, not written in ink but "grown" across the paper, each letter a network of fine black strands.

"Eleanor Vance."

The thread stopped at the surname. The final "e" in the sequence tapered into a single dot of black at the edge of the chart, as though the stain had travelled exactly as far as it needed and then ceased.

Clara stood in the stagnant water and stared at her sister's name, formed from a contamination that should have been random, washed in, meaningless. The room offered no sound. No wind. No ripple.

She did not know yet what it meant.

She only knew she would not destroy it. Even if the paper was ruined, even if the thread was wrong, even if she had to catalogue a thousand more charts and find the same name in all of them—she would preserve every one.

The branch on the window throbbed in the corner of her vision.

The water around her boots was perfectly still.

And at the bottom of the page, exactly at the "e" in "Vance", the black thread glistened as though it were still growing.
