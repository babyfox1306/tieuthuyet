# Chapter 7: The Negative Register

"Take my hand, then," Clara said to the roots, and the black veins tightened over her knuckles instead.

She had spoken aloud. A mistake. The air in the observatory did not absorb sound—it swallowed it, pressed it into the water stains on the walls, left the room thicker and heavier than before. The roots did not care for her voice. They cared for the page she held against her chest, the one she had refused to burn, the one whose charred edge they had climbed to reach her skin.

The sunbeam had shifted. It touched the table's corner now, nowhere near her hand. She could not bring the page to the full light without committing to its destruction, and she had already proved she could not do that. The refusal lived deeper than logic.

But a polished surface could redirect the light.

She twisted carefully, keeping the page pressed to her ribs, and dug the brass watch from her vest pocket. The chain sang against her buttons. The case was warm from her body. She angled the crystal face toward the fading beam and caught a narrow flash of white, threw it across the table, across her fingers.

The roots contracted.

Not an audible reaction. A physical one. They "crisped", pulled taut, drew away from the reflected glare like they had never climbed. The black lines shrank from her knuckles, retreating into the paper's charred edge, and left her skin clean.

She exhaled. She did not lower the watch.

She tested the reflection across the table's surface. The roots recoiled wherever the light touched. Useful. Document.

"Reflected UV induces contraction of vascular contamination without carbonization of the substrate."

She filed the observation and set the watch face-up, ready.

The C009 notation from her earlier mapping sat unfinished in her log. A lower shelf. Deliberately withheld from the main archive, buried beneath the main floor, accessible only through a service corridor that had partly flooded. She had marked it as a priority, and the roots had gathered around the notation on her map like they were reading over her shoulder.

She left the sunbeam.

The corridor stairs were iron, oxidized instantly where the waterline had touched them, the metal flecked with glittering points of rust that threw off pinprick amber light. She had to step carefully. The water was cold, stagnant, and smelled of peat and dissolved iron and something older she could not name.

The lower registry was a sunken room. The ceiling hung low, and the shelves had been built into the walls, their glass doors sealed with wax. A preservation attempt that had failed before it finished. The wax had softened in the humidity, the seals had broken, and the water had climbed.

She found the cabinet at the far end. A negative register. A ledger of records that had been removed or lost or never accessioned in the first place. It was a graveyard of catalogue entries.

She pulled the rubber gloves from her pocket and snapped them on.

The register was heavy with water. The pages stuck together. She separated them with a spatula, working by the weak light of her lamp.

"Vance." The section was near the back.

She found the page.

The expected entry was a rectangle of perfect, untouched white. Blank. No ink, no writing, no stamp, no stain. In a book where every other page was crawling with black branching, this void was preserved.

Clara's breath stopped.

The branching ink surrounded the void. It traced the shape of a human body. Not a full form—an outline. A negative impression carved in black. The roots mapped the span of a hand, the curve of a throat, the cage of ribs. The space inside was empty, pure, untouched.

"C010: Negative register, Vance entry. The record is absent, but the shape of the original cataloguer is preserved in the surrounding contamination. The ink cannot fill the space where a person should be recorded."

She could not stop seeing it. The blankness was the shape of Eleanor. The space her handwriting would have occupied. The outline of a hand that had once turned pages.

Her fingers trembled against the glass. She peeled back the next page.

Beneath the glass, under the register plate, a cluster of peat-black filaments lay pressed between the pane and a scrap of leather binding. They were fine, dense, matted. She lifted the glass with her spatula and angled the lamp.

The filaments were vascular. They branched and re-branched, forming capillary beds. But they were also fibrous, the same long-chain cellulose structure that paper left behind when it dissolved. Tissue and fiber. Body and record. Fused at the microscopic level, indistinguishable.

"C011: Filaments recovered from beneath register plate. Structure consistent with organic vascular tissue and archival fiber pulp. No discernible boundary between organism and document. Origin unknown."

She recorded both observations. She wrote them slowly, knowing that the act of writing was an act of preservation, and that preservation fed the roots. But she could not stop. She was an archivist. A record-keeper. The act of destruction was not a tool in her vocabulary. It was a failure state she refused to activate. Even now. Especially now.

"V-1889-03," she whispered. Eleanor's remembered catalogue sequence.

The room offered no answer. No echo. No whisper. No footstep in the corridor. No scent of perfume or hair oil or rotting wood.

No one was watching.

No one was coming.

She was alone with the shape of her sister preserved in negative ink, and she had no witness to tell her to stop.

On the shelf beside the register, a bone-dry moth lay pinned inside a cracked glass plate. The moth was decades dead. It had never touched the water. The glass was intact except for a single hairline fracture running through its center. And inside that crack, black capillaries had threaded outward, printing themselves on the moth's transparent wings in perfect parallel to the insect's own veins. The ink had found the specimen. Preservation had become its vector.

Clara lifted the moth plate and turned it in the light. The black lines matched the wing veins exactly. The ink was not just feeding on records. It was rewriting them. Imposing structure on whatever had been protected, whether paper or insect or skin.

She looked back at the negative register. The blank void where Eleanor's entry should have been.

She knew sleeving it would feed the network. The knowledge was no longer theoretical. It was proven. Every preserved page she had handled had thickened the roots. The Mylar she carried existed to protect documents, and every document she protected became fuel.

She slid the negative register into the protective sleeve anyway.

The blank space at the center—the negative shape of Eleanor's record—darkened. Not with ink. With a shadow that rose from the paper itself, spreading from the edges inward. The black branches pressed forward from the outline of the ribs, the throat, the hand.

And every glass plate in the lower cabinet tilted.

A small, formal pivot on their wooden mounts. The plates were not stacked. They were racked vertically, waiting for cataloguing. And now they angled themselves, as if by a single hand, toward the sealed wall at the back of the room. Toward the buried route she had not yet opened.

The black branches on every plate pointed the same way.

Clara's watch ticked in her pocket. The sound was loud in the silence.

She looked at the wall the plates were pointing toward. The wall where C009 had marked a chamber that did not appear on the observatory's maps.

The roots wanted her to follow.

And she had just fed them the shape of her sister.
