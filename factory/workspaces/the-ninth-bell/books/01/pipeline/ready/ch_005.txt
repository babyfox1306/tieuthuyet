Chapter 5: The Memory They Gave Me

Mara's voice on the phone was steady in the way people are steady when they have rehearsed a sentence for eighteen years.

"You're calling about Elin," she said. Not a question.

I was sitting on the floor of the tower's ground-level storage room, my back against a crate of damp newspapers that had been printed the year Elin died. The headlines were about council tax. Nothing about bells or cliffs or sisters. Gabriel had gone to check the causeway — tide was turning and he wanted to make sure we weren't cut off. The phone signal was one bar at best, but it held.

"I'm calling about the recordings," I said. "About what happened after."

A pause. Not the pause of someone searching for words. The pause of someone deciding how many to give.

"Clara, I can't—"

"You can. You will. I found a tape of my own voice in the tower. Someone built a confession out of pieces of my life — kitchen scraps, interview fragments, voicemails — and stitched it into a statement I have no memory of making."

The line crackled. Wind off the channel, or interference from the old wiring threaded through the walls.

"What kind of statement?" Mara asked.

"That I knew Elin would fall."

Silence. Longer than the first. When Mara spoke again, the rehearsed steadiness had split along one seam, and something rawer bled through.

"Your father did that to you."

The words landed flat. Not because they were shocking. Because someone had finally said them outside my own skull, where they'd been rattling around for years like loose change in a tin.

"Explain," I said. My voice was the one I used on the stand. The clipped, clean register that didn't invite preamble.

"After Elin died, you were —" Mara stopped. I heard her breathe. Once. Controlled. "You were twenty, Clara. You weren't eating. You weren't sleeping. You were sitting in rooms and not speaking. And Thomas moved you into the study within a week."

"The study."

"His study. The one with the reel-to-reel and the good Shure microphone he used for his guest lectures at the polytechnic. He told me he was helping you process. Those were his words. I remember them because I was sixteen and I thought 'processing' sounded clinical enough to be real."

I pressed my thumb against the bridge of my nose. The storage room smelled of brine and mildew and old ink.

"He recorded me," I said.

"Repeatedly. For months. I'd come over after school and you'd be in that study with the door closed, and when he finally let me in there were tapes everywhere — cassettes, Clara, the old kind, stacked in rows with labels in his handwriting. Date and your name. Always your name."

"What did he ask you?"

"The same questions. Every time. 'What did you see that morning.' 'What did you hear before the tower.' 'Where were you when Elin went upstairs.' And 'what did you tell her.'"

"And I answered."

"You answered the first few times. You gave different versions. Some were confused. Some contradicted each other. You said you were in the kitchen. You said you were in the hallway. You said you didn't remember at all. And Thomas would stop you each time and say—" Mara's voice shifted, dropping into an imitation that was barely an imitation at all. The cadence was precise. The patience was weaponized. "'That's not what happened, Clara. Think carefully. Try again.'"

"And eventually I gave him what he wanted."

"I don't know what he wanted. I only know the answers started sounding identical. Same words. Same pauses. Same breathing pattern between sentences. Like you'd memorized a script."

I was writing on the back of a damp broadsheet. My hand was steady. The letters were small and angular, the way I took notes during an audio restoration — labeling each observation before I could doubt it.

"What did I say, Mara. What was the script."

"You said you argued with her. That morning, before she went to the tower. You said you told Elin to be careful and she laughed at you. You said you watched her start up the stairs and you didn't follow."

"I left her there."

The phrase left my mouth before I could examine it. It sat on my tongue like something I'd swallowed years ago and forgotten was there. A stone in the gut. A splinter in the soft palate.

"Yes," Mara said. "That's the one. 'I left her there.' You said it at the end of every session. Every single one. Like a prayer. Like if you said it enough times it would become true."

I wrote it down on the newsprint. I LEFT HER THERE. Four words. I stared at them and tried to find a memory attached. There was nothing. no image of stairs. no sound of Elin's laugh. no texture of the banister under my hand. Just the words, clean and dry, stripped of sensory context the way a voice recording is stripped when you isolate the frequency band and discard everything else.

"Mara. Do you remember us arguing that morning?"

"No."

"Were we even together that morning?"

"I don't know. I wasn't there. I came over after. After the ambulance. Thomas called me and asked me to come sit with you because you were inconsolable. But when I arrived, you were sitting at the kitchen table staring at the wall. You didn't cry once, Clara. Not once."

I let that sit.

"The tapes," I said. "You said he had boxes. How many?"

"I lost count. Fifty? More. He'd play them back for you sometimes — play what you'd said the day before and ask you to confirm it. 'Is this accurate, Clara? Is this what happened?' And you'd listen to your own voice saying things that didn't match what you'd said the day before, and you'd look confused, and then he'd ask you to confirm again, and you always did. By the end, you always did."

The signal wavered. One bar flickered to nothing, then back.

"One more thing," I said. "The recordings in this tower — the ones connected to the other deaths — they're all edited the same way. Surgical cuts. Stitched audio. Someone has been doing this for years."

Mara's breath hitched. "I know."

"You know?"

"Thomas was obsessed with that tower. His whole life. After your mother died, after Elin — it was always the tower. He'd go out in the middle of the night and come back smelling like salt and machine oil. I asked him once what he was doing and he said he was maintaining the bells."

"Maintaining."

"I was seventeen. I didn't push."

The signal died. Not a goodbye, not a click. Just zero bars and a frozen screen. I tried redialing. Nothing. The phone sat in my palm like a dead thing.

I gathered my notes and went upstairs.

Gabriel's workspace was empty. I moved to the table where I'd been mapping the confession waveform and pulled the banker's box toward me. The family file. Thomas's records, shipped from his house after the stroke, sealed in cardboard with his handwriting on the exterior in black marker. I'd opened it once when it arrived and shut it within three minutes. Now I opened it properly.

Cassettes. Dozens. Clear cases, each labeled in Thomas's block letters. Dates, names, occasions. "Christmas_1998." "Elin_Birthday." "Mara_Visit_August." My fingers moved through them with the precision of a cataloguer, building a timeline in my head, cross-referencing dates against the eighteen-year gap Mara had described.

Most were domestic. The kind of household archaeology I'd spent a decade digitizing and archiving in my cloud. But toward the bottom of the first box, the character of the collection changed.

Cassettes without cases. Plastic shells, stacked loose, with small white labels on the spine. The handwriting was Thomas's but altered — tighter, more compressed, each letter drawn with a deliberateness that spoke of obsession rather than organization.

Each label had a date. Beneath the date, one word.

"Clarification."

I pulled one free. The label read: "14 September. Clarification." Standard 60-minute cassette. I turned it over. On the reverse, in the same constricted script: "C.V. — session 12."

C.V. Clara Vale. Session twelve.

My fingers had begun to tremble. Not visibly — a subcutaneous vibration, like the double pulse buried beneath the threshold of hearing on the tower recording. Present but not yet measurable.

I found my portable player in the equipment bag. Slid the cassette in. The counter read 14:37 and stopped — frozen at a number that meant something to whoever had last held the rewind button.

I pressed play.

Static. The click of a microphone being positioned. Then Thomas's voice, younger than I remembered it, harder, carrying an edge of controlled impatience that I had spent two decades learning to forget.

"Clara. Let's go over it again. Tell me what you said to Elin that morning."

My voice answered. My voice but not mine — twenty years old, flattened, emptied of everything that makes a person sound like a person. "I left her there."

"No. From the beginning. What happened before that."

"I left her there."

"Before that, Clara. Walk me through it. What did you see. What did you hear. And what did you do."

Silence on the tape. A long, aching gap that the machine recorded with perfect fidelity. Then my voice, small and scraped hollow: "I left her there."

Thomas again. Patient. The kind of patience that is the opposite of patience. "That's right. You left her there. You could have followed her upstairs, but you didn't. You stayed downstairs. You left her there alone."

I stopped the tape.

The phrase was his. It had always been his. He had placed it in my mouth and I had swallowed it and carried it for eighteen years like a stone slipped into my coat pocket while I slept.

I listened to my own guilt and I heard his cadence. The rhythm of interrogation. The architecture of suggestion. My confession in the bell — "I knew she would fall" — carried the same flat affect, the same syntactic closure, the same punishing simplicity. Not the halting, contradictory voice of someone recalling trauma. The polished, repeatable delivery of someone who had been coached.

I wasn't remembering Elin's death. I was reciting Thomas's version of it.

I pulled out the next cassette. "21 September. Clarification. C.V. — session 13." The counter on this one read 22:15.

I pressed play, and my father's voice filled the chamber like something that had been sealed inside the bronze of the bells, waiting.
