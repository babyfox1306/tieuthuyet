# Chapter 3: The Memory of Touch

The lock was digital, but her thumb moved across the keypad before her brain caught up—a code she didn't know she remembered.

4962.

The numbers weren't a birthday or an address. They were pure muscle memory. The worn spots on the keys were familiar under her fingers. She'd typed this sequence a thousand times.

The door clicked open.

"How did you know the code?" Lucian's voice was low at her shoulder. She hadn't heard him move, but he was there, a wall of heat against her back.

"I didn't."

She pushed the door into the dark foyer. Her hand found the light switch without looking. Another piece of her body acting without permission. The overhead light flickered, hummed, and settled into a sterile glow.

The air was stale. Closed windows. Dry dust. But underneath it, a ghost of coffee. Her coffee. She remembered buying the beans but couldn't remember making the pot.

She stepped inside, and her left arm lifted.

Unbidden. A command from a part of her brain she couldn't access.

Her middle finger found the doorframe. Traced down. A faint groove in the black paint—a scratch dug into the wood at precise wrist height. The lacquer was freshly split. A measurement of her own body's reach, left behind like a marker for a ghost.

Her hands had done this. The scratch was deliberate. Exact.

She pressed her thumb into the groove. Hard. The splinter bit into her skin.

"What did you find?" Lucian asked. He hadn't crossed the threshold. Waiting.

"A riddle I left for myself." She ran her finger along the scratch mark. "I was here. After the memory loss. I came back to my own apartment and measured my arm reach on my own doorframe. Why would anyone do that?"

He didn't answer.

She dropped her hand. "Unless they wanted to know exactly how much space they had for a fight. How far they could extend without hitting the frame. Close-quarters combat calibration."

The words came out flat. Clinical. She didn't know where they came from.

Lucian stepped inside and closed the door behind him. "You are faster than I expected."

"Don't patronize me."

"I'm not. You were trained to read environments. You are reading this one."

She moved to the kitchen. A single mug in the sink. A half-full glass of water on the counter. An open file folder spread across the island, weighted down by a salt shaker.

Floorplans. The Vertigo Building.

Her handwriting in the margins. *Roof access east. Service elevator. Fire door lock override.*

She touched the edge of the paper. Her own loops and lines. She'd planned the rooftop meeting. Not as an investigator planning a takedown. As an operative planning a route.

"When did I become a weapon?"

"You always were one," Lucian said. "You just forgot how you were forged."

The words hit like a slap. She looked up at him. He stood perfectly still in her kitchen, watching her with those dark, unreadable eyes. A stranger who knew her better than she knew herself.

"Where were you?" she asked. "During the missing months. Were you with me?"

"I was nearby."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you get."

She wanted to push. To scream. But the investigator in her—the part that still worked—told her to keep moving. Gather data. Don't let emotion collapse the process.

She searched the apartment.

Systematic. Her hands knew where to look before her brain caught up.

The bathroom. Under the sink. She knelt and reached past the cleaning supplies to the curved pipe at the back. Her fingers found the edge of duct tape.

She peeled it free.

A folded piece of paper. Her own handwriting.

71422.

She rose and held it up. "What is this?"

Lucian didn't reach for it. "A number you didn't want Rook to find."

"A safe combination? An account number?"

"A fragment of the ledger."

The ledger. The evidence she'd hidden. The thing Rook wanted badly enough to kill for. She had left herself a breadcrumb. A key she couldn't use yet.

"Why five digits? It doesn't fit anything standard."

"You wouldn't make it easy for yourself to decode if someone else found it first."

She folded the paper and pressed it into her back pocket. The weight of it felt like a live wire against her skin.

She looked down at her left wrist.

The pale skin where she'd rubbed it raw on the rooftop. The nervous habit she thought she'd developed.

But it wasn't a habit.

She lifted her hand. Deliberately.

Index finger to the inside of her wrist. A slow, intentional rotation.

The movement was perfect. Flawless. Her muscles had done this a thousand times. She felt the familiar shift in her posture. Her shoulders unlocked. The noise in her head—the screaming panic, the grief, the fragmented terror—faded into a quiet hum.

Another circle.

A third.

On the final pass, her trigger finger pressed down. A tiny, precise compression. A dry-fire sequence.

The world snapped into sharp focus.

"It's not a tic," she said. Her voice was steady. "It's a sequence. A reset protocol. It primes me for a shot."

Lucian's eyes tracked to her wrist. "Yes."

"Who trained me?"

"You know who."

"Rook."

"He designed the conditioning."

"And you?" She met his gaze. "What did you do?"

He didn't answer. His silence was heavy. Loaded.

She started toward the study. The room where she kept her work files. The laptop. The personal documents. If she had hidden anything else, it would be in there.

Her hand reached for the doorknob.

A hand closed around her forearm.

Firm. Warm. His fingers wrapped completely around the narrow bone of her wrist. The pad of his thumb pressed into the inside of her arm.

The world tilted.

Not a memory. A sensation.

The weight of his hand. The same hand. In complete darkness. She was on her knees. Concrete hard and cold through her jeans. The chemical tang of gun oil and cordite.

His voice against her ear. Low. Close enough that she felt the heat of his breath. *"Breathe in. Hold. Squeeze. Don't flinch."*

The boom of suppressed rifle fire.

The click of a bolt cycling.

His hand squeezing her shoulder. A benediction. A signal she had passed.

She was back in the hallway.

Her breath was ragged. Her skin was on fire where his palm rested. The overhead light was too bright. She could smell the cordite in his clothes.

"You were there." The words came out strangled. "In the dark. You held my arm like this. You taught me how to pull the trigger."

His expression was carved stone. But his eyes held something raw. A wound that hadn't closed.

"You needed to pass a test," he said. "I made sure you could."

"The test for the contract."

He didn't confirm. He didn't deny. He just held her gaze.

His thumb moved. A tiny, deliberate arc on the inside of her wrist. A message meant for her alone.

She felt it everywhere. The air between them was thick. Electric.

She pulled her arm free. Hard.

He let go instantly.

The absence of his palm felt like a cold draft against her skin.

"What's in the study?" she asked.

"The truth you aren't ready to find."

"Try me."

"The drawer of the desk. The locked one." His voice dropped. "If you open it, Rook will know you've found it. He will come for you directly."

"He's already coming. He was on the rooftop."

"That was a message. This would be a declaration."

A vibration cut through the tension.

His phone.

He pulled it from his inner pocket. Looked at the screen. His jaw tightened.

"He's here."

"Rook?"

"At the front of the building."

Sloane crossed to the living room window. Pulled the sheer curtain back half an inch.

The street below was a canyon of orange sodium lights and concrete. A single figure stood at the corner of the fire hydrant. Cassian Rook. Even at this distance, she recognized the lean frame, the careful stillness. He was looking straight up at her window.

He knew she was watching.

He raised a hand. A small, almost invisible gesture. A signal.

*Look down.*

Her eyes tracked to the alley beside the building.

A shadow moved. A shape resolved into a man holding a rifle. The glass of the scope caught the distant light of a passing car—a momentary, predatory glint.

Rook hadn't come alone.

The shadow moved below the apartment window—Cassian Rook was there, and the glint of a rifle confirmed he was not alone.
