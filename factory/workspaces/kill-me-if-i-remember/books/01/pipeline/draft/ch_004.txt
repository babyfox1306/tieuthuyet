# Chapter 4: The First Bullet

The bullet hit the wall where her head had been a second before.

Plaster dust exploded into the stale air of the apartment, a fine white mist that coated her tongue like chalk. Sloane Mercer didn't scream. She didn't have the breath for it. Her body, reacting with a primal efficiency that her conscious mind couldn't match, had already dropped her to the floor. She scrambled backward, her heels skidding on the hardwood, just as a second window shattered inward.

The sound wasn't the dramatic crash of cheap cinema. It was a wet, heavy thud of tempered glass giving up the ghost, followed immediately by the rhythmic percussion of automatic fire chewing up the drywall.

"Move," Lucian Vey said. He didn't shout. He didn't panic. The command was delivered in the same flat tone a man might use to comment on the weather.

He was already moving, a dark blur against the shadows of the hallway. He hauled her up by the elbow, his grip iron-hard but careful enough not to bruise the bone. Sloane stumbled, her heart hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird. The contract—the damn paper with her own handwriting condemning her to death—was crumpled in her fist.

"They aren't waiting for sunrise," she gasped, the sarcasm in her voice brittle and sharp. It was her only defense. If she didn't mock it, she would start shaking, and she wasn't sure she'd ever stop.

"Sunrise is the deadline," Lucian said, shoving her toward the kitchen. "Harassment is a hobby."

Another burst of fire tore through the living room, stitching a line of holes across the sofa she had been sitting on not five minutes ago. The smell of cordite filled the small space, acrid and metallic, overlaying the scent of her own fear.

"Who is shooting at us?" Sloane demanded, ducking as a lamp exploded above her head.

"Rook's cleanup crew. Or a competitor who thinks they can claim the bounty."

He reached the window above the sink, the fire escape ladder rattling in the wind outside. With a vicious strike of his elbow, he shattered the remaining pane, clearing the frame. He didn't check for glass on the sill. He just vaulted over it, landing with catlike grace on the iron grating. He turned back, extending a hand.

Sloane hesitated for a fraction of a second. Behind her, the front door groaned under the impact of a battering ram. She looked at Lucian's hand—calloused, scarred, steady. This man had been hired to kill her. He had stood on a rooftop while her sister died. And yet, he was the only solid thing in a world that had just turned into a war zone.

She grabbed his hand and jumped.

The cold night air slapped her face as she landed beside him. The iron grate vibrated under their feet. Above them, the city skyline was a jagged circuit board of lights and shadows, oblivious to the violence playing out in the small apartment.

"This way," Lucian said, pulling her down the stairs.

They descended the metal ladders in a clatter of noise that surely gave them away, but there was no choice. Sloane’s coat flapped around her legs, and she clutched the contract tighter, as if the paper could somehow shield her from the lead filling the air behind them.

They reached the bottom of the fire escape and dropped into the alleyway. It was damp, smelling of wet concrete and garbage. A stray cat hissed at them from atop a dumpster and scrambled away.

"Get in," Lucian ordered, pointing to a sleek, black sedan parked at the curb, its engine idling quietly. It had been obscured by the shadows, a ghost car waiting for its passengers.

Sloane wrenched the door open and threw herself into the passenger seat. Lucian slid into the driver's side, the movement fluid and efficient. He slammed the car into gear just as the alley window above them shattered, raining glass down on the hood like diamonds.

Lucian didn't speak. He navigated the streets with a terrifying precision, taking corners hard enough to throw Sloane against the door, threading the needle through late-night traffic that seemed nonexistent in this part of the city. He wasn't driving randomly; he was following a map in his head, a route that avoided the main arteries and the cameras that watched them.

Sloane watched him, her breathing slowly returning to normal. He looked calm. Too calm. His profile was carved from granite, his eyes scanning the mirrors, the street, the shadows.

"You knew," she said, her voice low. "You knew they were coming."

"I calculated the probability."

"And you still let us sit there until they started shooting?"

"We needed time." He glanced at her, a flick of his eyes in the darkness. "Time for you to see the apartment. Time for you to realize your life is a stage set."

Sloane looked down at her hands. They were trembling. She shoved them between her knees to stop the shaking. The apartment had been hers, but it hadn't felt like hers. The books were right, the furniture was familiar, but the soul of the place was missing. And now, pieces of it were being blown to bits by men she didn't know.

She dug into her pocket, her fingers brushing against something stiff and textured. She pulled it out, frowning in the dim glow of the dashboard.

It was a photograph.

She didn't remember picking it up. She must have grabbed it in the chaos, a reflex action, clutching at something tangible as her reality crumbled.

She held it up to the faint light. It showed a woman—her, unmistakably, though the version of her in the picture looked softer, younger, unburdened by the weight of federal investigations and missing months. She was laughing, her head thrown back, her hair loose around her shoulders.

And she wasn't alone.

An arm was draped around her shoulders, possessive but familiar. A man stood next to her, his face turned toward her, half-profile. He was handsome, with sharp features and dark hair, but Sloane couldn't place him. She stared at his face, searching her memory banks for a name, a context, a connection. There was nothing. Just a blank static hum.

He was a stranger.

"Why do I have this?" she whispered, more to herself than to Lucian.

Lucian spared the photo a glance. His jaw tightened, a muscle jumping beneath the skin. His knuckles whitened on the steering wheel.

"It was in the lining of your coat," he said. "Hidden."

Sloane turned the photo over. There was no writing on the back, just the smooth, glossy sheen of the paper. But she noticed the corner. The top right corner was bent at a precise, sharp angle, as if the image had been folded over and over again by someone who couldn't afford to expose the full print. Someone hiding it.

"I don't know him," she said, her voice rising with frustration. "I should know him. Look at how I'm looking at him."

Lucian swerved the car suddenly, taking a sharp right into an abandoned industrial park. The tires squealed on the asphalt. The motion threw Sloane against the center console.

"Hey!" she snapped, bracing herself.

"We have a tail," Lucian said tersely.

He killed the headlights, plunging them into near-total darkness. The car slowed, crawling forward on momentum alone. Sloane strained her eyes, looking out the rear windshield. Two sets of headlights swung around the corner behind them, cutting through the gloom like predatory eyes.

"Can we lose them?"

"Yes."

He slammed on the brakes. The car skidded to a halt. "Get out."

"What?"

"Out. Now. Run to the drainage tunnel ahead. I'll draw them off."

"You're crazy," Sloane said, unbuckling her seatbelt anyway. "You're the one with the gun. I'm just the investigator with a crumpled piece of paper."

"Go, Sloane."

He reached across her, his arm brushing against her chest as he opened her door. The contact was electric, a sudden spark of heat that shocked her more than the bullets. He lingered for a fraction of a second, his face inches from hers. She could smell the soap on his skin, the metallic tang of the gun oil, and something else underneath—something warm and woodsy that tugged at a memory she couldn't quite catch.

"Not without you," she said, surprising herself. It was a stupid, noble sentiment she had no business feeling.

"I'm right behind you."

But as she scrambled out of the car, the pursuing vehicles screeched to a halt fifty yards back. Doors opened. Silhouettes emerged. Before Lucian could get out, a muzzle flash erupted from the lead car.

The shot was impossibly loud in the confined space of the industrial park.

The rear window of the sedan shattered, safety glass spraying inward like glitter. Sloane ducked, covering her head. She looked back. Lucian was cursing, gripping his upper arm. Blood seeped between his fingers, dark and slick against the black leather of his jacket.

"Lucian!"

He didn't hesitate. He kicked his door open and fired three shots in rapid succession. The boom of the pistol was deafening. The attackers ducked for cover behind their open doors.

"Run!" he roared, vaulting over the hood of the car. He was slower now, favoring his left side.

Sloane didn't argue. She ran toward the dark mouth of the drainage tunnel he had pointed out. It loomed ahead, a concrete archway swallowing the night. She heard footsteps pounding behind her—Lucian.
