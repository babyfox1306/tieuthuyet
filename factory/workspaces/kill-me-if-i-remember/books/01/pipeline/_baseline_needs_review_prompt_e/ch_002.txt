# Chapter 2: Evidence That Should Not Exist

"Unload it," Sloane Mercer said, holding the signed contract over the open incinerator chute built into the rooftop floor. "Or your contract burns before I do."

The masked man didn't move his rifle away from her. He kept the barrel pointed low, angled at her knees, but his hands moved with an economy she recognized. Tactical training. Military or contractor background. He adjusted the red dot to the side, released the magazine with his thumb, and caught it mid-air. The action was practiced, fluid, almost lazy.

He held the magazine up in his palm. "You want to count the rounds?"

Sloane didn't answer. She watched his hands. He cleared the chamber with a sharp pull of the charging handle. A single round ejected, spinning in the moonlight, and he caught that too. He held both the magazine and the loose round out for her inspection.

"Satisfied?"

"Put them on the ground. Kick them to me."

He did. The magazine scraped across the gravel-topped surface. The loose round rolled and stopped against her boot.

She lowered the contract but didn't release it. She crouched slowly, keeping her center of gravity low, and picked up the ammunition. 5.56x45mm. Standard NATO. No markings to suggest it was a specialty round. She palmed the loose casing and matched its weight and rim thickness against the one she'd pocketed from beside Mara.

They were consistent.

The casing didn't confirm he'd fired the shot. It confirmed that his weapon class was capable of the wound pattern she'd seen in her sister's chest. Nothing more. A match of exclusion, not identification.

She straightened. Her knees ached from the cold rooftop.

"You've been here the whole time."

"Yes."

"You didn't fire that rifle."

"I didn't say that."

She met his eyes through the mask. The lenses were dark, non-reflective. Ballistic grade. Expensive gear for a man standing over a dead woman he was supposed to have shot.

"If you shot her," Sloane said slowly, "you used a different weapon. This one is cold. The barrel has no residual heat. You swapped firearms or you're the responding party, not the shooter."

The man said nothing.

Sloane pulled her attention back to the contract in her left hand. The paper was high-bond, the kind used in legal document printers. She held it to the fading moonlight. No watermark. No hidden embossing. Just words written in her hand.

She studied the letters.

Her hand.

The t was crossed low, almost level with the baseline. The y had a shallow loop, not a deep hook. The numbers—dates, times, a sum—were punctuated with a sharp underline beneath the final digit. A habit she'd developed during her first forensic accounting job. A way to prevent fraud in her own logs. She'd never seen another person do it.

The contract had that underline. Every single numeric entry.

The pressure breaks were even. No tremor. No hesitation in the strokes. This was not written under duress. This was written by a hand that knew exactly what it was doing.

She did not remember writing it.

She did not remember selecting the paper, the pen, the table where her wrist had pressed flat enough to leave a friction ridge she couldn't see but knew existed. She did not remember the bullet hole that had already existed when she'd woken up—a small, precise perforation above the sunrise clause.

Sloane touched the edge of that hole with her fingertip.

Blue security fibers. Embedded in the paper stock during manufacturing. The contract had been printed on forensic evidence paper. The same stock the department used for evidence registers, chain-of-custody forms, and sealed affidavits.

That paper was not available on the open market. It was controlled inventory.

Controlled inventory that she, as a forensic accountant contracted with the state, had signed access to.

The evidence was mounting that she had done this. That she had created this scene. That she had written the instructions for her own execution at sunrise.

She looked at the masked man. "What is your name?"

"That's not in the contract scope."

"I'm asking anyway."

"And I'm refusing." His voice was flat. Tired. As if he'd been expecting this conversation and had nothing but the same answers he'd given an hour ago.

She turned the paper over. No writing on the back. No stamped date. But the edges were crisp, not creased. It had been stored flat. Probably in a folder or envelope.

"You accepted a contract," she said.

"Yes."

"From me."

"From the woman who signed this, yes."

Sloane ignored the distinction. "What must happen before the sunrise order becomes binding?"

He didn't answer immediately. He shifted his weight, adjusting his stance. The rifle hung from a single-point sling now, muzzle toward the ground. Relaxed, but ready.

"The contract is active," he said. "The conditions are your domain, not mine."

"Don't speak in riddles."

"I'm not. I'm telling you that you wrote the terms. Everything I need to do is on that page. Everything you need to know is what you already know."

"I don't remember writing it."

"I know."

The admission caught her. She stared at him, waiting for more. He didn't offer it.

"You know I don't remember," she repeated.

"You said that when you woke up. Before you looked at the body. You said 'I don't remember coming here.' Then you saw her and you stopped speaking."

The letter formation was natural. The pressure variation matched her baseline. The private notation habit—the underline on every number—was present. This was not a forgery.

"It's impossible for me to have written this and not remember it."

The masked man said nothing.

Sloane looked past him, toward the shape on the concrete. Mara. Sister. Identity she couldn't fully claim right now.

She forced herself to walk closer.

The adhesive residue on Mara's wrists was visible from three feet. Medical tape. The kind used to secure IV lines. Not restraints. Not zip ties. The puncture in the chest was small, precise. Blood had pooled outward in a pattern that suggested the heart had stopped beating quickly but not instantly. The fabric around the wound was burned from close proximity. Stippling on the skin beneath.

Sloane knelt. She didn't touch her sister's body. She couldn't bring herself to do that yet.

The clothing damage was clean. A single tear corresponding to the entry point. No defensive wounds on the hands or forearms.

No signs of a struggle.

It was a clean forensic narrative. Too clean. As if the scene had been staged for someone who needed to believe the story without asking questions.

"She's not cold yet," Sloane said.

The masked man didn't respond.

"The blood hasn't fully darkened. If you shot her, you shot her within the last hour."

"I arrived at 12:47."

"Before me."

"Yes."

"And you found her like this?"

"I found her like this."

Sloane stood. Her knee cracked in the cold air. She looked at her hands. The contract was still in her left hand. Her right hand was tracing a small circle against the inside of her left wrist.

She told herself it was a nervous gesture. A grounding habit.

Then the masked man spoke.

"Drop the paper to the ground."

The instruction was soft. Almost gentle.

And Sloane's right hand completed the circle, tightened into a fist, and began to rise.

She caught it. Stopped it at the level of her stomach.

The motion was wrong. It wasn't a panic response. It was the first motion of a firing sequence. The circle. The fist. The draw.

Her body knew what to do before her mind decided.

She looked at the masked man.

He was watching her hand.

He knew.

A beam of light swept across the far end of the rooftop. A flashlight. Someone on the neighboring fire escape or the building across the gap.

The masked man moved.

He was behind her in two long steps, his body between hers and the light source. His hand found her shoulder and pulled her sideways, ducking her behind the bulk of a rooftop HVAC unit.

She didn't resist.

Her body didn't resist.

Her fingers found the fabric of his tactical jacket and held on. She felt the slow rhythm of his breathing beneath the layers of gear. Felt the solid weight of his frame against her back.

Her mind screamed that this man was a threat. A contractor holding a weapon over her sister's body.

Her hands disagreed. They held his jacket like she'd done it before. Like her body had learned this trust without telling her brain.

"Stay down," he said.

The light passed. Two seconds of darkness. Then a single sharp crack split the air.

A suppressed round.

The bullet punched through the contract she was still holding. It tore a hole through the paragraph immediately above the sunrise clause, sending shredded paper and blue security fibers spinning into the moonlight.

The masked man tackled her sideways. His body weight drove her behind the concrete base of the AC unit.

Air left her lungs. The contract ripped in half.

Someone on that fire escape wasn't here to watch.

Someone was here to destroy the evidence.

Mara's body lay exposed on the rooftop. And Sloane Mercer had just lost the only page that explained why she was supposed to die at sunrise.
