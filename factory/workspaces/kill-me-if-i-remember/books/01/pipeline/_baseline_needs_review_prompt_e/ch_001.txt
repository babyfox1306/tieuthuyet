# Chapter 1: The Contract Beside the Body

"Do not touch your sister," the masked man said, shifting the rifle barrel just wide of Sloane Mercer's reaching hand. "You paid me to shoot her."

The words landed like a physical blow. Sloane's knees were on the rooftop gravel, the cold already soaked through her jeans, her head thick with whatever had stolen the last three months of her life. She remembered dark kitchen tile, a phone ringing, the steam of coffee she never drank. Then nothing. A clean cut in the film of her existence.

The woman lay three feet away. Dark hair fanned across the concrete. Familiar boots. A grey shirt Sloane had borrowed last spring and never returned. Mara.

"Please," Sloane said. The word tasted wrong in her mouth. She didn't beg. She had never begged. But her sister was bleeding onto the roof of a building Sloane didn't recognize, and the man with the rifle was still holding it.

He didn't lower the weapon. He shifted it instead, a subtle rotation that kept her exactly in the kill zone while acknowledging she wasn't the current target.

Sloane let her eyes sweep the rooftop. Standard commercial building, maybe twelve stories. HVAC units, a satellite dish, a maintenance shed. A puddle near a clogged drain. And beside it, a wad of black chewing gum stuck to the grate. Something metallic glinted in the center of it.

A casing. Frangible round. She recognized the copper-polymer base, the distinctive machining marks. The petals had peeled back in the signature deformation pattern of a controlled fragmenting round. But it was wrong. The spread was too narrow. The petals hadn't fully separated.

That round hit something that matched its intended velocity. Close range. Controlled environment.

"Stand up," the man said. "Slow."

Sloane rose. Her body obeyed before her mind caught up, muscle memory moving her through a sequence she didn't remember learning. Balanced stance. Hands visible. Weight on the balls of her feet.

Professional.

She didn't know who she was anymore.

"Look at the paper," he said.

She looked down. A folded document lay on the gravel between them. She hadn't seen him throw it. She must have missed it in the first disoriented seconds.

"Pick it up."

She bent, keeping her eyes on his trigger finger the whole time. The paper was warm. Body heat. He had been carrying it close.

She unfolded it.

The handwriting hit her first. Her own. The sharp angles, the aggressive crosshatches on her sevens, the slight leftward lean she developed during law school exam weeks. The margins were tight, filled with abbreviations in her personal forensic notation system. PDAs. Job codes. Timestamps.

She knew this paper. She knew the feel of the pen she had used. She could almost feel the desk beneath her wrist, the weight of the decision pressing down.

But she didn't remember writing it.

The body was Mara Mercer. Female, twenty-eight. Target acquisition confirmed. Payment structure itemized. The client's name at the top was Sloane Mercer.

Her own name.

She had paid a contract killer to shoot her sister.

"Why?" The word came out flat.

The masked man didn't answer. He watched her, the rifle still between them, but his posture shifted. A fraction of a degree. He was waiting for something. Recognition. Memory. A crack in her composure.

She gave him nothing.

Sloane read the contract again. The language was clinical. Professional. The notations in the margins were her own. She recognized the little star she drew beside verified information. The tiny crosshatch for confidential. Every habit she had built over a decade of working with evidence, applied to a murder contract.

Beneath the careful handwriting, a line of text was scored so deep it had nearly torn the paper. Sloane ran her thumb over the indentation. She had pressed hard when she wrote it. Desperate. Certain.

*Mara survives initial contact.*

The phrase was wrong. Initial contact. Not the shooting. Not the fall. Initial contact.

Sloane looked at her sister's body. The blood had pooled beneath her shoulder, dark and viscous. But the fabric was torn at a strange angle. A clean puncture, yes. But there was another mark. A thin crescent of what looked like medical adhesive, hidden beneath the dark stain of the wound. And a pinpoint puncture, precise and medical, just above the collarbone.

She couldn't get closer. The rifle was still aimed at her chest.

"What happens at sunrise?" she asked.

The man's jaw tightened. The only part of his face she could see, but it told her everything. She had written that too.

"Finish reading," he said.

She dropped her eyes back to the page. Her handwriting grew tighter toward the bottom, smaller, as if she had been running out of space and time. The final clause was written in block letters, so hard the pen had nearly punched through the paper.

CLIENT TERMINATION AT SUNRISE.

And beneath it, her signature. Clear. Unforced. Her name in her own hand.

She stared at the words until they blurred. Client termination. Her termination. She had ordered her own death.

The fear hit her like a wall of ice water. Her chest seized. Her breath stopped. Her hand moved without permission, thumb finding the inside of her left wrist, tracing a small circle against the skin. A grounding habit, something she did when the world tilted too far.

But her fingers didn't stop.

They continued the motion, curling inward. The circle became the beginning of a grip. Her palm rotated. Her fingers found the space where a weapon would sit.

She caught herself. Forced her hand flat against the paper.

The man was watching. She saw it in the stillness of his body, the way he held his breath. He knew that gesture. He knew what it meant.

"Who are you?" she asked.

He didn't answer.

She moved.

It was instinct, pure and disconnected from thought. A pivot, a drop of her center of gravity, a grab for the rifle barrel. Her body knew how to disarm a man with a long gun. Her body knew how to close distance, how to use momentum, how to make his own weapon into a liability.

He countered her before she completed the motion. A sidestep, a rotation of the rifle, and suddenly his hand was on her wrist, not hard, his thumb pressed precisely into the soft space between the bones of her forearm. A restraint hold. Controlled. Gentle. He wasn't trying to hurt her.

He was trying not to.

"Sloane."

His voice dropped when he said it. The mask muffled his face, but his eyes didn't lie. They were dark, tired, and they knew her. Not as evidence. Not as a target. They knew her in the way that left bruises on the soul.

He said her name like it cost him something.

She looked at his eyes. Searched them for the answer to a question she didn't know how to ask. There was something there. A history she couldn't access, locked behind the blank wall in her head.

She felt nothing.

He saw it. She watched the recognition hit him. The hope that had been holding him together cracked, just a hairline fracture, and she saw the hurt bleeding through.

He let her go.

The rifle dropped back to low ready. He stepped away, creating distance she hadn't asked for.

"Read the contract again," he said. His voice was flat now. Professional. The intimacy was gone, sealed back behind whatever discipline he carried.

Sloane looked down at the paper in her hands. The wind stirred the edges. The sky was still dark, but the stars were fading. Sunrise was coming.

She traced the block letters with her fingertip.

CLIENT TERMINATION AT SUNRISE.

Her signature. Her handwriting. Her death warrant.

And somewhere in the blank space of her memory, she had known exactly what she was doing.
