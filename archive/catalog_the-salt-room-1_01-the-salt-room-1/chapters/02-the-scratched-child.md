The tape counter clicked from 007 to 008 in Nora's hand, though the machine behind her was still unplugged. The Salt Room door had opened one inch. Nothing breathed on the other side.

Nora did not move.

The rational part of her mind, the petty, overeducated part with invoices and archival gloves and a professional hatred of bad labeling practices, offered three possible explanations and immediately hated all of them. Mechanical slip. Residual tension in the counter wheel. Her own thumb brushing the reset stem.

Except her thumb was nowhere near the stem.

The plastic case bit into her palm. The little white numerals sat in their black window, smug as a witness who knew it could not be cross-examined.

"Fine," Nora said to the empty corridor. Her voice came out level, which was generous of it. "Evidence first."

She looked at the door.

It had been shut. She knew it had been shut because she had tried the knob, heard the lock complain, seen the line of salt lying unbroken along the threshold. Now the door stood open just enough to show a vertical strip of darkness and cold.

Not a person. Not a child. Not even the cheap theatrical mercy of a shadow where a shadow should not be.

Just an inch.

That made it worse.

Nora took one step closer, then stopped before her shoe crossed the salt. The crystals were not scattered randomly. They formed a thin ridge, as if someone had poured them with a careful hand and then dragged a card through the center to make a gap exactly the width of the door.

Quiet wrongness. Always start there.

She crouched, keeping her knees away from the floorboards, and angled her phone light down. Salt granules glittered against dark wood. No footprints. No smears. No tiny heel marks, no adult tread, no convenient evidence that a living person had slipped past her and staged a little heritage-hotel nightmare for fun.

The cold from the gap pressed against her face.

It smelled of salt, old cotton, and something mineral, like wet stone sealed inside a drawer.

Nora listened.

Under ordinary circumstances, a closed room had a tone. Walls held their own breath. Old pipes ticked. Wood complained. Air moved in and out of cracks with the patience of rot.

This room had nothing.

Not silence. Silence had texture. This was removal.

She counted the room tone under her breath. "One. Two. Three. Four."

Nothing answered. Good. Wonderful. A room with manners.

The counter in her hand clicked again.

009.

Nora's throat tightened so fast it felt mechanical.

She looked down at the deck. No power. No lit meters. No moving reels. Its cord lay slack on the runner rug behind her, the plug teeth dull in the phone light.

"Absolutely not," she said.

Her own words vanished into the corridor as if the walls had taken them and filed them away under irrelevant.

She stood, slowly. The urge to push the Salt Room door open had a clean shape in her mind, and because it had a clean shape, she distrusted it. Curiosity was not bravery. Curiosity was how people ignored obvious structural hazards and died under decorative plaster.

Also, there had been a child's voice on the tape saying her name after the machine stopped.

That detail deserved a category larger than decorative plaster.

Nora backed away from the Salt Room without turning her back on it. The inch-wide gap stayed an inch. The salt stayed salt. No hand curled around the edge. No eye appeared in the dark. The building did not indulge her with proof she could reject.

At the bend in the corridor, the air warmed by a few degrees, and the normal hotel sounds returned in thin layers: a distant drip, the soft tick of cooling pipes, the sea dragging itself against the rocks below the fogged windows.

She took the deck back to the parlor and set it on Agnes Vance's dining table.

Agnes had used the room as an office, or a shrine to paperwork pretending not to be a shrine. Ledger books sat in stacks of three. Twine-bound envelopes lined the sideboard. A brass desk lamp leaned over the table like it had spent years interrogating receipts.

Nora plugged nothing in.

She placed the recorder in the center of the table and put both hands flat on either side of it. Her fingers were cold. That was useful. Cold was measurable. Fear was just bad data with excellent marketing.

The counter sat at 009.

She took a picture of it.

Then another.

Then she removed the cassette, labeled it in her temporary log as A.V. TAPE 12, VOICE ANOMALY, COUNTER 007-009 AFTER POWER REMOVED, and put it in a separate evidence bag. The word evidence looked ridiculous in her handwriting, too legal and too hopeful, but cataloging was a form of aggression. Nora had built a career from refusing to let damaged sound stay vague.

She pressed her thumb against the ridge of the cassette case.

The urge came then, humiliating in its timing.

She wanted to lean into someone warm. Not a grand rescue. Not a hand over hers, not a body stepping between her and the corridor, not some scripted comfort with a steady voice telling her there was a reasonable explanation. Just warmth. A living shoulder. Human weight beside her at the table while the old hotel tried to edit itself around her.

Her shoulder tipped a fraction.

It found only the cold table edge.

The tape case sat under her thumb, hard and ridged and indifferent.

Of course. There was no partner in this story. No second pulse in the room. Nora had made a life with locked doors, careful departures, and relationships held at the exact distance where no one could ask what her family never said out loud. The absence did not ache prettily. It made the hotel larger.

"Congratulations," she told the empty chair across from her. "You are now the emotionally available option."

The chair, showing taste, said nothing.

Nora pulled the first ledger toward her.

The cover was navy cloth, its corners softened by handling. Agnes's handwriting filled the first pages in narrow columns: linen counts, soap orders, repair notes, room numbers, years. The early entries were practical enough to be comforting.

Room 3: two pillowcases missing.

South hall: runner fraying, replace before Easter guests.

Kitchen: preserve jars, twelve.

Then the pattern changed.

Not immediately. Not theatrically. There were no bloody underlines, no deranged marginalia, no helpful confession tucked into an envelope marked OPEN WHEN HAUNTED.

Instead, Nora found gaps.

A household inventory from a summer twenty-two years ago listed children's blankets, two blue enamel cups, one cracked hairbrush with yellow handle, three pairs of small sandals. The number three had been written hard, the downstroke cutting into the paper. Beside it, someone had later scraped at the page with a blade or a key.

Not crossed out. Scraped.

Fibers lifted in a pale wound where ink had been removed. The paper had thinned so much Nora could see the shadow of the next page through it.

She bent closer.

The scraped place sat beside a line that now read: small sandals, pairs.

No number.

"That's not accounting," Nora murmured.

It was editing.

She turned more pages. Agnes's records did not decay into madness. That would have been easier. They remained orderly, almost insultingly calm. Soap. Thread. Sugar. Sheets. Children.

No, not children.

Child.

By the end of that summer's section, plural nouns had been corrected. Sometimes by ink. Sometimes by abrasion. Sometimes by a careful strip of paper pasted over a line and rubbed smooth, as if the household itself had developed a skin condition.

Nora found a loose measuring card tucked into the ledger, a strip torn from the back of a cereal box, with pencil marks along one edge.

N.V. 46 inches.

Below it, another mark.

The initials beside the second mark had been scraped away so violently that the cardboard bowed. A ghost of graphite remained, not enough to read. The height was nearly the same as Nora's childhood mark. Maybe a fraction taller. Maybe only the angle of the pencil.

The back of Nora's neck prickled.

She did not know what the missing initials were. She did not let herself invent them. Inventing was how families got away with murder without ever having to commit one.

She photographed the card front and back.

Then she searched the sideboard.

The first drawer held silver napkin rings wrapped in tissue. The second held hotel stationery, brittle rubber bands, and guest keys with tarnished tags. The third stuck halfway, swollen with damp. Nora pulled harder. Something inside shifted, then gave with a dry clatter.

Photographs spilled forward.

Not albums. Loose prints. Envelopes. A handful of Polaroids gone sepia at the edges. Family history made portable enough to lose on purpose.

Nora carried the stack to the table.

She expected Agnes. She expected hotel guests, women in beach hats, stern relatives in black dresses, a version of Ruth with the closed face Nora knew from childhood dinners and phone calls that never lasted more than twelve minutes.

She got all of that.

Agnes stood on the hotel steps in one photograph, younger and unsmiling, one hand on a railing slick with sea air. Ruth appeared in another, hair pinned back, mouth set in a line that had not softened with age. There were dining room shots, porch shots, children blurred in motion at the edge of frames.

Children.

Nora slowed.

There she was.

Young Nora stood on the back terrace in a red summer dress, knees knobby, hair cut blunt at her chin. She held a tin pail in one hand. Her mouth was open, not quite smiling, as if someone had called her name at the wrong second.

The sight hit with the dull force of bad restoration work, when a buried frequency suddenly rose through hiss and became a human breath.

Nora had seen childhood photographs of herself before, but not many. Ruth disliked old pictures. That was the phrase she used, disliked, as if photographs were a food she had politely refused.

In this one, young Nora was not alone.

A second child stood directly beside her.

Or had.

The face had been scratched out.

Nora's hand went still.

The damage was not age. Not mold, not silvering, not heat exposure. A hard point had been driven into the glossy surface again and again, gouging out the child's face in short violent strokes. The rest of the photograph remained intact: terrace rail, gray sea beyond, young Nora's red dress, the child's shoulder pressed close to hers.

Too close to be accidental.

The scratched child wore a pale shirt with a ruffled collar. One sleeve brushed Nora's bare arm. Their shoulders slanted at the same angle, both bodies turned toward the unseen photographer. The erased child stood inside Nora's space, not at the edge of the group, not half-cropped or incidental.

Placed.

Nora swallowed.

No name rose in her memory.

Nothing helpful cracked open. No golden childhood scene. No remembered laugh. No sudden certainty. Her mind offered only the photograph and the rude absence carved into it.

She turned the print over.

On the back, Agnes had written a date in blue ink and nothing else. No names. No place. No tidy grandmother caption. The blankness felt less like forgetfulness than discipline.

A strip of old transparent tape clung to one corner on the back of the photograph, yellowed and brittle. Under its lifted edge, trapped like tiny evidence in amber, was a single grain of dry salt and one short black thread.

Nora stared at them longer than she stared at the scratched face.

Salt did not belong on the back of a stored photograph unless the photograph had been somewhere salt could settle. A pocket. A room. A hand that had touched the threshold.

The black thread was no longer than an eyelash, but thicker. Cotton, maybe. From clothing. From a blanket. From the old black binding tape used on hotel linens.

"Agnes," Nora said quietly, and hated that it came out sounding like an accusation.

The hotel did not answer.

The deck on the table gave one soft click.

Nora's eyes snapped to it.

The counter still read 009.

No movement. No sound after that. Just plastic settling, probably. Tension cooling. A machine being a machine with terrible timing.

She reached for her phone.

Her first instinct was not to call Ruth. That, at least, was consistent with twenty-nine years of self-preservation. Ruth Vance treated questions like stains: dab quickly, never rub, deny the mark was there if anyone else noticed.

But Ruth was alive. Ruth had been young in the years Agnes's records changed. Ruth had known the hotel before Nora's memory became a torn receipt.

Nora photographed the scratched picture, the scraped inventory, the height card. Then she opened Ruth's contact.

The phone rang long enough for Nora to regret calling and not long enough to hang up with dignity.

"Nora." Ruth's voice came through clean and cool. Not sleepy. Not surprised. "Is something wrong?"

That was Ruth's gift. She could make concern sound like a locked medicine cabinet.

"I'm at the hotel," Nora said.

"I assumed that."

"You could have started with hello."

"I could have. Are you injured?"

"No."

"Then what is it?"

Nora looked at the photograph on the table. Young Nora stared toward a person she could not remember. Beside her, the other child's face had been obliterated by a human hand.

"Agnes kept records," Nora said. "Detailed ones."

A small pause. "Agnes kept everything."

"Not everything. Some things were removed."

"Nora."

There it was. The warning note. Her name flattened into a doorstop.

Nora pressed her thumb to the cassette case until the ridge hurt. "There are household inventories. Someone scraped entries off the paper."

"Old paper does strange things by the sea."

"Paper doesn't selectively scrape nouns into singular form."

Silence.

Nora listened to the line. Ruth's room tone was faint and domestic: a refrigerator hum, a far-off television too low to understand. The sound of a life continuing in a house where no salt lay across any threshold.

"Why are you going through Agnes's private things?" Ruth asked.

"Because Agnes left me a hotel full of them."

"She left you a property. The lawyer said it needed sorting. That doesn't mean you should disturb every drawer."

"Disturb is an interesting verb."

"It is an accurate one."

"No. It's a defensive one."

Ruth exhaled through her nose. Nora could picture her standing in her kitchen, one hand on the counter, eyes closed in that practiced way she had when Nora became inconveniently specific.

"Some families keep poor records," Ruth said. "Some people correct mistakes."

"With a blade?"

"Nora, listen to me."

"I am. Occupational hazard."

"Do not make this into something dramatic."

The laugh that escaped Nora was small and sharp. "A child has been scratched out of a family photograph."

The line went dead quiet.

Not disconnected. Not empty. Nora could still hear the faint electrical bed of the call. Ruth was there.

Nora looked back at the picture. "One child's face is gone. The rest of the image is intact. I'm standing beside them."

Ruth said nothing.

"Close beside them," Nora added. "Shoulder to shoulder."

"Nora."

This time her name sounded different. Less command. More impact.

Nora's skin tightened.

"Who was the child?" she asked.

Ruth's breathing changed.

It was almost nothing, a break in rhythm, but Nora had trained herself to hear edits. The half-second before a splice. The breath someone took before lying or before deciding not to.

"Old photographs get damaged," Ruth said.

"That is the worst answer you've ever given me, and you've had range."

"Put it away."

"No."

"Put it away, Nora."

"Who scratched out the face?"

"I don't know."

The speed of it was wrong. Too immediate. Too polished. A statement worn smooth from previous handling.

Nora stood, chair legs scraping the floor. "You knew there were photographs here."

"Agnes had boxes of photographs. Everyone has boxes."

"Not everyone vandalizes children out of them."

"Stop saying it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you know what you're talking about."

Nora's grip tightened around the phone. "Then correct me."

Ruth did not.

The room seemed to contract around the table: the ledger, the measuring card, the cassette bag, the photograph with its small trapped grain of salt. Each object sat under the lamp with obscene patience.

From somewhere down the corridor, not near enough to be a voice and not distinct enough to be a sound, came the idea of a click.

Nora turned her head.

Nothing followed.

Ruth said, "Where are you in the hotel?"

"The parlor."

"Have you opened the back room?"

Nora went very still.

She had not mentioned a back room.

"What back room?" Nora asked.

Ruth's answer came too carefully. "Agnes had storage rooms."

"That's not what you asked."

"Nora, the hotel is old. It is not safe to wander through it at night."

"It's afternoon."

"Then don't wander through it at all."

Nora looked toward the hall. The Salt Room waited beyond the bend with its inch of darkness and its neat threshold line. Her scalp prickled as if cold fingers had combed through her hair, except no one was there, and she refused to give the hotel hands.

"You know about the room at the end of the back corridor," Nora said.

"I know Agnes kept too much. I know you have always had a talent for cutting yourself on things better left dull."

"That's almost maternal."

"Don't."

"Then answer the question."

Ruth's voice lowered. "Leave Agnes's papers alone. Box up the linens. Call the estate service. Sell the building if you want, keep it if you want, burn sage if that makes you feel modern. But do not start digging through old things because you heard a noise in an empty hotel."

Nora's pulse slowed.

She had not told Ruth about the tape.

"Who said anything about a noise?"

Another silence.

There. A splice. Not in audio, in blood.

Nora sat back down, slowly, her gaze on the damaged picture. Young Nora's printed face looked almost irritated, as if even at seven she had objected to being posed. The scratched child beside her had been reduced to gouges and white paper scars.

"I found Agnes's tapes," Nora said.

Ruth breathed once. Too softly.

Nora let the sentence sit. Let the line carry it. Let her mother decide how much fear to show.

"Throw them away," Ruth said.

Nora's mouth went dry.

"Interesting."

"They'll be moldy. Useless."

"Some play."

"Nora."

"One said my name."

The television hum on Ruth's end disappeared. Maybe muted. Maybe Ruth had moved into another room. When she spoke again, her voice had thinned.

"That is not funny."

"No one is laughing."

"Machines pick up things. Old recordings bleed through."

"Yes," Nora said. "Print-through. Magnetic transfer. Cross-talk. Lovely list. None of it explains why the counter advanced after the deck was unplugged."

Ruth's breathing became the loudest sound on the line.

Nora closed her eyes for one beat, sorting sound from fear. Hiss. Breath. Salt. Tape drag. Silence.

"Who was standing beside me?" Nora asked.

"Come home."

The words landed too fast, too naked.

Nora opened her eyes.

"I am home, technically. You and Agnes made that legally inconvenient."

"Do not be clever with me."

"Then stop being vague with me."

"I am trying to keep you from hurting yourself."

"By lying?"

"By asking you not to tear open things that were closed for a reason."

Nora leaned over the photograph. Under the tape, the salt grain caught the lamp glow, small and crystalline. A room key tag lay somewhere in the sideboard drawer behind her, dozens of them, ordinary and labeled.

The Salt Room door had opened without one.

"Closed things don't stay closed because you ask politely," Nora said. "They stay closed because someone locks them."

Ruth made a sound that might have been Nora's name if it had survived the trip from thought to mouth.

Nora slid a fingernail under the corner of the photograph's backing paper. Not enough to damage it. Just enough to see the edge of the frame behind it, because the print had come from an old wooden frame wrapped in tissue. She had set the frame aside when the photo loosened.

Something resisted.

Tape.

Not the yellowed strip on the photo. A newer strip, hidden between the frame's cardboard backing and the print, its edge barely visible.

Nora stilled.

"Nora?" Ruth said.

She lifted the frame with one hand, phone pinned between shoulder and ear. The backing bowed away. A brass key, flat and old, hung beneath a strip of clear tape gone cloudy with age.

A paper tag was folded behind it.

Nora did not pull it free yet.

Her heart had become a metronome with bad intentions.

Ruth's voice sharpened. "What are you doing?"

Nora stared at the hidden key.

"Someone scratched a child out of Agnes's photograph," Nora said.

Ruth stopped breathing for one full second, then whispered, "Do not sleep in that room tonight," before the line went dead and Nora saw the salt room key taped to the back of the damaged frame.
