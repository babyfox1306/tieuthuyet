She poured the tea herself, hands trembling only slightly, and smiled at me like we were old friends. “You must be exhausted,” she said. “I know how hard it is to start over.”

I stood in the doorway of her sitting room, my fingers still cold from the hallway. The room was smaller than I’d expected—cramped, almost claustrophobic, with heavy curtains that blocked out most of the afternoon light. A single lamp glowed on a side table, casting long shadows across the floor. The walls were papered in a faded floral pattern that might have been pretty once, decades ago. Now it just looked tired. Like everything else in this house.

“Thank you,” I said, and took the seat across from her.

She was nothing like I’d imagined. From Marcus’s description—a fragile woman, a condition—I’d pictured someone broken. Bedridden. Hollow-eyed and drooling. But she was composed, almost elegant, in a pale blue cardigan that hung loose on her thin frame. Her hair was dark and pulled back from her face, and her eyes were the color of old pennies. They watched me with a sharpness that didn’t match her trembling hands.

“Marcus told me you’d be coming,” she said, pushing a cup toward me. “I asked him to send you up when you arrived. But he likes to do things his own way.”

“He interviewed me first.”

“Of course he did.” She laughed, a soft, breathless sound. “He likes to assess people. Decide if they’re safe. He’s been doing that a lot lately.”

I wrapped my hands around the teacup, letting the warmth seep into my fingers. The tea was strong, slightly bitter. I took a sip anyway.

“He said you’ve been unwell,” I said carefully.

“Unwell.” She repeated the word like it was a foreign language. “That’s one way to put it. I have good days and bad days. Today is a good day. I woke up knowing my own name, which is more than I can say for some mornings.”

She said it lightly, but I caught the edge underneath. The fear that lived just beneath the surface.

“That must be difficult,” I said.

“It is what it is.” She shrugged, a gesture that seemed rehearsed, practiced for visitors. “You learn to adapt. You learn to keep things close that matter. Names. Faces. The shape of your own hands.”

She held up her hands, splaying her fingers. Her nails were clean but uneven, bitten down to the quick.

“I’m sorry,” she said suddenly. “I haven’t even asked your name. Marcus told me, but I want to hear it from you.”

“Anna,” I said.

“Anna.” She smiled, and something flickered in her eyes. Something I couldn’t read. “Anna what?”

I hesitated. Just a fraction of a second, but she caught it. Her smile widened.

“I saw your application,” she said. “In Marcus’s study. You only wrote ‘Anna.’ No last name.”

“I prefer to keep things simple.”

“Of course.” She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to a whisper. “But I know your full name, Anna. I saw it on the form.”

My blood went cold.

I stared at her, searching her face for any sign of a joke. But she was serious. Completely serious. And that was impossible, because I had never written my full name on that application. I had written only Anna. Just Anna. The way I’d written it for two years, ever since I’d left that name behind.

“I don’t have a last name,” I said, keeping my voice steady.

“Everyone has a last name, Anna.” She said it like a lullaby. “Even people who try to forget them.”

The room felt smaller suddenly. The shadows seemed to press in from all sides. I wanted to stand up, to walk out, to count my forty-seven steps back to the rental car and drive away and never look back. But I didn’t. I stayed. Because I needed to know what she knew.

“I don’t understand,” I said.

“You will.” She sat back, picking up her own teacup. Her hands were steadier now, as if the conversation had calmed her. “Tea helps. It always helps. My mother used to say that a cup of tea could fix anything except a broken heart.”

“Your mother?”

“She died when I was young. Very young. I barely remember her face.” She touched her collarbone, and I noticed for the first time that she was wearing a silver locket. Small, oval, with a delicate chain. “This was hers. I wear it every day. It’s the only thing I have left of her.”

She unclasped it and held it out to me.

“I want you to have it.”

I blinked. “What?”

“Take it.” She pressed it into my palm. The metal was warm from her skin. “I have no use for it anymore. And you look like you could use something to hold onto.”

I looked down at the locket in my hand. It was beautiful, in a simple way. The silver was tarnished in places, worn smooth by years of handling. I turned it over and saw something that made my breath catch.

A scratch. On the inside. Tiny, barely visible, but unmistakable. As if someone had tried to pry it open with a knife.

“What happened here?” I asked.

She tilted her head, studying me. “I don’t remember. It’s been there as long as I can remember. Maybe my mother tried to open it once. Maybe she lost the key.”

“It doesn’t have a keyhole.”

“No,” she said softly. “It doesn’t.”

I wanted to ask more. I wanted to ask why she was giving it to me, a stranger, a woman she’d met ten minutes ago. But before I could form the words, she stood up.

“I’m tired,” she said. “The good days never last long. But I wanted to meet you. To see your face.”

“Why?”

“Because I know things, Anna. Things I’m not supposed to know. And I think you do too.” She walked to the door and paused, her hand on the frame. “The east wing hallway has a locked door. Did Marcus tell you about it?”

“He said it was off-limits.”

“It’s the old nursery.” She smiled, but there was nothing warm about it. “That’s what we tell people. But the truth is, I don’t know what’s in there. I’ve never been inside. Marcus keeps the key on his person at all times.”

She stepped into the hallway, and I followed, the locket still warm in my hand.

“Be careful,” she said, without turning around. “This house has a way of keeping secrets. And so do you.”

She disappeared down the hall, her footsteps barely audible on the thick carpet.

I stood there for a long moment, the locket clutched in my palm. The scratch on the inside seemed to pulse against my skin, a tiny wound that wouldn’t heal.

I walked back to my room, my mind racing. The wife knew my full name. How? I had never written it. I had never told anyone. Not in two years.

I closed the door behind me and locked it. Then I pulled the job application from my bag.

I had written only “Anna.” No last name. No middle initial. Nothing.

But she had called me by my full name. A name I hadn’t used in years. A name I had buried along with everything else.

I sat on the edge of the bed, the locket in one hand, the application in the other.

Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the window. The garden was dark now, the dead trees casting long shadows across the brown grass.

I looked down at the locket again. The scratch on the inside caught the light.

Someone had tried to open it. Someone had wanted to see what was inside.

I wondered if they ever found out.

I slipped the locket into my pocket, next to the photograph of Clara. The one who had left suddenly. The one who had written: Don’t trust the quiet.

I closed my eyes and counted.

Forty-seven steps from the front door to the car. That was the number. That was the constant.

But tonight, nothing felt constant.

Tonight, I felt like I was standing on the edge of something I couldn’t name.

I opened my eyes and looked at the application one more time.

Anna. Just Anna.

But she had said my full name.

And that was impossible.
