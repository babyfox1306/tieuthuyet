I counted forty-seven steps from the rental car to the front door. I always count. It’s how I know when I’m being watched.

Forty-seven. That number meant something once. Three years, four months, and eleven days of counting the same cracked ceiling tiles in my cell. Forty-seven tiles long, thirty-two wide. You learn things in prison. How to read a room without moving your eyes. How to smile when you want to scream. How to count.

The iron gate groaned when I pushed it open. Estate, they called it in the ad. That was generous. It was a house—massive, cold, the kind of place that swallowed light before it could reach the windows. The stone was dark, almost wet-looking, even though the sky was clear. No flowers. No welcome mat. Just a brass knocker shaped like a lion’s head, its mouth frozen open mid-roar.

I lifted it and let it fall.

The sound echoed inside like a gunshot.

The door opened before I could knock again. A man stood there—tall, silver at the temples, wearing a sweater that probably cost more than my last apartment. He had the kind of face that didn’t give anything away. Clinical. Controlled. His eyes swept over me once, cataloging, filing.

“You’re early,” he said. Not accusatory. Just factual.

“Traffic was light.” I kept my voice neutral. Warm but not eager. That was the balance I’d practiced in the motel mirror this morning. Trustworthy enough to hire, forgettable enough to disappear into the background.

He stepped aside. “Come in.”

I crossed the threshold and started counting again without thinking. Thirteen tiles to the foyer. Fourteen to the staircase. The air was cold and still, like a room that hadn’t been aired out in years. The furniture was expensive—deep wood, cream upholstery, everything matching everything else. It looked like a showroom. No books on the coffee table, no dishes in the sink. Nothing that said someone actually lived here.

“I’m Marcus,” he said, leading me into a study off the main hall. “You spoke to my assistant on the phone.”

“Anna,” I said.

The name slid off my tongue like it belonged to me. It had for two years now. Before that, it was something else. Before that, nothing. You pick up names the way other people pick up receipts—useful for a moment, then disposable.

He gestured to a chair across from his desk. I sat. He didn’t.

“My wife has a condition,” he said, folding his arms. “She’s fragile. Physically and mentally. She requires stability. Routine. The last housekeeper we had was with us for six years, and even she found the arrangement… challenging.”

“What happened to her?”

His jaw tightened. A micro-movement, barely visible. “She left suddenly.”

Three words. No explanation. No follow-up.

I filed that away.

“What would my duties be?” I asked.

“Cleaning. Cooking simple meals. Laundry. The house runs itself, mostly, but my wife needs someone present during the day when I’m at work. She doesn’t like to be alone.” He paused. “There are rules.”

“I’m good with rules.”

“Are you.” It wasn’t a question. He picked up a folded piece of paper from his desk and handed it to me. “Read it. Out loud, please. I want to make sure you understand.”

I unfolded it. Neat type, bullet points. I read:

“No visitors. No personal calls on the house line. No entering the east wing under any circumstances. No questions about the locked room on the second floor. No loud noises after eight PM. Meals served at eight, noon, and six. My wife’s dietary restrictions are posted in the kitchen. If she asks for something not on the list, the answer is no.”

I looked up. “What’s in the east wing?”

“That’s not your concern.”

“I’m just asking so I know what to avoid.”

“You avoid it entirely.” His voice didn’t rise, but something in it sharpened. “The door stays locked. The key stays with me. If you see it open, you close it and walk away. Don’t look inside. Don’t listen at the door. Don’t ask my wife about it.”

“And the locked room on the second floor?”

“Same principle.”

I folded the paper and slid it into my pocket. “When do I start?”

He blinked. “Just like that? No negotiation on salary? No questions about the schedule?”

“You said stability and routine. That sounds fine to me.”

It wasn’t fine. It was perfect.

A locked room. A fragile wife. A husband who guarded his secrets like a dragon hoarding gold. This was exactly the kind of house I’d been looking for.

The robbery was supposed to be simple. In and out. A few pieces of jewelry, maybe a safe if I could crack it. A job to pay off the debt that followed me out of prison, the one that whispered in my ear every time I closed my eyes. I’d found this house through a connection—a former cellmate who knew someone who knew someone. Wealthy family. Isolated property. Minimal security.

I hadn’t expected the interview to feel like a confession.

“Good.” Marcus reached into his desk drawer and pulled out a set of keys. “Front door, kitchen, your room. The silver one opens the pantry. The brass one opens the gate out back. Don’t lose them.”

I took the keys. They were cold against my palm.

“I’ll show you to your quarters,” he said. “Follow me.”

We walked through the main hall toward the back of the house. The staircase curved upward, dark wood gleaming in the dim light. I counted as I walked. Twenty-three steps from the study to the kitchen. Eleven to the back hallway. The house was bigger than it looked from the outside, full of corners and shadows and doors that led to places I couldn’t see.

“The previous housekeeper’s room is still made up,” Marcus said, stopping in front of a plain wooden door. “She left in a hurry. Didn’t take all her things.”

“Can I look through them? See if there’s anything useful?”

“Throw them out if you want. Or keep them. I don’t care.”

He said it like he meant it. Like the woman who had lived in this room for six years had already been erased from his memory.

I opened the door. The room was small but clean—a bed, a dresser, a window that faced the back garden. The sheets were white and stiff, the kind you find in hotels that don’t expect guests to stay long. On the dresser, a hairbrush. A paperback book with the spine cracked. A photograph face-down in its frame.

I picked it up.

A woman with dark hair and a tired smile stood in front of the same iron gate I’d walked through thirty minutes ago. She looked ordinary. Forgettable. The kind of face that blended into crowds.

Clara, I guessed. The one who left suddenly.

I turned the photograph over. On the back, in neat handwriting: Clara. Don’t trust the quiet.

A chill ran down my spine.

“Your first day is tomorrow,” Marcus said from the doorway. “Eight AM sharp.”

“I’ll be there.”

He nodded once and walked away. His footsteps faded down the hall, and then the house was silent again.

I sat on the edge of the bed and counted to steady myself. Forty-seven steps from the car to the front door. That was the number. That was the constant. Everything else was noise.

I slipped the photograph into my pocket, next to the list of rules. Then I stood up and walked to the window.

The garden was dead. Brown grass, bare trees, a stone fountain that had long since stopped running. Beyond it, a wall. Beyond that, nothing. Just trees and sky and the road that led back to town.

This was my life now. Small rooms and locked doors and secrets I wasn’t supposed to ask about.

I’d been in worse places.

I heard a sound behind me—soft, almost nothing. The creak of a floorboard.

I turned.

She was standing at the top of the stairs, just visible through the open doorway. Pale. Thin. Her hair hung loose around her shoulders, dark against a white nightgown that made her look like a ghost. Her eyes were wide and dark and fixed on me with an intensity that made my breath catch.

She didn’t move. Didn’t speak. She just stared.

And then her lips parted.

She mouthed a single word.

Slowly. Deliberately. A word I couldn’t see, but I felt it land in my chest like a stone.

Anna.

A name I never gave her.
