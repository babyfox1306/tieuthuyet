The Croft folder stayed closed.

I rested my hand on the worn cardboard without opening it. I had already studied the fuel-line photograph, the marina receipt, and the fitting Victor recovered. Returning to them would not give me a new answer. It would only let grief disguise itself as strategy.

I opened the record of Victor's choices instead.

He had known enough to expose me for weeks. He chose private messages.

He could have killed me at the warehouse. He chose to keep talking until I moved.

He could have protected himself with visible police and lawyers. He chose rooms where he could watch my reaction.

He wanted an ending with an audience.

I called him from the blank phone.

"I have the replacement index," I said. "Names, cases, current routes. I will give it to you with a recorded confession."

"Now you sound tired."

"I am."

"Tomorrow. My house. Ten o'clock."

"No security."

"You are not in a position to set terms."

"Then you can keep guessing whether the index is real."

The pause told me he still wanted it.

"Ten o'clock," he repeated. "Front door."

The call ended.

He had invited me inside. There would be no stolen service code, no looped camera, no impossible entry to explain. I would arrive visibly enough for his doorbell to record and leave only if the recorder still belonged to him.

Sasha hated that part.

"If the footage survives, you are the last person seen with him."

"He'll delete it himself before the confession. He won't want a copy outside his control."

"Prediction."

"Yes."

"And if he doesn't?"

"I leave without acting."

The answer cost me nothing in the safe house. I would learn its price at his door.

Priya handed me a sealed medical pouch.

"One dose," she said. "If the seal breaks before you control the room, discard it. If you cannot use it without creating obvious trauma, walk away. If he collapses for any other reason, call emergency services."

"Understood."

"Say it."

"The compound is detectable. The cover depends on an external examination, his real cardiac history, and no contradictory scene. There is no perfect cause of death."

Priya nodded and released the pouch.

David arrived an hour later. It was the first time we had stood in a room without Lena or a lawyer.

He placed a small brass key on the table.

"Victor's front-door key," he said. "For David emergencies, he called it. Don't use it. If he refuses to let you in, the plan ends."

"Then why bring it?"

"So you know I could have opened the door myself and chose not to."

He took the key back.

"The family meeting is on his calendar for eleven tomorrow morning," he continued. "If he misses it, I go to the house. I identify him. I make the call. The crematory has his existing preauthorization on file from an estate plan. I still have to sign the disposition permit."

"You verified the preauthorization?"

"Sasha did. So did another lawyer who doesn't know your name."

That mattered. Our plan no longer depended on a single person assuring everyone else that a document existed.

"Lena knows?" I asked.

"She knows Victor used her and that I am helping stop him. She does not know how."

"Keep it that way."

David studied me. "If you fail, do not go near her."

"I won't."

"If you succeed, do not call this justice."

I looked at the closed Croft folder.

"I won't."

After he left, my phone received one message from Evelyn.

Victor updated his insurance yesterday. He prepared a silver drive at 4:12 PM and carried it home. Whatever he wants you to find, he chose it before you called.

The detail did not mean she had access to his house. It meant her network still watched the storage service Victor used. She had given me the exact limit of her knowledge.

I wrote back:

Why tell me?

Her answer came a minute later.

Because prepared gifts are never gifts.

I saved the exchange to the isolated laptop and did not respond.

Victor had prepared the drive before our final meeting. If it appeared after his death, there would be no question about when he placed it or whether he had time during a fight. The loose end would be deliberate.

Mara's photograph stood beside the laptop. In it, she was laughing at something outside the frame.

"You were the first stone," I told her. "You are not every stone after."

The distinction came late. It still mattered.

At nine the next evening, Sasha confirmed C-010 remained held. Each recipient confirmed no replacement package had arrived. Holt's sealed copy remained sealed.

At nine thirty, Priya's office rotation assigned her the overnight unattended-death queue. Chance had opened the right lane. If the assignment changed, she would call one word—red—and I would stay in my car.

At nine fifty-eight, I stood on Victor Rhodes's porch with an empty memory drive in my pocket and the sealed pouch flat beneath the dressing on my injured shoulder.

The doorbell camera turned toward me.

Then the front door opened.
