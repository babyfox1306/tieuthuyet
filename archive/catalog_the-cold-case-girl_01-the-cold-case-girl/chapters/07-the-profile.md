When Victor Rhodes needed a woman silenced, he did not touch the body. He made the system do the killing for him.

I traced the sentence across Sasha's legal pad with one finger, feeling the indentations my pen had left. The words were already committed to memory, but writing them down made them real. Made "him" real.

Sasha's office smelled like stale coffee and printer toner, the blinds pulled against the late afternoon sun. She sat across from me, arms crossed, watching me work through the case file she'd spread across her desk like an autopsy.

"You've been quiet for twelve minutes," she said. "That's either very good or very bad."

I looked up. "He left me a voicemail."

Sasha's face didn't change, but her shoulders tightened. "When?"

"Last night. After I found Mara's diary."

"And you're just telling me now?"

"I needed to process it before I shared it." I pulled out my phone, found the recording app, and hit play.

Victor Rhodes's voice filled the small office. Warm. Almost friendly. The kind of voice that belonged to someone who'd hold a door for you while planning how to make you disappear.

"Hello, Nadia. I've been looking forward to this."

I watched Sasha's jaw clench as the message continued. The words were careful — no threats, no demands. Just a man who knew exactly who I was and wanted me to know he knew.

When it ended, Sasha let out a breath. "That's not a warning. That's an invitation."

"It's a test." I set the phone down. "He wants to see how I respond. Whether I'll flinch."

"Will you?"

"No." I kept both hands on the legal pad. "But I'm accelerating the timeline. We need to move faster than he expects."

Sasha reached into her briefcase and pulled out a manila folder so thick it had to be held together with rubber bands. "Then you need to see the full file."

She slid it across the desk. The label read ELISE MARCHETTI in faded marker, but someone had crossed it out and written INACTIVE — UNSOLVED underneath.

"What's in it that wasn't in the public record?"

"Everything that matters." Sasha pulled off the rubber bands and spread the contents across the desk. "Start with the witness recantation."

I picked up a notarized statement from a woman named Helena Cruz. Her original testimony placed Victor Rhodes at Elise's apartment the night she was murdered. She'd seen him leave at 2:47 AM, seen Elise's silhouette in the window, seen the argument that ended with a slammed door.

The recanted version said she'd been mistaken. That it was too dark to be sure. That she'd been pressured by the police to name a suspect.

"What changed her mind?"

"Rhodes's attorney. Gerald Vance. He filed three lawsuits against her employer in the span of a week. Threatened to have her deported." Sasha's voice was flat, professional. "She has two kids. She caved."

I set the statement aside and picked up the next piece — a typed transcript of a voicemail, timestamped three days before Elise was murdered.

The words had been transcribed by an evidence clerk, but the original recording had been deleted from the system. The transcript read:

"He said if I told anyone, he'd—"

The cut-off was abrupt. Technical. But I knew the truth now. That was a fingerprint of control. Rhodes didn't shout. He didn't threaten. He simply made sure people understood the consequences of defiance.

"Who did she leave this for?"

"Her college roommate. A woman named Lina Torres." Sasha pointed to a note in the margin. "Lina gave the recording to police. The original vanished from evidence; this clerk's transcript is all that survived."

"Who had access?"

"Rhodes, the lead detective, two evidence clerks, and Gerald Vance after he filed a preservation request." Sasha pulled out a financial document — a bank statement from a shell company called Meridian Holdings. "Meridian paid Vance's investigations firm two hundred thousand dollars. The transaction cleared three weeks after police suspended active work on Elise's case. Victor's initials appear on the transfer authorization."

I studied the paper trail. The shell company. The investigators who pressured Helena's employer. Victor's initials.

"He funded the cleanup."

"Cleanly. Quietly. Through layers no one wanted to untangle." Sasha leaned back. "That's his signature. He doesn't leave fingerprints. He leaves bank statements and legal filings and intimidated witnesses."

I absorbed the information, letting it settle into the profile I was already building. Victor Rhodes was patient. He was methodical. He understood the system well enough to use it as a weapon.

But more than that — he understood people.

He knew Helena Cruz had children. He knew Lina Torres didn't have the resources to force an evidence inquiry. He knew which investigators would accept private money to turn pressure into paperwork.

And now he knew I was coming for him.

"He's not a monster," I said slowly. "Monsters are chaotic. They kill when they're angry, when they lose control. Rhodes kills when he's cornered."

Sasha's eyebrow arched. "Explain."

I pulled a fresh sheet of paper from her legal pad and started writing. "Elise was going to expose him. She had evidence — something she was planning to take to the press. Rhodes couldn't let that happen, so he eliminated the threat. But he didn't do it himself. He made sure someone else was there."

"How do you know?"

"Because the evidence at the recovery site never showed who killed her. No usable blood, no DNA, no surviving witness. Elise's body was found in Forest Park three days later, but someone had stripped the scene of anything that led back to Rhodes." I tapped the paper. "He uses leverage, not violence. He finds people who owe him or fear him, and he makes them do the dirty work."

Sasha was quiet for a long moment. "That's still a profile. You need a chain."

"Then show me the rest."

She pulled a second Meridian statement from the back of the folder. Fifty thousand dollars had gone to Patricia Dornan nine days before she gave Julian Croft his alibi. Patricia had later admitted that Julian was not with her the night Mara died. Her recantation had come too late to undo the acquittal.

"This proves Meridian bought the lie," I said. "It doesn't prove Rhodes controlled Meridian."

"This does." Sasha laid down a certified evidence-access log from the Croft prosecution. Victor Rhodes, then a chief deputy prosecutor, had opened the sealed witness folder twice in the week before Patricia testified. He had never been assigned to Mara's case. Beside the log was Elise's handwritten list of Meridian account numbers, recovered from the lining of a book she had left with Lina Torres.

One of the numbers matched the account that had paid Patricia.

The conclusion was no longer pattern recognition. It was a chain.

"He protected Mara's killer," I said.

Sasha looked at the documents before she looked at me. "He protected an asset. Croft moved money through the marina for Meridian. Rhodes bought the alibi, preserved the asset, and collected the debt later."

I touched Elise's list. "And Elise found the same account."

"Which gave Rhodes a reason to stop her."

I had spent three years believing Julian's acquittal was ordinary corruption: money pressing on weak points until the system bent. The pages in front of me gave the pressure a name.

For now, I had a profile to complete.

I spread the evidence across the desk and started building the framework. The pattern was clear if you knew where to look. Rhodes killed only when cornered. He used leverage, never direct violence. He was patient, methodical, and utterly convinced of his own superiority.

The last part was the most dangerous.

"He'll kill again," I said. "He can't help it. When someone threatens his control, he eliminates the threat. And right now, I'm the threat."

"So what's the plan?"

I didn't answer immediately. Instead, I picked up my phone and opened a new note. The words came easily, each one precise and final:

"The system failed Elise. I won't."

"Priority one is corroboration," I said. "One bank statement and one access log give us a chain. I need the original Meridian ledger before I call it proof in public."

"And if you find it?"

I met Sasha's eyes. "Then I finish what I started."

She didn't ask for details. She didn't want to know. That was the unspoken rule of our arrangement — she provided the legal cover, and I handled the rest.

But as I gathered the files and prepared to leave, I saw the worry in her face. I could not tell whether her silence meant doubt, judgment, or fear.

So was I.
