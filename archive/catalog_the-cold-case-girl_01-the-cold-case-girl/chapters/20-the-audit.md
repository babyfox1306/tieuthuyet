Wren's voice was flat, clinical. "His office is a fortress. Twenty-four-hour security, biometric locks, a server room with offline backups. The safe at his house is worse — motion sensors, a panic room, and a direct line to the police."

I listened, and I calculated. There was only one way in.

"You're sure about the offline backups?" I asked.

"The server room doesn't connect to any network. No cloud, no remote access. If he has a physical file on you, it's either in that office safe or the one at his house." A pause. "I could try to get eyes on the building's maintenance schedule. Maybe find a window."

"And the house?"

"You'd need a small army and a lot of luck. He's got cameras covering every approach, and the neighborhood has private patrols. The office is the weaker target."

I leaned back in my chair, the leather creaking under me. The office was the weaker target. That still made it a nightmare — just a slightly smaller one.

"Send me everything you have on the building. Floor plans, shift changes, camera blind spots."

"Already compiling." Wren's voice softened, just a fraction. "Nadia, this is next-level. If you get caught — "

"I won't."

"You don't know that. Rhodes isn't some random target. He's a professional. He's been waiting for someone to come at him."

I let the silence stretch. She was right, and that was the problem. Victor Rhodes had built his life around being untouchable. He'd studied predators, learned their patterns, and used that knowledge to destroy them. But he'd made one mistake: he'd let me get close enough to see him.

And now I knew his weakness.

"Prepare a burner phone," I said. "I need to make a call that can't be traced."

"Who?"

"Sasha. I need a legal distraction."

The line went quiet. I could almost hear Wren processing, her mind racing through the implications. She was good at that — seeing the full picture when others only saw fragments.

"Fake lawsuit?"

"Something that requires his presence. A deposition, a court appearance, anything that pulls him out of that building for a few hours."

"It'll have to be airtight. His legal team will tear it apart otherwise."

"That's why I'm calling Sasha."

I ended the call and dialed the number Wren had given me. Sasha picked up on the third ring, her voice sharp and professional.

"Nadia. This line is secure?"

"It's a burner. I need a favor."

"I'm listening."

I laid it out quickly — the building, the security, the need for a window. Sasha listened without interrupting, and when I finished, I heard her exhale slowly.

"You're asking me to file a fake lawsuit against one of the most prominent figures in true crime media. A man with resources, connections, and a team of lawyers who do nothing but watch for this kind of thing."

"I know."

"And you want it to hold up under scrutiny long enough to get you inside his office."

"Yes."

Another pause. I could hear the click of a keyboard in the background, Sasha already pulling up files and templates.

"I can do it," she said finally. "But the timeline is tight. I'll file a complaint of corporate espionage — claim he stole proprietary research from a client of mine. It's plausible enough, and it'll require a preliminary hearing. But his lawyers will see through it within a week, maybe less."

"A week is all I need."

"Then I'll have the paperwork ready by tomorrow afternoon. I'll need a name for the plaintiff — someone who won't be traced back to you."

I thought for a moment. "Use a shell company. One of the ones Wren set up for the network."

"Done." Her voice hardened. "Nadia, if this goes wrong — "

"It won't."

"You keep saying that. But you're not invincible, and neither is this network. One crack, and it all falls apart."

I didn't answer. She was right, and we both knew it.

The call ended, and I sat in the silence of my apartment. A fake lawsuit, a break-in, a file that could destroy everything I'd built. It was a gamble, but it was the only move I had.

Priya called.

I answered, and her voice came through in a rush, breathless and panicked.

"Nadia, I need you to listen to me. RH03 moved onsite. The state board sent an auditor to join the review."

I felt my spine stiffen. "When did you find out?"

"An hour ago. The onsite phase was unannounced — standard procedure, they said. But the auditor is known for being thorough. He's already pulled the last two years of case files."

My mind raced. Priya had altered records for me twice in the past year—Marcus Webb and Daniel Ortega. Small adjustments, subtle enough to avoid notice. But a thorough audit meant someone would be looking closely.

"Have they found anything?"

"Not yet. But they're going through everything. The signatures, the timestamps, the chain of custody logs. If they reach Marcus Webb — "

"They won't." I kept my voice steady, even as my pulse quickened. "You know what to do. Stay calm, cooperate, don't volunteer anything. If they ask about a specific case, you answer directly and move on."

"Easy for you to say. You're not the one sitting in an office with a state auditor going through your files."

"No, I'm the one who's going to lose everything if you crack."

The words came out sharper than I intended. I heard Priya's breath catch, and I softened my tone.

"Priya, listen to me. You're good at your job. Better than most. You've been doing this for years, and no one has ever questioned your work. This is just an audit. A routine check. They'll look, they'll find nothing, and they'll move on."

"You don't know that."

"No. But I know you. And I know you can handle this."

Silence. Then a shaky exhale.

"I'll call you if anything changes."

"Good. And Priya — don't alter anything else. Not now. Not until this is over."

"I understand."

She hung up, and I held the phone while the implications of the call settled.

The state phase of the same review had begun at the exact moment I was planning to move against Victor Rhodes.

Coincidence. Or pressure.

I thought about the timeline. RH03 had been opened internally weeks earlier, before anyone knew I was targeting Rhodes. The onsite timing still felt too perfect, but feeling was not proof.

I dialed Wren again.

"Tell me about the state auditor assigned to Priya's office."

A moment of typing. "Gregory Walsh. Been with the board for twelve years. Known for being meticulous, but he's never flagged a medical examiner's office before. His last five audits closed without findings."

"Has he ever worked with Rhodes?"

"Not that I can see. But Rhodes has connections everywhere. He could have made a call, mentioned the office, suggested a closer look."

"Can you find out?"

"I can try. But if he's smart, he won't leave a trail."

I closed my eyes, the pieces clicking together. Rhodes was methodical. He'd spent years studying killers, learning their patterns, understanding how they operated. He knew that the network was my support system, my safety net. If he could destabilize it, one thread at a time, he could isolate me.

And then he could destroy me.

"The onsite review," I said, more to myself than to Wren. "It doesn't feel like a coincidence."

"I don't know that for sure."

"No, but I do."

I hung up. The dark screen reflected a face that looked older than it had a month earlier.

I typed a message to Victor Rhodes: "The audit — your doing?"

My finger hovered over the send button. I imagined his response — that calm, measured voice, the tilt of his head as he lied to me. He'd deny it, of course. He'd say it was a routine check, nothing to worry about.

Fear supplied its own certainty: the review was his move, a way to remind me that he could reach anyone I cared about. I deleted the conclusion with the message. I needed evidence before I treated either as fact.

I closed my eyes and whispered into the empty room: "He's not just hunting me. He's dismantling me piece by piece."

The words hung in the air, heavy and sharp.

I needed to move faster. The fake lawsuit, the break-in, the file — everything had to happen before Rhodes tightened the screws any further. But Priya was in danger, and I couldn't protect her without drawing attention to myself.

I was trapped in a web of my own making, and the spider was closing in.

I opened my eyes and reached for my laptop. The plan was still viable, but it needed adjustments. A faster timeline. A backup for Priya. A contingency for every possible failure.

I started typing, my fingers moving with practiced precision. The work was the only thing that kept me grounded, the only thing that made sense in a world where every move I made could be my last.

Outside, the city hummed with late-night traffic and distant sirens. I knew Victor would make another move. I did not know when or through whom.

And I was planning mine.

The game was far from over.
