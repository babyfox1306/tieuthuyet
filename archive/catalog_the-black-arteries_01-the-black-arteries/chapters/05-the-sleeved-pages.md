"The sleeve is swelling," Clara said, and the page inside pressed black veins against the plastic like fingers against glass.

She said it aloud because the alternative was to let the observation sit in her throat like something swallowed wrong. The sleeve was archival grade, four mil Mylar, heat sealed on three sides with a seam she had checked twice before trusting. The page inside was a coastal survey from 1889, water damaged around the lower margin, ship tracks drawn in red ink that had faded to rust. She had cleaned it with a soft brush, flattened it between blotters for six hours, assigned it catalogue number 814, and sealed it against the humidity of this drowned building.

The black veins did not respect the seal.

They moved against the inside of the plastic, branching, thickening, pressing so hard against the material that the Mylar bulged at the center. The pressure was steady. Deliberate. She touched the surface with the back of her finger. The plastic was cold. The veins were warmer.

A loose scrap of shipping manifest lay on the table six inches from the sleeve. The scrap was torn, waterlogged, ink blurred to illegibility. A single black filament extended from the corner of the scrap, thin and hesitant, barely visible against the wood grain. The sleeved page was wrapped in a dense mat of vascular lines. Thicker. Darker. Fed.

She did not write "fed" in her notes.

She wrote "concentrated colonization behavior in preserved materials requiring further observation". That sentence was true and safe. It contained nothing that could be called a confession.

Clara pulled her pocket watch from her vest and wound it, counting the clicks. The ritual steadied her hands. She checked her fingertips for ink stains. Clean. She checked the seam of the sleeve again. Sealed. The black branches should have been contained.

They were not contained.

They were moving.

She had cleaned the page. Numbered it. Sleeved it. Guarded it. And the attention she had given it was a meal. The scrap on the untreated table was starving. The sleeved record was thriving.

She did not burn the sleeve.

She reached for a second sheet of Mylar, cut it to size, and sealed the entire assembly inside a double barrier. She added silica gel to the pocket. She labeled the new sleeve "risk isolate — do not destroy".

She was fortifying the enemy. She could feel the contradiction in her chest, a pressure like the veins against the plastic. But she could not destroy a record. She could not burn the page. The act of preservation was the purpose of her presence in this building, and if preservation was wrong, then the entire structure of her profession collapsed into water and ash.

She labeled the sleeve. She placed it in the far corner of the table, away from the untreated scraps, away from the water seeping across the floor.

The untreated scraps stayed thin.

The sleeved page began to press new filaments against the double layer.

Clara turned away and walked toward the main dome.

The dome was the largest open space in the observatory, a vault of iron and glass that had once held the great refractor telescope. The telescope was gone, removed decades ago, but the track remained, a brass circle embedded in the stone floor. Water covered the lower half of the track. The glass panels above were clouded with age and condensation, but the morning light pushed through, white and direct.

She needed baseline data. The star chart plates from the eastern cabinet had been stored in a lower drawer that had flooded. She had pulled the plates this morning, eight of them, submerged glass rectangles that had spent years in the dark. The first plate she examined showed the winter solstice of 1881, astronomic coordinates etched into the surface, ink lines faded but legible.

She set the plate on a slanted observation table and angled a hand mirror to catch the mid-morning beam.

The light hit the glass. The etched lines caught white, clean and old. But beneath the light, in the condensation that clung to the cold surface, black threads were forming. They did not follow the astronomic coordinates. They drew a different map. A vertical descent. A spiral that tightened as it fell, turning inward, dropping through layers of stone and water and peat-dark sediment.

The exact geometry she had sketched beneath the grate in the lower archive.

Clara's breath hitched. She disciplined it.

"The same structural pattern," she said.

The water in the room did not answer. The glass did not answer. The black branches thickened on the plate, tracing the route over and over, a spiral that never touched the original etched lines, never crossed the astronomic data. The route existed in the space between the coordinates, in the condensation, in the attention she was giving it.

She photographed the plate. Documented the location. Filed the observation under "unresolved environmental patterning — repeat structure observed in multiple surfaces".

The branches continued forming while she wrote.

She moved the plate out of the light. The branches slowed but did not stop. They continued tracing the descent, patient, unceasing, as if the route was not a response but a memory.

The Horology corridor was the oldest section of the observatory, built into the original 1840 foundations. The drawers were oak, swollen with moisture, their brass handles green with oxidation. Clara pulled the third drawer from the top, the one she had resisted opening since her arrival.

The drawer was labeled "Components — refractor accessories, 1929–1935".

The accession slip inside was dry. Perfectly dry. Impossible in this flooded building, where every surface wept and the air itself was saturated, but the paper held no tide line, no curl at the edges, no softness in the grain. The ink was Eleanor's. Tight, small, leaning slightly left. "Lens set, 4 inch, refractor component, accessioned March 1932."

And the black branches stopped at the border of the slip.

They framed it.

A perfect rectangle of white paper surrounded by black vascular roots. Not a single line crossed the name. Not one filament touched the date. The branches curved around the entry with the precision of a pen stroke, drawing attention to Eleanor's handwriting while never violating the paper.

Clara's fingers were cold. She checked them for ink. Clean.

She slid the slip from the drawer.

The black branches did not follow. They held their frame, empty now, an invitation to replace what she had removed. She closed the drawer. The frame remained on the oak surface, a silhouette of the slip she had taken, black lines holding the shape of a name.

"It knows her."

She did not say this aloud. The thought was too large for the air, too close to the conclusion she was not ready to reach.

She placed the slip in her journal, between two clean pages, and closed the cover.

The corridor was silent. The water dripped from the ceiling in a steady rhythm, one drop every three seconds. Clara counted the intervals. Fourteen drips before she moved. Fifteen before she turned. Sixteen before she walked back toward the dome with Eleanor's accession slip pressed against her chest in the safest folder she had.

She used the sunlight aggressively after that.

She carried a second mirror into the dome and positioned both to concentrate the white beam across the star chart plate. The black threads shriveled, turned brittle, and crumbled to charcoal ash. A clean kill. She swept the ash into a collection jar and labeled it "sample 5A — solar termination residue".

She repeated the process on the swelling sleeve.

The double Mylar barrier had slowed the colonization, but the black filaments had found the seam and were pressing through. She held the sleeve in the beam for three minutes. The branches charred. The pressure against the plastic relaxed. The sleeve went still.

But the roots did not retreat.

They gathered.

The loose scrap on the table remained untouched, a single thin filament still extending from its corner. The sleeved page, now clear of active surface contamination, began to collect new threads from the air. From the water on the floor. From the condensation on the window. The preserved page was a lodestone, and the black networks in the room were drifting toward it.

Clara watched the process for seven minutes. She clocked the direction of the threads, their speed, their density. She took notes. She did not destroy the page.

She could not destroy the page.

She placed the double-sleeved record in a metal box lined with blotting paper, sealed the box with archival tape, and wrote "quarantine — priority observation" on the lid.

Her hands were steady. Her voice, when she spoke, was flat.

"Procedure holds."

The room accepted the statement without argument.

Clara sat on the crate she used as a stool, the water lapping at the heels of her boots. She pulled her pocket watch from her vest. The brass was warm against her palm. She wound it, counting the clicks.

"Eight-fourteen through eight-twenty."

She mouthed the catalogue numbers. The ritual held her together, kept the edges of her panic from fraying.

"Eleanor would have organized this by date, not function."

The thought arrived without invitation. She let it sit in the silence of her skull, turned it over, and waited for the echo that never came. No voice answered. No presence filled the empty corridor. Just the water, the glass, the slow crawl of black at the edge of her vision.

She had put the Eleanor slip in her safest folder.

She had sealed it between clean pages.

She had checked the seal.

And now, standing to retrieve the star chart plate from the observation table, Clara saw that her journal was open.

She had not left it open. She never left it open.

The Eleanor-labeled slip was face-up inside the cover. The black branches on the glass plate above the journal arranged themselves into the same route: the descent, the spiral, the deep, the lower levels where the grate covered the absolute dark.

Clara touched the slip. The paper was cold. The branches on the glass flexed, held the shape, and went still.

She did not turn the slip over. She did not close the journal. She looked at the route drawn on the glass, the same geometry she had sketched beneath the grate, the same spiral she had documented in the condensation, the same descent that had been tracing itself across every surface she preserved.

The ink was not writing a message.

It was drawing a path.

And it wanted her to follow.
