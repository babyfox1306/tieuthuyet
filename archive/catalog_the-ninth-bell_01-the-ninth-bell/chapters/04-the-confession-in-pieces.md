At 400x magnification, the confession split into jagged continents—each syllable an island with a shoreline that didn't match its neighbor. I stared at the waveform on my monitor, the coffee in my hand growing cold, my pulse a steady, furious drum against my ribs. The voice that had spoken from the ninth bell, the voice that sounded like mine, was not speech at all. It was suture.

I zoomed in further. Six hundred times magnification. Eight hundred. The image degraded into pixel blocks, but the structure remained visible: between every phonetic boundary, a vertical cut so clean it could only be surgical. No formant transition. No glottal pulse carrying through. Just absolute digital silence, then a new fragment beginning. Like a ransom note assembled from newspaper clippings, but in sound.

My hands moved to the keyboard before my mind fully consented to what I was seeing. I opened my archive search tool, a piece of custom software I'd built during my master's thesis for exactly this kind of forensic comparison. It had taken me three years to code, and I'd never used it on anything more urgent than academic disputes over disputed broadcast recordings. Now it was going to dissect my own voice.

I loaded the nine-second bell segment into the source window. The software asked for a reference database. I pointed it at the family recordings folder. Twenty-three thousand files, spanning eighteen years of compulsive archiving. My finger hovered over the execute button.

"This is insane," I said aloud. The storage room answered with silence, the hum of the hard drives, the distant suck and pull of the tide against the causeway stones. Gabriel was still out there somewhere, checking the water level, probably hoping I'd leave before he had to explain further. I didn't care what Gabriel hoped anymore.

I pressed execute.

The software began its work silently, a progress bar creeping across the screen at an agonizing pace. I watched it for thirty seconds, then forced myself to look away. Pacing the narrow aisle between crates, I counted my steps. Seven to the wall. Seven back. The floorboards creaked in the same places every time, a rhythm I was beginning to memorize.

The progress bar reached twelve percent. I sat down, then stood up again. My body didn't know what to do with this kind of waiting—this suspended state between knowing and not-knowing. I'd spent eighteen years in that gap, straddling the chasm between what I remembered and what I'd been told. This was no different. Just a new flavor of the same old vertigo.

Twenty-seven percent.

The software was comparing the bell voice against every recording I'd ever made in my father's house. Every kitchen argument. Every birthday dinner. Every quiet evening when I'd left the recorder running on the mantelpiece, capturing the ambient texture of a family that was already falling apart. I'd archived it all, never knowing why, never admitting to myself that I was building a library of evidence for a crime I couldn't name.

Forty-one percent.

I opened the spectral display manually, pulling up the first candidate match the software had flagged. It was a file called Kitchen_04_Nov.wav. I knew it without opening it—the bad compressor bearing, the 60Hz thrum, Elin's laugh like broken glass. But I opened it anyway, because I needed to see the match with my own eyes.

The software had highlighted a single syllable: the word "knew" from the confession. It was 0.3 seconds long, a diphthong that moved from near-high front to high back. The software had matched it to a fragment from the kitchen recording, specifically Elin's voice saying "I knew you'd say that" to our father during the argument about the fish.

I played them side by side. The confession's "knew" and Elin's "knew." They were identical. Not similar—identical. The same harmonic structure, the same formant trajectory, the same micro-vibrato in the vowel. The software had found a perfect match.

I closed my eyes. When I opened them, the progress bar had reached sixty-three percent.

More matches were flooding in now, the software spitting out results faster than I could examine them. The word "she" from the confession matched a recording of my mother reading aloud from a novel in 1999. The word "would" matched Elin again, this time from a phone call I'd recorded without her knowledge—a conversation about a boy she was seeing, her voice light and conspiratorial, a version of my sister I'd almost forgotten.

The word "fall" matched me.

I stopped breathing. The software had flagged a file called Clara_Interview_05.wav, dated three years ago. It was a professional recording, not a family tape—a remote broadcast interview I'd done for a BBC Radio 4 program about audio forensics. The host had asked me about the ethics of recovering sound from damaged media, and I'd said something about how "the truth has a way of surfacing, even from the deepest fall."

The word "fall" from the confession was my own voice, professionally recorded, broadcast across the country, then stolen and stitched into a confession I never made.

I checked the metadata. The interview had been conducted from a studio in Manchester. Three hundred miles from Dunmouth. Three hundred miles from the tower, from the family archive, from everything I'd assumed was the source of the forgery.

The progress bar hit one hundred percent. The software displayed a summary screen: twenty-three matched fragments, sourced from eleven different recordings. Six from family tapes. Five from my professional archive. Two from broadcast interviews conducted at remote facilities.

The confession was a collage. A mosaic of stolen syllables, harvested from my entire recorded life, assembled with surgical precision into a sentence I had never spoken and would never speak.

I leaned back in my chair, my hands flat on the desk, and let the silence settle around me like ash. The maker had reached beyond the family home. Beyond the tower. Beyond everything I'd thought was the scope of this intrusion. They had accessed my professional archive, the cloud storage where I kept my broadcast work, the remote servers that hosted years of public recordings.

This wasn't a local operation. This wasn't my father's study, his reel-to-reel, his labeled cassettes. This was someone with reach. Someone with knowledge of my entire audio footprint. Someone who had planned this for longer than I wanted to calculate.

I pulled up the final fragment, the last word of the confession: "anyway."

The software traced it to a broadcast interview I'd given at the Manchester Media Centre in 2019. I remembered the session—a panel discussion on digital preservation, the moderator's voice flat and bored, the audience questions predictable. I'd said something dismissive at the end, some throwaway comment about the limitations of the technology, and I'd ended it with "anyway."

The room tone matched. The background artifacts—the hum of the ventilation system, the distant rumble of a tram passing outside the studio—matched. The waveform was a perfect extraction, a syllable lifted cleanly from its context and grafted onto the end of a false confession.

The maker had reached three hundred miles to find that word. They had sifted through hours of my professional work, isolated a single syllable, and placed it at the end of a sentence designed to destroy me.

I closed the software. I closed the archive. I sat in the dark of the storage room, the only light the glow of the monitor, and I let myself feel the full weight of what I'd found.

The confession was fabricated. I had proof. But the proof didn't tell me who made it, or why, or how far their reach extended. It only told me that I was being hunted by someone who knew me better than I knew myself.

The final word of the confession—"anyway"—traced to a broadcast interview I gave in Manchester, three hundred miles from the tower, proving the maker reached well beyond the family archive to silence me.

And I had no idea who they were.
