The raven did not move when Marget Halloway stepped out of the taxi.

She paid the driver without looking at him. He took her money without meeting her eyes. The engine revved before she had both feet on the ground, and the car was already turning around by the time she pulled her bag from the back seat. He did not look back. She did not blame him.

The road to town was a single gray ribbon through the fog, dissolving behind the taxi as if the moor was stitching itself shut. She turned to face the graveyard.

It was not a graveyard anymore. It was a crater. The ground had dropped six feet in the center, dragging the headstones down with it, leaving the perimeter fence hanging four feet in the air like a collapsed hem. Moss clung to everything—the stones, the soil, the iron gate hinge that had rusted through. Not the soft green moss of an old churchyard. This was black. Fine. Almost charcoal, smearing against the granite when she tried to wipe it off.

The air smelled of wet slate and dead earth. No birdsong. No insects. Just the low sound of the wind passing over the basin, a sound that was almost a whisper, though she could not make out the words.

At the center of the depression, on the largest stone, sat the raven. It did not preen. It did not tilt its head. It sat perfectly still, chest-deep in the mist that pooled in the basin, as if it had been carved from the same slate as the stone beneath it.

Marget slung her bag over her shoulder and stepped through the ruined gate.

The ground felt wrong under her boots. Spongy. Unstable. Not like soil at all—it was too fine, too dark, like standing on a layer of crushed seeds. She lifted her foot and saw the imprint hold its shape, the turf refusing to spring back.

She picked her way down the slope, past the fractured stones and the exposed roots that looked like veins pulled from a body.

"You are early."

The voice came from behind a collapsed mausoleum. She stopped.

Arthur Penhaligon stepped into the gap, wiping his gloved hands together as if brushing off dust. He was tall, narrow-shouldered, wearing an overcoat that fell to his knees. His shoes were spotless. Not a smear of mud, not a blade of grass. As if he had walked here on a path only he could see, between the raindrops.

"The schedule said three weeks," she said. "I thought I would get a head start."

"Good." He did not smile. "You know what you are looking for."

It was not a question.

"Settlement readings. Soil composition. Stone temperature and grain degradation." She recited it from memory. "You want a full survey of the collapse, stratification of the peat layer, and a structural report on the remaining stones."

"Three weeks." He pulled a pocket watch from his vest—an antique, gold, heavy. He did not look at the time. He just held it, as if the gesture was a habit he could not break. "You will stay in the cottage at the edge of the property. No visitors. No calls to the town. You work, you record, and you leave."

"Who do I report to?"

"My office. Weekly. In writing." He tucked the watch away. "And you will keep a personal ledger of every stone you touch. Temperature. Grain. Your own physical impressions."

She touched the worn leather of her journal through her bag. "I always do."

"Then we understand each other." He turned to leave, then stopped. His eyes—pale, almost colorless—fixed on hers. "One more thing. Leave the central stone alone until you have surveyed the rest. Start from the perimeter and work inward. By the time you reach it, you will have a baseline."

"Why?"

"Because I am paying you not to ask questions." He walked toward the gate. His shoes left no prints. He passed through the gate without touching it. The mist swallowed him before he reached the road.

She watched him go, then turned back to the graveyard. The raven was still watching her. Its head had not moved. Its gaze was absolute.

She started at the perimeter, as instructed.

The first stone was a simple slate tablet, sunk a good two feet into the peat. She knelt beside it, unzipped her bag, and pulled out her tools—measuring tape, a small hand trowel, the leather-bound ledger that had followed her through every job she had taken. The spine was cracked. The pages were soft from handling.

She recorded the angle of the lean, the depth of the black moss, the temperature of the air.

Then she touched the stone.

The grain was wrong. Normally, slate felt cool and clean, a crisp break. This one felt greasy, almost soft, as if the water had loosened the bonds between the minerals. She pressed her fingertips flat against the surface and waited.

The cold did not come from the stone. It came from inside it. A persistent vibration, low and humming, like a note played just below the range of hearing. It was not temperature. It was resonance. The stone was ringing, and her hand was the damper.

She pulled her hand back and stared at her fingers.

The tips were white.

She flexed them. They felt thick, dull, as if wrapped in cotton. She had not felt the cold while her hand was on the stone. The sensation had been neutral. Anesthetic.

Oswin had warned her about stones that lied. "When the stone stops talking to your skin, you stop trusting the stone." She had never understood what that meant.

She understood now.

She opened her ledger. "C001. Persistent cold vibration. Temperature depressed markedly below ambient. Immediate numbness in fingertips upon removal."

She forced herself to continue.

Stone by stone. The pattern held. Every carved surface she touched carried the same deep vibration, the same false neutrality that masked the cold. By the fourth stone, her fingers were clumsy. By the sixth, she had to write with her left hand.

She stopped at the edge of the central basin, looking down at the raven. It still had not moved. Its head was fixed in her direction now, as if it had turned at some point she had not seen. The black eye was dry, unblinking, focused.

The central stone was a monolith. Twice her height, unmarked by age, sitting at the exact center of the sunken ground. The moss around its base was darker than the rest. Thicker. The air around it shimmered slightly, a heat haze made of cold.

She opened her ledger to the final page.

"C006. Central stone. Raven perched stationary. Temperature differential severe at perimeter. Numbness advancing up the fingers of my right hand."

She looked at her right hand. She could see the blood slow in her capillaries, the skin paling from the fingertips to the second knuckle. She rubbed her thumb over her calluses. The motion was automatic.

She could not feel it.

She pressed harder. Rubbed until the skin reddened in patches, but the numbness did not break. The cold had sealed the nerves behind a wall she could not force.

The raven turned its head.

It was the first movement it had made. A single, mechanical swivel of its head until one black eye was fixed directly on her. The mist swirled around its base. The cold from the stone seemed to pulse, a heartbeat in the ground.

She looked at her right index finger. It was bloodless.

She pressed it against her own cheek.

And felt nothing.
