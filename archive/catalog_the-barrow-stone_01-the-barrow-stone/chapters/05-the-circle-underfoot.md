Marget Halloway had expected to find something dead at the center of the cold circle—instead she found a stone that had never been touched by the raven.

Dawn was a gray bleed across the moor. The mist hung lower than the day before, dense at the ankles and thinning at the chest, as if the ground was breathing upward. She had the map in her pocket, folded into a square that fit against her ribs. The lines she had drawn the night before, when her right hand still moved on command.

The cold perimeter she had mapped traced a rough circle around the central standing stone. She had assumed the center was the standing stone itself. That was the mistake. The true center was thirty feet away, triangulated where the three deepest cold readings converged.

She walked the line slowly, her left thumb pressed to the compass she had brought from the village. The needle swung once, settled, and pointed her to a patch of ground that looked like every other patch of ground on the moor.

Except the grass was green. Bright, wet, thriving. A perfect circle of life in the dead skin of the graveyard.

She knelt. She touched the soil with her left hand. The grass was cold at the root, but the ground beneath resisted her pressure like stone.

She drew her knife and cut into the sod.

The moss on the surface snapped under the blade. It did not weep or bleed. It curled inward, retreating from the steel as if it recognized the shape of an enemy. The green fibers pulled tight against the earth, exposing a darker surface beneath.

She cut a larger square. The turf peeled away cleanly.

The stone beneath was not granite. It was slate. Dark, uniform, polished smooth by years of pressure. The slate was set flush with the soil as if it had been placed by someone who expected to find it again in the dark. Someone who knew the moss would cover everything else but would never take root on this surface.

She cleared the last of the clinging fibers from the center and found the carving.

"Root of Barrow."

The letters were deep and deliberate. Professional. Worn at the edges by time but still legible, still clear. This was not a scratched warning. It was a dedication. A name for something that had been built beneath her feet.

She placed her right palm flat against the slate.

She felt nothing.

The surface was cold. She knew it was cold because her left hand felt the heat bleed from the exposed stone. The temperature difference was sharp, almost painful. But her right hand registered nothing. No temperature. No grain. No resistance. The skin touched the slate and the slate existed in a separate world that her nerves no longer inhabited.

She pressed harder. The arm did not report the effort. She was pressing her own dead weight against the stone, and the stone accepted it without giving anything back.

She pulled her hand away and stared at it. Pale. Capable-looking. A liar.

She tapped the slate with her left knuckle. The sound was solid. She tapped it with her right knuckle. She heard the impact, a soft thud, but she could not feel the vibration travel up her bones.

The pattern was artificial. She had confirmed it. The cold circle was not a natural phenomenon. It was built. Dug. Engineered by someone who understood the ground and the moss and the way the moor took things back. The slate was a cap. A seal. And whatever it was sealing was the thing that breathed through the central stone.

She moved her fingers against the edge of the slab. Her left hand found a seam. The slate was not a solid block. It was a lid.

She worked her knife into the seam and pried. The seal broke with a whispering pop of released pressure, a vacuum pulling air into the space beneath. She lifted the edge and found a hollow, dry and dark, the walls lined with a clay that had hardened into ceramic.

A lead tube rested inside.

It was pitted, the surface etched by years of contact with the slate and the soil. She lifted it out with her left hand. The lead was cold. The cap was stuck. She twisted it against her thigh and felt the resistance break.

Inside was a roll of paper.

She drew it out carefully. The fibers were brittle but intact, darkened at the edges by damp that had never quite reached the ink. She spread it across her knee and held the edges down with her dead right hand. The paper did not tear. It unfolded like a map, revealing lines of iron-gall ink written in a hand she recognized from the carving.

"Keepers of the Stone."

Below it was a list of names. Dates. A single notation beside each: "took the root."

Walter Henley — 12th of April, 1907.

Mary Sutter — 3rd of September, 1909.

Jacob Fenn — 19th of January, 1912.

She read them slowly, her left index finger tracking the words. There were six names on the first sheet. She unfolded the second.

The last entry was written in a different hand. The ink was darker, the pen pressure heavier, the strokes less controlled.

"Oswin Kerrow — 23rd of March, four years ago."

She opened her mouth. The air in her throat was cold.

A voice cut through the mist behind her.

"You were told to stay out of the circle."

She did not turn. She knew the voice. Arthur Penhaligon stood at the edge of the bright grass, his coat buttoned to the throat, his shoes perfectly dry.

He stepped closer. His eyes went to the exposed slate. To the lid she had pried open. To the paper in her hands.

"That is a four-hundred-year-old grave marker," he said. "You have damaged it."

"It is not a marker," she said. "It is a seal."

"It is none of your concern."

She stood. The lead tube was in her left hand. The paper was in her right. She could not feel the paper. She looked down at her own grip and saw that her fingers were not fully closed. They were wrapped around the document loosely, as if she had forgotten how to hold things.

She tightened her left hand on the tube.

"What is the Root of Barrow?"

Arthur's jaw tightened. "You will not learn it from a piece of paper."

"Then tell me."

"I hired you to clear the graves. I paid you to remove the moss and log the markers. I did not pay you to dig up the ground or read the names of people I have already buried."

"Oswin Kerrow is on this list."

Arthur stopped.

"Four years ago," Marget said. "You told me he disappeared. He did not disappear. He took the root. What is the root?"

Arthur stared at her. The silence stretched until the raven shifted on the central stone, its wings scraping against the granite.

"You look tired, Miss Halloway."

"Do not change the subject."

"I am not changing it. I am ending it. You will put the paper back. You will seal the slate. You will finish the removal contract by the end of the month, or I will find someone who will."

"Someone who does not ask questions."

"Yes." He looked at her right hand. The one that held the paper without feeling it. "Someone who still has all their fingers."

The threat sat between them cold and exact.

"I will not put it back," she said.

Arthur took a step backward. His eyes flicked from her face to the document in her dead hand.

"Then you carry it," he said. "And you learn what happens to the people who carry it."

He turned and walked into the mist. His footsteps were soft and dry. He did not look back.

Marget waited until the mist swallowed him. Then she looked down at her right hand. The paper was still there. She could see it. She could not feel it.

She brought her left hand up and touched her right forearm. The skin was cold. She pressed harder. Nothing.

She rolled up her sleeve. The tissue of her forearm looked normal. Pink. Alive. But when she touched it, there was no signal. Her thumb pressed into her own flesh and she felt only the pressure in the thumb. The arm beneath it was gone.

She wrote the entry in her ledger with her left hand. The letters were uneven and jagged. "Right arm—sensation absent to mid-brachium. Motor function declining."

She did not put the paper back.

She sealed the lead tube into her satchel. She covered the slate with the turf, but she did not press it down. The moss could have it back. It would cover it by nightfall anyway.

She left the graveyard without looking at the raven.

The walk to the village was long. The wind cut through her coat. She could not tell if her right hand was cold. She could not tell if it was warm. She could not tell if it was there at all except that she could see it swinging beside her hip, a foreign object strapped to her shoulder.

The pub had one lit window. She took a chair near the glass. The wood was warm. She smelled coal smoke and cheap ale. The pub owner looked at her twice and said nothing.

She set the lead tube on the table. She uncapped it with her left hand. She drew out the paper.

She unfolded the paper in the pub light and read the last entry: 'Oswin Kerrow—took the root on the 23rd of March, four years ago.'
